- Source: J.P. Morgan — Sector Spotlight: Software (2026), Innovation Economy. Authors: Dan Hardman, James Carlin, Nick Candy, Vincent Harrison. Published Aug 2026.
- Core claim: AI is causing "evolution not extinction" in software — dispersion across winners and losers, not a universal lift or wipeout — with value migrating from code toward proprietary data, distribution, and orchestration layers.
- Framework: JPM's software defensibility matrix (9 moat categories x 4 company types, scored 1-5) + the valuation-framework shift (Growth + FCF Margin R² = 69.2%, up from growth-alone regime)
- Applies when: evaluating software companies, understanding AI's impact on SaaS business models, assessing venture/PE positioning, M&A thesis construction in software
- Breaks when: a single AI model achieves winner-take-all at the foundation layer (JPM assumes model competition continues); or if enterprise AI buying reverts to build-internally (2025 data shows 76% purchasing, but that could reverse); or if outcome-based pricing proves unscalable (only 5% pure outcome-based today)
- Practitioner counterpoint: The "SaaS-pocalypse" valuation compression is real — median EV/Rev at 3.7x is below pre-COVID levels despite better margins (EBITDA +6pp, FCF +9pp). But JPM notes fundamentals are solid; the compression is about *uncertainty* around AI positioning, not deteriorating businesses. The 47% AI-to-production conversion rate (vs 25% for traditional software) and 4x PLG motion rate for AI apps suggest the disruption is arriving through shadow-IT and bottom-up adoption, not top-down procurement — which is harder for incumbents to defend against even with strong distribution moats.

## Key data points

### The token economics trap
- Token costs fell ~1000x (H1 2023 → H1 2026, log scale)
- But tokens per task rose as workloads shifted from chatbots → RAG, coding, research, agentic
- Net: total cost per task declined only modestly, not the 1000x the headline suggests

### Public market repricing
- 172 public software companies analyzed (TTM as of June 30, 2026)
- Median revenue growth: 13% (down from 29% peak in 2021)
- Median EBITDA margin: 25% (+6pp); median FCF margin: 22% (+9pp)
- Median EV/Revenue: 3.7x (down ~5x from post-peak)
- Valuation now explained by Growth + FCF Margin combined (R² = 69.2%), not growth alone
- Infrastructure software: 8.2x EV/Rev; Application software: 3.3x — widest gap ever

### Category-level multiples (EV/TTM Revenue, H1 2026)
- Cloud, data, AI infrastructure: 9.1x
- Security & identity infrastructure: 6.8x
- Vertical software: 4.6x
- Consumer, commerce, transaction: 4.0x
- Horizontal software: 2.7x

### Private market bifurcation
- AI-enabled deals overtook non-AI in cumulative count (~17k vs ~15k)
- AI-enabled companies raise larger rounds, higher valuations, less dilution, faster cycles at every stage
- Series D median pre-money: approaching $1.6B
- Fundraising timelines stable (12-18 months between rounds)

### Monetization state (messy, early)
- 70% of software companies launched AI features
- Only 41% of those monetizing them; 53% of monetizers still on traditional subscription
- Top pricing change driver: customer demand for usage/outcome-based (46%)
- Outcome-based pricing tied to: cost savings (36%), revenue generated (18%), both (18%)
- Hybrid subscription + usage: 31% and growing

### Enterprise buying behavior shift
- Build vs buy flipped: 47% built internally (2024) → 76% purchased (2025)
- AI buying cycles convert to production at 47% (nearly 2x the 25% rate for traditional software)
- AI app spend via product-led growth: 40% (incl. shadow AI) vs 7% for traditional software
- Measuring AI value: 35% "informal feedback," only 25% use KPIs/dashboards

### M&A
- AI-enabled exits: 452 (H1 2026 projected), overtaking non-AI (238)
- AI-enabled acquired at $132M median vs $85M for non-AI
- Non-AI exits: 70% M&A + 28% buyout, ~2% IPO (public window essentially closed)
- PE add-on acquisitions: 41% of deals (2026), up from 33% in 2016

## Structural insights

**Value migrates up the stack.** Model competition pushes value away from foundation models toward deployment, orchestration, governance layers. Open-source/open-weight models make "one-model" strategies less attractive → orchestration layers that run multiple models side-by-side gain importance.

**The AI infrastructure premium is structural, not cyclical.** Companies positioned near data, compute, governance, and orchestration layers of enterprise AI earn recurring demand regardless of which applications succeed. This is the new durable-revenue thesis.

**Monetization remains the open question.** Most software companies haven't solved AI pricing. The market is experimenting across subscription, usage-based, hybrid, and outcome-based models. Cost savings is the easiest proof point (36% of outcome-based pricing). Revenue generation is harder to attribute.

**Shadow AI is the Trojan horse.** 40% of AI application spend enters via product-led / shadow-IT motions (vs 7% for traditional software). This bypasses the procurement moats that incumbents depend on. The governance gap (only 25% have KPIs/dashboards for AI impact) means enterprises are spending on AI they can't measure.
