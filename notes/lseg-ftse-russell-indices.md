# Note · FTSE Russell — the #3 index provider whose real power is gatekeeping: deciding which countries' bonds the world must buy, and now which venue's prices they are marked at

> Market-structure / benchmark-power note · Maps to **Course 04** (Competitive Strategy — index oligopoly, brand power, licensing), **Course 11** (Global Strategy & Macro — WGBI inclusion as capital-flow policy), **Course 07** (Data — who sets the ruler), **E9** (Geopolitics — China, Korea, India and Russia decisions), **E4** (Governance of benchmarks) · Written 2026-10-25 · Part of [[lseg-hub]]; companions [[lseg-tradeweb-control]]; compare [[tradeweb-rates-em]], [[spglobal-hub]], [[blackrock-hub]]

- **Source:** LSEG corpus: FTSE Russell press releases 2019–2026 (~280 kept). This includes semi-annual country-classification results, the Tradeweb partnership items (2023-10-25, 2024-06-10, 2024-10-08), the Cboe (2020) and CME (2026) licence extensions and ETF selections. Also the FTSE Russell investor webinar (2024-12-10), results calls and ARs. **Excluded on purpose:** 167 routine index-review and reconstitution releases (listed in `index.tsv`). **Caveat:** no MSCI or S&P DJI voices in the corpus. Market-share claims are FTSE's.
- **Core claim:** FTSE Russell is "the third largest provider of benchmark and index solutions globally" (Bassett, 2024-12-10). It makes ~£900m–£954m revenue, ~2/3 subscription, ~66% EBITDA margin (2025), with ~$18–20tn benchmarked. Its commercial position is #3 behind MSCI and S&P DJI. Its *structural* position is bigger than that. Through the WGBI and its country-classification process it decides when sovereign-bond markets join the global benchmark. That means China (2021, phased over 36 months), Korea (2025), India (EMGBI 2025) and Russia (removed 2022). Since March 2025 it also decides that those bonds are priced off Tradeweb FTSE closing prices, run by its parent's subsidiary. The franchise grows with passive (ETF AUM on FTSE indices $1.8tn in 2025). It is exposed to self-indexing, mandate losses (a 2024 ETF contract loss cost £15–20m), and the fading ESG cycle it rode in 2019–22.

## 2. Three sources of power

1. **Brand and licences (commercial).** Russell 2000/1000, FTSE 100 and FTSE All-World, with exclusive derivatives licences: Cboe for options, CME for futures to 2037, Eurex for FTSE 100 (2021). "94 of the top 100 asset managers use our indices" (Bassett, 2024-12-10). BlackRock links more than $430bn of AUM to 125 FTSE Russell products in iShares (AR 2023), and renewed the licence on 2025-10-29.
2. **Classification (quasi-regulatory).** The country-classification process is what moves sovereign flows. Governments lobby for upgrades: the PBoC (2020–21), Korea's Deputy PM (2024) and Vietnam's MoF (2025) all welcomed decisions. Bassett frames it as neutral: "grounded in the principle of accessibility."
3. **Pricing (benchmark administration).** Before 2025, fixed-income indices priced off *Refinitiv evaluated pricing*. Examples: "Pricing provided by Refinitiv evaluated pricing service at local market close (4pm New York)" (US Municipal index, 2019-04-29), and the extended Refinitiv supplier deal (2019-01-10). Since 2025 they price off Tradeweb FTSE closing prices. "Tradeweb FTSE pricing is considered best-in-class as it's volume-driven, it's algorithmic, and it's governed as a benchmark." (Bassett, 2024-12-10). See [[lseg-tradeweb-control]].

## 3. Positioning vs MSCI and S&P

- "If Bloomberg is a walled garden, we aspire to be the open meadow." (Bassett, on fixed-income indices, 2024-12-10). On equities: "we believe we're well-placed to compete with MSCI in international" (Bassett, same webinar).
- Ambition (2020): "FTSE Russell aims to be the world leader in multi-asset indexing" (Samad, 2020-04-06).
- Self-indexing threat, admitted early: "having an investment strategy is not quite the same as being able to implement that investment strategy as an index" (Lea Carty, 2021-10-01). An analyst noted asset-based clients "shifting towards self indexation" (Giblat, 2021-10-01).

## 4. Headwinds

- **Mandate concentration.** The 2024 ETF loss went to a boutique FTSE had helped grow (Bassett: "we helped them grow to be quite a little bit bigger"). Ian White (Autonomous) asked whether to read it as erosion of Russell brand power (Q3 2024). Asset-based fees are partly unprotected by collars (Schwimmer, May 2025).
- **Quiet renewal years** "limited price realisation" (AR 2025). Subscription growth was "modest" in Q3 2025, then re-accelerated to +7.9% (Q3 2026).
- **ESG reversal.** The 2019–22 releases are dense with climate, TPI and Paris-aligned launches. ESG passive AUM on FTSE indices fell from $296bn (2022) to $262bn (2023, AR). Sustainable adoption in FTSE's own survey dipped from 88% to 80% (2023).
- **Regulation.** EU BMR third-country rules forced an EU-authorised FTSE entity (AMF, Nov 2025; ESMA endorsement Jul 2026).

## 5. What the claims don't prove

1. **"Third largest"** is FTSE's self-description. Industry rankings vary by revenue vs AUM benchmarked.
2. **"Robust index governance"** is asserted in each country decision. The corpus has no dissent, consultation feedback or minutes.
3. **The Tradeweb price switch** is presented as quality ("more representative, transparent and robust"). No back-test against the old evaluated prices is published in the corpus.
4. **$18tn / $20tn "benchmarked"** mixes active benchmarking with passive tracking. Only passive AUM generates asset-based fees.

## 6. Strategic read (inference)

- **WGBI is the crown jewel, not Russell.** The Russell brand earns licence fees. WGBI *membership* directs trillions in sovereign demand, and LSEG has tied its pricing to an affiliate venue. That joins index, data and trading in fixed income the way no competitor has.
- **Fixed income is where FTSE can win.** In equities it is #3 against MSCI and S&P. In bond indices its WGBI heritage, Yield Book analytics, LCH data and Tradeweb prices form a stack that only Bloomberg (with its own indices) and ICE (with its own pricing) can match. Octus (2026) extends this into private credit.
- **Country classification is soft geopolitics.** Every inclusion is a diplomatic event (China during US–China tension, Korea, India, Vietnam). FTSE's neutrality claim carries real weight. It is also a political exposure the risk register never names.

## 7. Canon lenses

- **Information goods and lock-in** ([[canon-shapiro-varian-information-rules]]): benchmarks are standards. Switching costs live in mandates, derivatives and tracking error.
- **Power** ([[canon-helmer-7-powers]]): branding (Russell 2000) and cornered resource (WGBI membership decisions).
- **Thinking strategically** ([[canon-dixit-nalebuff-thinking-strategically]]): governments, issuers and managers play an inclusion game; FTSE's commitment to published criteria makes the threat credible.
- **Geopolitics** ([[canon-allison-essence-of-decision]]): index decisions as organisational-process outputs that have state-level effects.

## 8. Practitioner counterpoint

- **For:** WGBI inclusion (China, Korea, India) is among the few index decisions that move tens of billions of dollars. FTSE has earned that by being conservative and rules-based. Venue-derived closing prices are executable, unlike model prices.
- **Against:** passive managers pressure index fees every renewal. The 2024 boutique loss shows mid-size mandates are portable, and the ESG product wave has gone into reverse.
