# Note · Goldman Sachs AWM & private credit — buying wrappers and channels to make fees 'durable', and sitting on both sides of the private-credit boom just as it is tested

> Strategy / capital-allocation / risk note · Maps to **E2** (Capital Allocation — paying for distribution, often in stock; fee durability as a valuation lever), **E3** (M&A, Deals & Restructuring — earnouts, buy-then-sell of PFM, deal-size ladder), **E6** (Crisis Leadership & Resilience — the 2026 BDC redemption wave and how a firm talks through it), **Course 11** (Global Strategy, Macro & Responsible Business — bank retreat, non-bank credit, sovereign anchor capital, retail suitability) · Written 2026-10-25 · Companion to [[goldman-hub]], [[goldman-strategy-financials]], [[goldman-ai]], [[blackrock-hub]], [[wss-private-markets-wealth]], [[wss-credit-distressed]]

Rowe Price, Industry Ventures ×2, Innovator ×2, QIA, LCN, NEOS), the 2019–2025 shareholder letters, the Investor Day 2020 and 2023 AWM decks, and 14 *Exchanges* / *Top of Mind* / Insights items on private markets (Koester 2022; Karoui/Reynolds 2022 and 2025; Rowan 2023; Arougheti 2024; Blostein/Bantwal, Bantwal, Zelter, Marks/Arougheti/Lynam, Lyon/Brandmeyer, Swildens 2026). No regulator, short-seller, academic or retail investor speaks. **Missing:** 10-K segment detail, deal financials (no target revenue or earnings is disclosed for any acquisition), GS's own BDC redemption data, and any statement of how GS allocates originated loans between its funds and third parties. **Date quirks:** the NEOS release is filed 2026-09-03 but its dateline is Aug 12 2026; the *Cracks in Private Credit* page is dated 2026-04-29 but its podcast companion (*Pressures in Private Credit*, slug `cracks-in-private-credit`) is dated 2026-05-11 and includes May 1 recordings; letters are dated to fiscal year-end but published the following spring.
- **Core claim:** Since 2019 Goldman has spent roughly **$9–10bn of announced consideration** *(derived, headline terms only)* on asset- and wealth-management deals, and almost none of it buys investment alpha. It buys **wrappers and distribution**: an RIA channel (United Capital, $750M cash, 2019), RIA custody (Folio, 2020), European and insurance scale (NN IP, €1.7B, 2022), a 401(k) target-date channel (T. Rowe Price, up to $1B stake, 2025), venture secondaries (Industry Ventures, $665M + up to $300M), buffer ETFs (Innovator, ~$2.0B), options-income ETFs (NEOS, up to $2.25B), sovereign anchor capital (QIA, $25bn target) and a net-lease product with GBM origination built in (LCN, ~80% in stock). The later deals are paid **partly or mostly in GS equity with earnouts**, and every one is justified by the same phrase: more *durable* revenue. It worked on the numbers: AUS went **$1.9T (2019) → >$4.0T (Jun 2026)**, management and other fees **$7.74bn (2021) → $11.54bn (2025)**, and alts fundraising hit **$438bn since Investor Day 2020**, with a new **$750bn fee-paying alts AUS by 2030** target. Meanwhile **Capital Solutions Group (Jan 2025)** put GS on the *origination* side of private credit while AWM runs **~$145bn** of private credit on the *investing* side. The 2026 stress (retail BDC redemptions at ~2× the 5% cap, software borrowers exposed to AI) tests both seats at once. The house line, repeated by every GS voice, is *technical, not fundamental; illiquid by design; not systemic*. That line may be right, but the corpus never addresses the question it raises most sharply: who decides which originated loan goes to which pocket.

*Durable* is the constant. The vehicle changed:

| Period | Where durability was supposed to come from | Line |

| 2025–26 | Wrappers and channels: ETFs, 401(k), secondaries, NNN | "The acquisition strategically expands the firm’s more durable revenue and reinforces its commitment" (Innovator, Dec 2025); LCN "expands the firm’s more durable revenues" |

The PFM sale is the hinge. GS bought an advice channel in 2019, sold it four years later to a larger RIA, and kept that RIA as a custody and product client. The lesson GS drew *(inference)*: own the **manufacturing and the pipes**, not the advisor's desk. Every later deal fits that rule.

## 4. Both sides of private credit

**Origination seat (GBM).** CSG combines the Financing Group, Financial Sponsors coverage and alts-manager coverage, plus "an alternatives origination group within Capital Solutions." Solomon: the One GS approach lets the firm "channel the growing synergies between our clients in Global Banking & Markets and those in Asset & Wealth Management." Lyon (Jun 2026) describes "a front row seat in that across our agency and asset and wealth businesses."

**Investing seat (AWM).** The private-credit co-heads' aim is "to create superior returns for our investing clients, benefitting from our exceptional sourcing capabilities in Global Banking & Markets." LCN repeats the pattern: an AWM product fed by GBM corporate origination, with GS's own GBM as the buyer's financial adviser.

**Bridge (Solomon, Jan 2025):** "we sit at the center of the ecosystem between public markets and private markets."

### The conflict-of-allocation question *(the corpus is silent; everything below is inference)*

When one firm originates a loan, it can (a) hold it on its balance sheet, (b) syndicate it to third-party lenders (Apollo, Ares, Oaktree — all GS clients and podcast guests), (c) place it in its own funds, or (d) place it in its own *retail* vehicles (G-Series evergreen funds, the BDC complex Bantwal co-runs). Each choice has a different fee and risk profile for GS. The questions a client or regulator would ask:
1. **Allocation policy.** Is there a written rotation or first-look rule between GS funds and third-party clients? Not disclosed.
2. **Pricing.** Who marks a loan GS originated and also holds in a fund? Brandmeyer himself says PE "marks were slow to come down" after rates rose.
3. **Retail vs institutional.** Bantwal says GS raised institutional money first and retail as an add-on. Which pocket gets the better vintages is not stated.
4. **Advice inside the deal.** GBM advised GS on LCN. That is routine for a bank buying a company, but it shows how little separation the One GS model intends.

The structure is Apollo's model (Zelter: origination is the bottleneck) inside a bank that also advises the borrowers. That is a strength in a boom and a governance exposure in a bust (see [[canon-jensen-meckling-agency-theory]]).

## 5. The 2026 stress — the house line vs the guests

**What happened (per GS's own hosts and researchers):** *Top of Mind* framed it as "several high-profile defaults", valuation worries, software/AI exposure and a surge in BDC redemption requests. Nathan opened the Mar 2026 episode: "private credit has gone from one of the hottest asset classes to perhaps one of the coldest."

**Numbers GS put on it:**
- Blostein (GS Research, Mar 2026): redemptions "running, on average in the first quarter, at around 10 percent unannualized", against a 5% cap. About $230bn of NAV in retail vehicles implies $50–70bn of net outflows to fund; "evergreen retail funds in private credit will remain in net outflows throughout 2026 and likely 2027". Software ~25% of direct-lending exposure.
- Bantwal (GSAM, Mar 2026): GS's non-accrual rate is 12bp against ~1.54% for the top-20 BDCs; GS evaluated deals for AI risk "going back to 2023 when we turned down our first deal due to concerns around AI disruption." His stress case: a 15% default rate at 50% recovery costs 7.5 points, turning a ~10% BDC return into ~2.5%: "That would be a bad day."
- Lynam (GS Research, May 2026): retail BDCs ~15% of private-credit AUM; PIK 7–8% of BDC income; realized losses below the historical average.

**The positions:**

| Voice | Seat | Position | Line |
|---|---|---|---|
| **GS house** (Bantwal, Blostein, Lynam, Lyon; Solomon) | Originator, manager, researcher | Technical (retail outflows), not fundamental; dispersion ahead; better vintages for institutional money; not systemic | Solomon's 2025 letter: concerns about underwriting and AI-exposed software "are a reminder that the credit cycle has not been repealed." |
| **Howard Marks** (Oaktree) | Rival lender | Most skeptical guest: a "gold rush" (≈$2T × 1% fee ≈ $20bn a year), the lender's advantage "arbitraged away", buyers may not have been informed. Still not systemic | "So everybody hangs up a shingle as a direct lender. That's a gold rush." · "Now the flaws have been exposed." |
| **Michael Arougheti** (Ares) | Rival lender | The structure worked as designed; non-traded BDCs <10% of the market; max redemptions ~$5bn a quarter against ~$85bn of quarterly syndicated-loan trading; stress will "shift market share in private credit" | "So there is no asset liability mismatch, there is no run on the bank in these funds." |
| **Jim Zelter** (Apollo, Apr 2026) | Rival lender | Define the market broadly (~$40T including IG, real estate, ABF), and direct lending is a $2T corner with "a little bit of an intersection accident"; the real risk is AI capex returns | "But I would say that the headlines are a lot louder than the spreads right now." · Biggest underestimated risk: "I think the return on invested capital of AI." |
| **Marc Rowan** (Apollo, Feb 2023) | Rival lender, pre-stress | Investors replace banks; warned that scale kills excess return | "The moment we become mainstream, we're no longer offering excess return per unit of risk." |

**Synthesis:** everyone, including the skeptic, says *not systemic*. The disagreement is about **who bears the loss and who was told**. Marks's software arithmetic (25% of a book losing half = −12.5%, −25% at 1:1 leverage: "not abysmal. It's just bad.") and Bantwal's (15% defaults → 7.5 points) land in the same place. The market-definition fight is positioning: Zelter's $40T dilutes the software story, while Marks's and Lynam's ~$2T direct-lending figure concentrates it.

## 6. The semantics of gates

The cleanest lesson in the corpus is a vocabulary fight:

| Speaker | Word chosen | Line |
|---|---|---|
| Bantwal (GS) | Not *semiliquid* | "But the reality is that it's not semiliquid, it's illiquid." |
| Lynam (GS) | *Feature*, then concedes the word | The 5% limits "are a feature, not a bug"; in the next breath, "these redemption gates, as they've been described in some forums, they're not new." |
| Arougheti (Ares) | Not a *gate* | "I take issue with the term gate, it's not really a gate." |
| Marks (Oaktree) | *Should* | "one of the most interesting words in the English language is should." · "Liquidity is a problem whenever a vehicle that invests in private assets promises liquidity." |
| James Reynolds (GS, Jul 2022) | — | Predicted that retail access would bring closer regulatory scrutiny and disclosure, four years before the stress (paraphrase) |

**Read:** a 5% quarterly cap is a gate in function and a feature in the prospectus. All the managers argue the contract worked; the investors' complaint is that the *sales* language ('semi-liquid', 'evergreen', 'interim liquidity') promised something the contract did not. That is a disclosure and suitability problem for wealth distributors, GS's G-Series channel and the T. Rowe target-date funds included *(inference)*. See [[wss-private-markets-wealth]] (evergreen and semi-liquid wrappers) and [[canon-akerlof-market-for-lemons]].

## 7. The PE-alpha admission

Michael Brandmeyer (GSAM XIG CIO, Jun 2026): "private equity has actually gone negative in terms of alpha to the public markets for the first time over the last two years." Brandmeyer's method: match private and public buy and sell dates (direct alpha). Brandmeyer's explanation: smoothed marks make PE roughly flat when public markets rise more than 10%, and public markets were up 40–50% over those two years. Lyon adds that illiquidity premiums once ran "as high as 1200 points" and have compressed. In the same episode Brandmeyer says "the system is gummed up" (buyout holds of almost seven years) and Lyon says "the existential question is your funding model."

**Why it matters:** GS is selling private-markets access into 401(k) defaults and ETF-like wrappers at the moment its own CIO reports that PE's excess return over public markets is, for now, negative. That doesn't refute the long-run case (the explanation is a fair one about smoothing), but it moves the selling point from *alpha* to *diversification and access*. Note the same smoothing that flattered PE in down markets hides losses in private credit (see [[canon-mauboussin-success-equation]]).

## 8. What the claims don't prove

1. **'Durable' is asserted, not shown.** No release separates organic growth from acquired AUS, gives deal-level revenue, or shows fee rates by channel. ETF and buffer products carry lower fees than flagship alts funds; the mix effect on margin is not observable.
2. **Deal prices can't be tested.** No target revenue, EBITDA or margin is disclosed for any acquisition. Earnouts (IV up to $300M through 2030; NEOS and Innovator performance conditions; LCN ~$150M) mean the final price is unknown.
3. **Paying in stock hides the cost.** LCN ~80% in equity, and IV, Innovator and NEOS partly. The share count effect isn't stated. Stock is cheap currency only when GS shares trade richly, which they did in 2026 (ROE ~20%+).
4. **The alts denominators move.** Net inflows → gross fundraising (2019 → 2020); "alternatives business" ($540bn) → "total alternative assets" ($706bn); fee-paying vs total AUS in the 2030 target.
5. **Track records are self-reported.** IV's 18% net IRR and LCN's 10.8% net cash-on-cash are each firm's own calculation.
6. **The stress numbers come from interested parties.** Every stress datum here comes from a manager or a GS researcher whose firm earns fees from the asset class. GS's own BDC flows are not disclosed beyond the 12bp non-accrual figure.
7. **The allocation question isn't addressed at all.** Absence of evidence of conflict is not evidence of controls.

## 9. Strategic read *(inference)*

- **The strategy is a valuation trade.** Fee revenue gets a higher multiple than trading revenue. Buying fee streams with GS stock, at a time when GS stock is rated on trading-heavy earnings, converts volatile equity into durable revenue: an arbitrage on GS's own multiple, if the market re-rates the mix.
- **Distribution beats manufacturing in a crowded field.** T. Rowe's target-date funds and the ETF wrapper are where flows are sticky by default (see [[canon-thaler-sunstein-nudge]]: the 401(k) default is the most powerful nudge in finance). GS is buying its way onto the default option rather than winning allocator by allocator.
- **Liquidity is the product.** Secondaries (IV, Vintage), credit trading (Lyon's "burgeoning private credit trading aspect") and ETF wrappers all sell liquidity on illiquid assets. That is a market-maker's instinct applied to private markets, and it links to [[goldman-markets-platforms]].
- **AI cuts both ways inside one firm.** Solomon sells AI as the 2026 capital-markets catalyst ("Technology and AI-led innovation should be a catalyst for significant capital markets activity in 2026", IV close), while AWM's credit book is exposed to the software borrowers AI threatens. See [[goldman-ai]] and [[wss-credit-distressed]].
- **The stress is a share-shift opportunity for the largest platforms.** Arougheti says so for Ares; the same logic favours GS's institutional-first private credit over retail-heavy rivals, if its marks hold.

## 10. Canon lenses

- **Agency** ([[canon-jensen-meckling-agency-theory]]): one firm as adviser, originator, manager and distributor. The allocation policy is the contract that would govern the conflict, and it is not in the record.
- **Information asymmetry** ([[canon-akerlof-market-for-lemons]]): retail buyers of 'semi-liquid' funds know least about marks and liquidity terms. Marks's "should" is the lemons problem in one word.
- **Fragility of liquidity promises** ([[canon-taleb-black-swan]]): liquidity sleeves (20–30% per Arougheti) and 5% caps are sized for normal redemption, not for a correlated rush.
- **Margin of safety** ([[canon-klarman-margin-of-safety]]): Bantwal's 6× debt against a 24× valuation halving is a margin-of-safety argument; it holds only if marks are honest.
- **Scale and distribution power** ([[canon-helmer-7-powers]]): the deals buy channel and scale economies, not process power in investing.
- **Nudges and defaults** ([[canon-thaler-sunstein-nudge]]): private markets inside target-date funds turn an active choice into a default.

## 11. Practitioner counterpoint

- **Bull:** GS is doing what every winning manager has done — building shelf breadth and wrappers while banks retreat from lending. Fees grew 12% a year, alts raised a record $115bn in 2025, and the private-credit book reports 12bp non-accruals. A bank that originates can underwrite better than a fund that only buys. The stress is retail-technical, and institutional lenders get better spreads.
- **Skeptic:** GS bought an RIA and sold it in four years, ran a consumer bank and exited it, and is now buying ETF shelves with stock at a cyclical high. Its PE alpha is negative, its private-credit growth relies on a retail channel now in outflow, and its two seats in private credit create a conflict it hasn't explained. 'Durable' was also the word for Marcus.
- **Synthesis:** the fee build is real and measurable. The private-credit position is a leveraged bet on underwriting quality and on the firm's ability to govern its own conflicts. The proof will be the 2027 vintage marks and whether the T. Rowe target-date funds take in net flows.

*Answers for (a)–(c):* (a) United Capital $750M / $25B = **3.0%**; Innovator ~$2.0B / $28B = **7.1%**; NEOS $2.25B / $30B = **7.5%**; LCN $410M / ~$3B = **13.7%** (8.7% upfront only). (b) 5% × $230bn = **$11.5bn a quarter**; $50bn / 11.5 ≈ **4.3 quarters**, $70bn / 11.5 ≈ **6.1 quarters**: roughly 1–1.5 years, consistent with "a year plus" (longer if NAV falls). (c) Bantwal: 15% × (1 − 0.5) = **7.5 points** of loss, so ~10% becomes **~2.5%**. Marks: 25% × 50% = **−12.5%** unlevered, **−25%** at 1:1 leverage. Marks's case is worse because it assumes a sector-wide wipe-out concentrated in one industry plus leverage. Both say *not systemic* because the losses sit with equity-like fund investors at low leverage (~1:1) rather than with deposit-funded banks.
