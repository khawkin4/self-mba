## Core claim

Markets are efficient enough that active stock-picking is a loser's game. So **build the portfolio from risk
premiums**, not forecasts. Own only premiums that pass a strict evidence test, diversify across as many
independent ones as you can, rebalance mechanically, and expect years of underperformance as the price of
admission.

His 2026 extension: a 60/40 portfolio is **not** diversified. Its risk is ~85–90% equity, and stocks and bonds can
fall together (1931, 1969, 2022). So he argues for swapping part of the bond sleeve for alternatives whose returns
come from different risks.

## Framework

### Step 2: which premiums qualify (Berkin & Swedroe, *Your Complete Guide to Factor-Based Investing*, 2016)
A premium must be:
1. **Persistent** across long periods and economic regimes
2. **Pervasive** across sectors, countries and asset classes
3. **Robust** to different definitions
4. **Implementable** after real-world costs
5. **Explainable** by a risk or behavioural story for why it should last

In the equity zoo of 400+ published factors, only **five pass**: beta, size, value, profitability/quality and
momentum. Across asset classes he adds **carry** and **defensive**.

### Step 2: valuation spreads are the timing signal you shouldn't trade on
- The value-vs-growth valuation spread is the best predictor of the value premium, much as market valuations are
  for the equity premium.
- It has **little short-term power** and useful power at **~10-year horizons**. Use it to hold through drawdowns,
  not to time entries.
- **Claimed evidence:**
  - Oct 2020 – Aug 2026: Dimensional US Marketwide Value returned 17.9%/yr, **+4.9pp/yr over CRSP 1-10**.
    International value returned 21.3%/yr, **+7.9pp/yr**.
  - AQR Style Premia (QSPRX, 10% vol target) lost **~29% cumulatively over 2018–2020**, then returned **18.9%
    annualized over 2021–2025**, with a Sharpe of about 1 and near-zero equity correlation.
  - **[Fund and index returns as he reports them; not independently checked. Start and end dates are chosen at a
    drawdown trough, which flatters the comparison.]**
- The lesson he draws: after a factor's drawdown, spreads widen, so **rebalance into it**. Selling is the error
  behind most investors' bad results. His examples are SRRIX buyers who sold after losses in 2017–19, then missed
  +44.6% (2023) and +33.1% (2024).

### Step 4: rebalancing rule (5/25)
- Rebalance an asset class when it drifts outside the **tighter** of **±5 percentage points** or **±25% of its
  target weight**.
  - 40% target: act at 35% or 45% (the 5pp band is tighter).
  - 10% sleeve: act at 7.5% or 12.5% (the 25% band is tighter).
- Check the bands on a schedule (quarterly is common) rather than daily.
- ⚠ **Not found in his 2025–26 Substack.** The rule comes from his older books. It is verified here only through
  secondary sources (Ben Carlson's 2014 *A Wealth of Common Sense* post and later summaries).
- **How he applies it to alternatives (2025–26 posts):** rebalancing is the mechanism that captures factor and
  alternative premiums after drawdowns. Add to a lagging sleeve when it breaches its band. Do not sell it on
  recency.
- **A 2026 complication he reviewed** (*When Everyone Trades the Same Factor Playbook*, 2026-05-25): crowded factor
  funds rebalance in the same direction at the same time ("anomaly-driven demand"). That is another argument for
  staggered rebalance dates (see Hoffstein's timing-luck point in the methodology note).

### Step 2: sizing the alternatives sleeve
- **1/N is a good starting point** across qualifying alternatives. It avoids pretending to know which will win. He
  then adjusts it four ways; the details are paywalled at Financial Advisor (Jan 2026).
- **The menu, with his named risks for each** (2026-08-20):
  - **Reinsurance:** catastrophe risk, unrelated to GDP. Risks: event clustering, underwriting cycles.
  - **Long-short factor funds:** model, crowding and execution risk.
  - **Senior secured, sponsor-backed private credit:** credit-cycle, liquidity and valuation risk.
  - **Private infrastructure:** regulatory and operating risk.
  - **Private real estate:** appraisal-smoothed volatility.
- **The three costs he says are real:**
  - **Illiquidity:** size it against actual spending needs, not risk tolerance.
  - **Complexity:** manager selection matters a great deal.
  - **Tracking-variance risk:** you will trail the S&P for years and want to quit. He calls this one purely
    behavioural.
- **His own portfolio (Aug 2026, "not a recommendation"):**

  | Sleeve | Weight |
  |---|---|
  | AQR long/short factor funds | 14% |
  | Reinsurance | 14% |
  | Private credit (CCLFX, CELFX, FRBBX) | 9% |
  | Non-traded REITs | 10% |
  | Infrastructure | 6% |
  | Other alternatives | → ~55% total |
  | Public equity | 25% |
  | Private equity | 11% (he counts it as equity) |
  | Money market | rest; **no traditional bonds** |

  - He can redeem at least **20% a year** of each illiquid sleeve, which he says is far above his spending.
  - This is a retiree's portfolio with funded goals. It is the opposite of a three-fund default. Treat it as one
    expert's preferences, not a template.

### Step 7: what the factor research implies for active slices
- Active funds lag after costs even when their stock picks are good. Trading costs and fund flows eat the edge
  ("good stock picks, bad trading", Aug 2026).
- Machine learning can't pick winning funds, but it helps screen out losers (May 2026).
- Benchmark every tilted or alternative sleeve against its own index **and** against the plain portfolio it
  replaced.

### Private credit: the questions he says to ask (Apr 2026)
- Are fee figures gross or net of leverage income? His CCLFX breakdown: ~1.50% management and operating costs,
  plus ~1.75% interest on borrowings, for ~3.25% "all-in".
- Are redemption figures **gross or net** of new subscriptions and reinvested dividends?
- What is the comparison benchmark?
- What does look-through leverage do to the NAV? His own arithmetic: at **~65% look-through leverage, a 10% fall
  in loan values cuts NAV by ~16–17%**.
- Has the fund lived through a full recession? CCLFX launched in **2019**. He concedes it has not.

## Applies when
- Building the core and tilt weights in an IPS (step 2): the five-criteria test is a good filter for *any* product
  pitch, not just factors.
- Writing the rebalancing rule before you need it (step 4).
- Deciding whether *any* illiquid sleeve fits: check liquidity needs first, tolerance second.

## Breaks when
- **Sample and start-date dependence.** Most of his 2026 "factors are back" evidence starts at the 2020 trough. The
  2009–2017 stretch with little value premium, and the 2018–20 drawdown, are just as real.
- **Appraisal-smoothed alternatives.** Private credit, non-traded REITs and interval funds report low volatility
  partly because they are marked infrequently by managers ("volatility laundering", a paper he reviewed himself
  in Aug 2026). Low reported correlation may be an accounting effect, so mean-variance logic overweights them.
- **Capacity and access.** Endowment-style portfolios assume long horizons and top-quartile managers. He notes
  that institutional success "should not be treated as automatic evidence" a strategy suits individuals.
- **Fees.** Interval and alternative funds charge 1–2%+ against index funds at 0.03–0.10%, plus tax inefficiency
  in taxable accounts.
- **Young accumulators.** A 30-year-old with high human capital and decades of savings ahead
  ([[unit-10-personal-finance-operating-system]]) gains less from replacing bonds with illiquid alternatives than
  a retiree does.

## ⚠ Bias: the CCLFX dispute, both sides

**Background.** CCLFX (Cliffwater Corporate Lending Fund) is a ~$33B private-credit interval fund. It must offer
to repurchase at least 5% of shares a quarter and may go to 7%. Swedroe has written repeatedly in favour of it
since at least Jun 2025 (*Why Cliffwater Was My Choice for Private Credit*).

**Swedroe, *Setting the Record Straight* (2026-03-09).** A rebuttal to an unnamed "recent piece of analysis":
- **Track record:** 9.54% net return since inception and no net realized losses. Calling out a short window is
  cherry-picking.
- **Cash and leverage:** low cash is deliberate (revolving credit replaces cash drag). Leverage is stable once all
  financing sources are counted.
- **Redemptions:** "nothing unusual" about them.
- **Unfunded commitments:** matched by contingent credit lines.
- **Auditor:** Cohen & Company is a reputable IPA-100 firm.
- **Marks:** the fund warehouses loans for other lenders that provide a valuation backstop, which can justify a
  higher mark on the same loan.
- **Process:** the critic never contacted Cliffwater.
- **No ownership disclosure appears in that free post** (checked in the pulled text).

**Nemeth, *Setting the Record Straighter* (Mispriced Assets, 2026-03-11).** The critique:
- **Undisclosed ownership.** Swedroe owns CCLFX and CELFX (he said so on a White Coat Investor podcast,
  Jan 2026), and roughly **10 pro-Cliffwater posts** since Mar 2025 carried a disclosure only once.
- **Timing.** Two days after "nothing unusual", **Bloomberg reported ~14% redemption requests against a 7% cap**.
  Cliffwater was also reported to be selling ~$1B of loans through Evercore.
- **Cash and debt.** Cash fell **76%** ($375M → $92M) against **$6.3B of unfunded commitments**. Total borrowings
  rose **$6.8B → $9.8B** in six months. Counting borrower-level fund leverage, the effective figure is 70–150%.
- **Marks.** Non-accruals are 0.63% against ~2.7% at public BDCs, which suggests the marks, not the loans, differ.
  The "valuation backstop" is a synthetic mark that does not appear in the filings.
- **Auditor.** It had PCAOB findings on Level 3 valuation work at a fund, in 2021 and 2017.
- **Stress model.** NAV falls from **$9.46 to $0.35** over eight quarters at a 10% loss rate. **[The critic's own
  model. Extreme, and not reviewed here.]**
- **Nemeth's incentives:** he says he is not short and cannot be. He does publish a paid Substack and a CCLFX
  "forensic audit" series, and attention is his business too.

**Swedroe's later response (no named reply to Nemeth found in the free posts).**
- ***Private Credit Under the Microscope*** (2026-04-02): aimed at Bloomberg's coverage.
  - Rebuts: the 3% fee figure (most is leverage interest), "fleeing" (the 7% is gross, and Cliffwater reported net
    inflows), concentration (top 25 credits ~12% vs ~60% at BDCs), and the CDO analogy (fund leverage ~0.2×,
    look-through ~0.65×).
  - **Concedes:** illiquidity is structural and redemptions can queue or be suspended. There has been no full
    recession test. Valuations can lag. Leverage amplifies losses.
  - **Adds a full disclosure** that he invests in CCLFX and CELFX and advises RIAs that allocate to it.
- ***The Financial Media Is Failing…*** (2026-06-09): requests hit ~17% in a later quarter. He argues gated funds
  invite over-requesting and that net flows matter. YTD to May 2026: 12% redeemed vs ~7% new subscriptions, ~1%
  reinvested dividends and ~12% loan repayments, so **net cash flow was positive**. **[His figures, unaudited.]**
- He has since carried a CCLFX disclosure on related posts (Apr 17, Apr 28, Jun 9, Jun 22, Aug 4, Sep 30) and has
  reviewed papers critical of private-credit marks: PIK-reliant BDCs mark identical loans higher (Oct 2026), and
  "volatility laundering" (Aug 2026).

**Fair reading.**
- The disclosure criticism was valid for the March post, and he fixed the practice afterwards.
- The redemption criticism was borne out in the short run: requests ran at 2–3× the cap for successive quarters.
- His gross-vs-net point is also correct arithmetic, and no forced-sale collapse had shown up in his data through
  May 2026.
- The real crux is unsettled: **are Level 3 marks right, and what happens in a true recession?** Swedroe now
  concedes this is untested.
- For this repo: treat his private-credit and interval-fund advice as **the view of an interested party**, and use
  his own four questions (fees net, flows net, benchmark, look-through leverage) on any fund he recommends.

## Practitioner counterpoint
- **Ben Felix / PWL** debated tilts vs plain cap-weight in 2025 (see the methodology note): factor advocates
  disagree among themselves on whether tilts are worth the tracking error.
- **Bogleheads three-fund** advocates would say his 55%-alternatives portfolio buys complexity and fees to fix a
  problem (stock-bond correlation) that shows up only in some years.
- **Moontower** ([[moontower-options-intuition]]) agrees on the volatility risk premium: Swedroe calls autocallable
  and put-writing income "real income for real risk, not a return of capital", which matches Moontower's "premium
  is compensation for underwriting, not income". Swedroe also publicly retracted his criticism of Calamos's CAIE
  after talking with the issuer (Jun 2026). That is the engagement he faulted Nemeth for skipping, and it is also
  how issuer access can soften an outside critic.

## The one-line habit
Write the band (the tighter of ±5pp or ±25% of target) next to every sleeve in the IPS. For any sleeve someone
recommends, ask the five-criteria test **and** "does the recommender own it?"
