# Note · BlackRock's two-front backlash — attacked as too green by Republican state officials and not green enough by the left, BlackRock exited pledges, settled cheaply, fought hard, and pushed the choice back to clients

> Governance / crisis note · Maps to **E6** (Crisis Leadership & Resilience), **E8** (Stakeholder & Investor Relations), **E4** (Corporate Governance & the Board), **Course 11** (Responsible Business), **Course 08** (Negotiation) · Written 2026-10-25 · Companion to [[blackrock-hub]], [[blackrock-fink-letters]], [[blackrock-outside-voices]]

- **Source:** all in `_ingest/raw/blackrock/articles.jsonl`:
  - stewardship and ESG releases: the 2020 net-zero offering and survey, the Bodnar hire (2021), Baringa (2021), and Voting Choice (2022, 2024);
  - newsroom statements: CA100+ (2024), NZAM (2025), the Tennessee AG case (2025), the AG letter on China (2025), the DOJ/FTC filing (2025) and NYC Comptroller Lander (2025);
  - the 2021–2023 AGM transcripts, including vote results;
  - the Fink letters.

  Political actors appear only as BlackRock describes them. Lawsuit texts are **not** in the corpus.
- **Core claim:** BlackRock's peak commitment was the January 2020 client letter: exit thermal coal, integrate ESG into 100% of active strategies, join CA100+, and be "the global leader in sustainable investing". From 2022 that commitment came under fire from both sides at once.
  - **From the right:** state anti-boycott laws, a Tennessee AG lawsuit, a Texas-led coal antitrust suit backed by DOJ/FTC, and 17 AGs on China disclosures.
  - **From the left:** shareholder proposals, the NYC Comptroller, and claimed European client losses.
  - **The response follows a consistent four-part playbook:**
    1. **exit** collective pledges and cite legal risk (CA100+, NZAM);
    2. **settle** where it is cheap, promising "transparency" (Tennessee);
    3. **fight** where the legal theory threatens the index model (the coal antitrust case);
    4. **hand the choice to clients** (Voting Choice, the Egan-Jones non-ESG policy, opt-in climate stewardship).
  - **One frame for both flanks.** The same *fiduciary / politicization* argument is used against critics on the right and on the left.

## 2. The playbook, move by move

| Move | When used | Example | Cost to BlackRock |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Exit and cite law** | Collective pledges that look like coordination | CA100+ (kept a non-US foothold through BlackRock International); NZAM | Reputational, mostly in Europe |
| **Settle cheaply** | State consumer-protection claims | Tennessee: "even greater transparency" | Disclosure, no stated policy change |
| **Fight** | Theories that threaten the index model | Coal antitrust: "based on an absurd theory that coal companies conspired with their shareholders to reduce coal production." | Legal fees; a loss would create a precedent against common ownership |
| **Go direct** | Factual disputes | The China letter answered on X via @BlackRock_Fact | Tone risk |
| **Hand choice to clients** | Proxy voting and stewardship | Voting Choice ($2T+ eligible in 2022); Egan-Jones; opt-in climate program offered to NYC plans | Gives up some voting influence |
| **Restate the fiduciary frame** | Every case | "It is our clients' money, not ours." (Fink, 2023 AGM) | None. The frame works in both directions |

## 3. The language trail

- **Risk factors:**
  - 2024 deal releases: "increasing focus from stakeholders regarding environmental, social and governance matters".
  - 2025 releases: the same line reads "environmental and social-related matters".
  - "highly qualified and diverse professionals" becomes "highly qualified professionals".
- **Board releases:** in 2023 the release counts "five are women". In 2024–26 they speak only of expertise and "unique perspectives".
- **Strategy pillars:** "leading in sustainable investing" is in the 2023 and 2024 letters and absent from 2025 on ([[blackrock-fink-letters]] §2).

## 4. What the claims don't prove

1. **There are no lawsuit texts in the corpus.** The AGs' and DOJ/FTC's own arguments are known only through BlackRock's rebuttals.
2. **The settlement terms are undisclosed** beyond "transparency".
3. **Client attrition is claimed but unquantified.** Paul Rissman said at the 2023 AGM that "BlackRock has begun to experience client attrition". BlackRock's record flows (2024–25) suggest any losses were small relative to the total, but flows by region and mandate aren't broken out.
4. **Voting Choice uptake** ($530B in 2022, ~$600B in 2024) is reported by BlackRock. How clients actually voted is not disclosed.
5. **"Legal considerations" is never explained.** Which laws, which risks? The CA100+ and NZAM statements don't say.

## 5. Strategic read (inference — not BlackRock's words)

- **Antitrust is the existential risk, not ESG.** A theory that index owners plus engagement equals collusion (the coal case) would threaten every large index manager's stewardship model. That explains why BlackRock fights this one hardest, and frames it in the administration's own language of energy independence.
- **Voting Choice is the structural defence.** If clients vote their own shares, BlackRock can say it holds no position. That defuses both flanks and shrinks the concentration argument used against it. It also weakens BlackRock's governance influence, which may be the point.
- **The left gets a sharper tone than the right.** Lander gets a jab ("as you pursue your next career opportunity"). State AGs get law and policy arguments. That tracks where the larger political and regulatory risk sat in 2025.
- **Europe is the unpriced exposure.** "100% of our largest client relationships in Europe" had net-zero commitments (NZAM statement). The US retreat creates mandate risk there that the corpus can't measure.

## 6. Canon lenses

- **Crisis communication** ([[canon-stone-difficult-conversations]], [[canon-patterson-crucial-conversations]]): separate the *what happened* conversation (facts: China disclosures) from the *identity* conversation (is BlackRock political?). BlackRock answers facts combatively and identity with the fiduciary frame.
- **Game theory** ([[canon-schelling-strategy-of-conflict]], [[canon-dixit-nalebuff-thinking-strategically]]): exiting pledges is a credible commitment *not* to coordinate, which signals to the antitrust plaintiffs. Voting Choice is a way of *tying one's own hands*.
- **Power** ([[canon-pfeffer-power]]): state treasurers control mandates, which gives them leverage. BlackRock answers by making itself less of a target, not by fighting every case.
- **Ethics** ([[canon-badaracco-defining-moments]], [[canon-sandel-justice]]): is a universal owner obliged to manage system-wide externalities, as The Shareholder Commons argued in 2021–22? BlackRock's answer is no, unless clients ask.
- **Allison's models** ([[canon-allison-essence-of-decision]]): read the NZAM exit as a rational actor (legal risk), as an organizational process (legal and compliance review), and as government politics (US vs Europe businesses inside BlackRock).

## 7. Practitioner counterpoint

- **For BlackRock:** a fiduciary serving Texas pensions and Dutch pensions can't take one side. Choice-based architecture is the honest answer. Record inflows suggest clients agree.
- **Against:** in 2020 BlackRock *chose* to lead and gained sustainable-AUM growth from it ("sustainable assets grew by 64% organically last year versus the industry's 27%", Fink, 2021 AGM). Exiting when the politics turned is the definition of a reversible commitment, and it teaches every stakeholder to discount the next one.
- **Both:** for any firm with a heterogeneous client base, the lesson is to make values commitments only where you can sustain them under a change of government.

## 8. Open questions to track

1. The outcome of the Texas-led coal antitrust case (motion to dismiss filed March 2025).
2. Do the NYC pension plans actually terminate the BlackRock index mandate?
3. Any EU regulatory or client response to the NZAM exit (e.g. SFDR fund reclassifications)?
4. Does Voting Choice grow past ~$600B, and does BlackRock ever publish aggregate client voting outcomes?

### Rep
**You run investor relations at BlackRock in May 2025. Write the 150-word holding statement for the DOJ/FTC filing**, then compare it with the actual one. Use these facts:
- BlackRock filed a motion to dismiss in March 2025.
- The suit is backed by Texas plus 12 AGs.
- BlackRock's flows were a record $641B in 2024.

Then do the numbers.

**(a)** If 2% of US public-pension index mandates (assume $1.2T) were pulled at an average fee of 2bp, what is the annual revenue loss?
*Answer:* $1.2T × 2% = $24B. $24B × 0.0002 = **$4.8M**, negligible against more than $20B of revenue.

**(b)** Explain why the revenue math understates the real risk. What does an adverse antitrust *precedent* do to the stewardship model across ~$5T of index equity?
