# Note · JPMorganChase vs the capital rules — one argument for six years (rules push intermediation out of banks and make Treasuries fragile); the posture went from "adjust" to "fight" to a qualified win

> Regulation / market-structure note · Maps to **Course 11** (Global Strategy, Macro & Responsible Business — regulation as a market force), **Course 03** (Microeconomics & Strategy Foundations — how rules shape industry structure), **E9** (Geopolitics & Macro for Decision-Makers), **E8** (Stakeholder Relations — lobbying in a shareholder letter) · Written 2026-10-25 · Companion to [[jpmc-markets-venues]] and [[jpmc-capital-allocation]]

- **Source:** `_ingest/raw/jpmc/articles.jsonl`: Dimon's letters AR2019–AR2025 (the regulation sections), the 4Q20, 4Q21, 4Q22, 1Q23, 4Q24, 4Q25 and 2Q26 calls, Barnum's regulation section at the 2025 Investor Day, the 2020-06-29 capital update, the 2024-06-27 stress-test statement, and the 2025-12-19 Treasury-clearing explainer. Rule text itself (Basel III endgame proposals, eSLR, G-SIB method) was **not** read. Every number below is JPMC's own claim about the rules.
- **Core claim:** The argument never changes. Capital and liquidity rules (G-SIB surcharge, SLR, LCR, Basel III endgame, CCAR) treat Treasuries, repo and market-making as riskier than they are. That shrinks dealer balance sheets relative to markets, pushes lending and trading to nonbanks that "do not step in" under stress, and makes the Treasury market fragile, so the Fed ends up as the intermediary. What changed is the **posture**: no relief requested (2020) → "red lines" and repo dislocations (2021) → "we will simply have to adjust" (2023) → quantified opposition, "+25%" capital and "+50%" for market-making (2024) → public dispute with the Fed (2024) → "un-American" G-SIB surcharge but "mixed" re-proposals and a pivot to liquidity rules under deregulation (2026). It is the dealer's half of the Treasury market-structure story that Tradeweb tells from the venue side.

## 1. Chronology of the argument

| Date | Item | What JPMC says (verbatim where quoted) |
|---|---|---|
| 2020-04-06 | AR2019 letter (COVID) | "we will not request any regulatory relief for ourselves." Yet rules "can improperly prevent healthy, well-capitalized banks from lending freely in times of stress." Calls for a later review of "poorly coordinated, poorly calibrated and poorly organized rulemaking." |
| 2020-06-29 | First Stress Capital Buffer | SCB 3.3%, raising the standardized CET1 minimum from 10.5% to 11.3%. Buybacks halted under the Fed directive. Dimon: "We will continue to maintain a fortress balance sheet that allows us to do our most important job" |
| 2021-01-15 | 4Q20 call | QE-driven deposit growth makes SLR and G-SIB "binding". Piepszak: "satisfying these heightened requirements is certainly not costless". Dimon: "If we were on the international standard, our G-SIFI would be 2%, not 4%." |
| **2021-04-07** | AR2020 letter | "Over the past two years, we saw significant dislocation in the U.S. Treasury (UST) repo markets, which were certainly linked to these regulations." LCR, SLR and G-SIFI "created red lines that we cannot cross." "if large players cannot intermediate in markets because of regulatory requirements, the Fed will have to do it" |
| 2022-01-14 | 4Q21 call | Barnum: "we don't expect the Basel III end game in and of itself to increase the dollars of capital that we need to carry" |
| 2022-04-04 | AR2021 letter | "Banks’ dramatic decline in marketmaking ability relative to the size of the public markets is a factor in the periodic disruptions". "The U.S. implementation of G-SIB requirements does not enable a level playing field — plain and simple." G-SIB costs ~$30B of extra Tier 1 versus a European basis and drags ROE by ~15% (Dimon's estimate) |
| 2023-04-04 | AR2022 letter (written during SVB) | Basel endgame "seems likely to increase, yet again, capital requirements for banks in general" and for trading in particular. "we will simply have to adjust to them immediately." Six responses, including "some credit is better held in a nonbank." Worry list includes "large dysfunctional markets", naming the Gilt and Treasury markets |
| 2023-04-14 | 1Q23 call | Endgame now "likely to be worse". Dimon: "We do expect higher capital from Basel IV, effectively and, obviously there's going to be an FDIC assessment". Also: "no bank should keep a loan, if possible. That's how much capital is now being required for loans." |
| **2024-04-08** | AR2023 letter: new section on protecting market-making | Endgame "would increase our firm’s required capital by 25%, making our requirement 30% higher than it would be under the equivalent European Union proposal." "The new rules would raise capital requirements by 50% for major banks" (market-making). "Some regulators seem to think that market making is a speculative, hedge fund-like activity" |
| 2024-06-27 | Stress-test statement | The Fed's OCI benefit "appears to be too large". Standardized CET1 15.0% vs 11.9% required, "approximately $54 billion of excess capital" |
| 2025-04-07 | AR2024 letter | "The supplementary leverage ratio and G-SIB capital rules also treat U.S. Treasury securities and repurchase agreements as far riskier than they actually are." QE/QT plus "the restrictions put on market making by primary dealers – will likely lead to much higher volatility in the treasury markets." "The CCAR stress test, in particular, is highly flawed." |
| 2025-05-19 | Investor Day (Barnum) | Endgame severity "now seems less likely". "SLR should clearly be fixed, it's a low-hanging fruit, it should be done, and it will certainly bring some benefits somewhere." Wants a holistic look at "the last 10 years of capital and liquidity rules" |
| 2025-12-19 | Treasury clearing explainer | SEC mandate (Dec 2023): FICC and CME Securities Clearing approved; "Other clearing houses, such as ICE, are seeking regulatory approval." Cash Dec 31 2026, repo Jun 30 2027. JPM Securities is a direct participant offering sponsored ("done-with") clearing |
| 2026-01-13 | 4Q25 call | Dimon on capital buffers: "There should be no buffers." |
| **2026-04-06** | AR2025 letter | "Our initial reactions to the revised Basel 3 Endgame and GSIB proposals are mixed." "The GSIB surcharge is still broken." At ~5.0%: "Frankly, it’s not right, and it’s un-American." Deregulation: "Positive effects of comprehensive deregulatory policies. This was badly needed and long overdue." Liquidity rules lock up over $1T, and credit for the discount window would add almost $500B of lendable liquidity (Dimon's estimates). "market makers are less able to intermediate in extremely volatile markets" |
| 2026-07-14 | 2Q26 call | Four fixes: "They should get rid of the double count in operating risk capital. They should get rid of the double count in market risk capital." Plus G-SIB recalibration and short-term wholesale funding. Barnum: the system is "flush with capital, but at the margin, less flush with liquidity" |

## 2. The market-making defence in numbers (AR2023 letter, 2024-04-08)

| Item | JPMC claim |
|---|---|
| Support cost of market-making | ~$7B a year, "including over $2 billion in technology spend alone each year" |
| Capital in Markets | ~$70B |
| Securities inventory | ~$500B, "largely hedged" |
| Daily notional | ~$2T bought and sold |
| Revenue | ~$100M on a typical day, ~$30B a year; a fee of "one hundredth of a cent" per dollar of notional |
| Loss record | No quarterly loss in 10 years; lost money on 30 trading days (~1%); March 2020: two loss days, +$2.5B for the month |
| Industry inventory | Banks hold "70% as much inventory in securities as they did before the 2008 financial crisis" while markets have almost tripled |
| CCAR market shock | A hypothetical $18B one-day loss with no recovery |

The same letter's contrast: "Many large market participants — for example, hedge funds and high-frequency traders, among others — have no obligation to make markets." The AR2022 version is blunter: "many of the new “shadow bank” market makers are fair-weather friends —they do not step in to help clients in tough times."

## 3. What the claims don't prove

1. **The repo link is asserted, not shown.** "certainly linked to these regulations" is a claim. The letters give no balance-sheet data showing JPM hit its SLR or G-SIB constraint on the September 2019 or March 2020 dates. Other explanations (reserve scarcity, Treasury settlement, basis-trade unwinds) are not addressed.
2. **Capital impact numbers are JPMC's own models.** "+25%", "+50%" and the 30%-above-EU figure were computed by the bank lobbying against the rule. No independent estimate is in the corpus.
3. **The no-loss-quarter record argues both ways.** It shows a well-run trading business. It also shows the stress the rules target hasn't happened since 2008, which is what a regulator would expect from a well-capitalised dealer.
4. **"Fair-weather friends" lacks data.** No market-share-in-stress statistics are given for nonbank market makers. JPM's own e-trading survey says volatility is traders' biggest daily challenge for four years running ([[jpmc-markets-venues]]), which is consistent with either side.
5. **Deregulation benefits are framed as client benefits.** "hundreds of billions of dollars in extra lending annually" (AR2024) and lower mortgage costs are plausible but are also shareholder returns. Barnum himself warns of "managing expectations".

## 4. Strategic read (inference — not JPMC's words)

- **Why the posture flipped in 2024.** In April 2023 the bank was absorbing SVB-era scrutiny and buying First Republic at regulators' request; adjusting quietly was the cheap move. By April 2024 the endgame proposal was public and quantifiable, peers were litigating, and the politics were shifting. Opposition became cheap. Dimon's numbers arrived exactly when they could matter for the re-proposal.
- **The real asset is the balance sheet, not the venue.** Dimon never talks about venues ([[jpmc-markets-venues]]). He talks about inventory, repo and capital. In a world where all-to-all and Treasury clearing move execution onto platforms, the bank's edge is that it can warehouse risk in stress. The regulation fight is a fight to keep that edge cheap.
- **Treasury clearing is the test.** Cleared cash Treasuries (Dec 2026) and repo (Jun 2027) should net dealer exposures and release balance sheet, which is a stated aim in JPM's explainer ("increase the intermediation capacity of dealers"). If SLR relief also arrives, the binding constraint Dimon described in 2021 loosens. Then the argument that rules caused fragility can be checked against the next stress episode.
- **Nonbank growth is partly a choice the bank encouraged.** "some credit is better held in a nonbank" (2023) and the $50B direct-lending commitment (2025) show JPM both blaming and feeding the shift. The 4Q25 call calls NBFI lending regulatory "arbitrage" while disclosing ~$160B of JPM exposure to it.
- **Liquidity is the next front.** Barnum's "less flush with liquidity" and Dimon's ">$1T" locked-up claim mark where 2027 lobbying goes: LCR treatment, discount-window credit, short-term wholesale funding in G-SIB.

## 5. Canon lenses

- **Industry structure** ([[canon-porter-competitive-strategy]]): regulation acts as a cost on one class of competitor (G-SIBs) and as a subsidy to substitutes (nonbanks, principal trading firms). Dimon's competitor list adds Citadel Securities in 2026 ([[jpmc-mission-forces]]).
- **Normal accidents** ([[canon-perrow-normal-accidents]]): tightly coupled Treasury, repo and futures markets with thin intermediary capacity are Perrow's recipe for surprise. Dimon's "red lines" are a coupling argument.
- **Antifragility and buffers** ([[canon-taleb-antifragile]]): Dimon wants buffers usable in stress ("There should be no buffers" on top of requirements). Taleb would separate redundancy you can spend from redundancy locked by rule.
- **Agency in lobbying** ([[canon-jensen-meckling-agency-theory]]): the agent (bank management) argues for rules that raise returns and claims clients benefit. Read the client-benefit claims with that incentive in view.

## 6. Practitioner counterpoint

- **Bank view (JPMC):** rules built after 2008 overlap and double count. They were calibrated for a different rate world. They made the safest dealers least able to provide liquidity when it's most needed. The 2020 Fed intervention proves the point.
- **Regulator view (not in the corpus; inferred from the rules' stated aims):** capital is the cost of the implicit public backstop. G-SIB surcharges price systemic size. The Fed stepped in during 2020 because of a pandemic, not because of the SLR.
- **Venue view** ([[tradeweb-derivatives-regulation]]): Tradeweb welcomes clearing and transparency mandates because they push flow onto platforms. That is the same rules, read as opportunity rather than cost.

### Rep
Using only §2: (a) If ~$30B of annual Markets revenue comes from ~$2T of daily notional, what is the implied revenue per $1M traded, assuming ~250 trading days? Does that square with "one hundredth of a cent"? (b) If the endgame raises capital for market-making by 50% on ~$70B, how much extra capital is that, and what pre-tax return on it would hold the Markets ROE constant if Markets earns ~$30B revenue at a ~50% overhead ratio? (c) Write a 150-word reply from a Fed staffer to Dimon's "fair-weather friends" line, using one fact from §3.

*Answer for (a):* $30B ÷ (250 × $2T) = $30B ÷ $500T ≈ **$60 per $1M**, or 0.006 cents per dollar. That is the same order of magnitude as "one hundredth of a cent" (0.01 cents, or $100 per $1M). The letter rounds up. *(b):* 50% × $70B = **$35B** extra capital. Pre-tax profit ≈ $30B × 50% = $15B on $70B (≈21%). Holding that return on $105B needs ≈ $22.5B pre-tax, which is **+$7.5B** from the same business. That is why the bank fought.
