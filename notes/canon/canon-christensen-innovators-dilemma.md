# Note · Christensen — *The Innovator's Dilemma*

> Canon · Cluster: Landscape & Competitive Awareness · Maps to **Course 04** (Competitive Strategy), **Course 10** (Entrepreneurship)

- **Source:** Clayton Christensen, *The Innovator's Dilemma* (1997)
- **Core claim:** Good companies fail **not despite doing everything right, but because of it.**
  Rational, customer-driven decision-making can be the *mechanism* of your own disruption.
- **Framework:**
  - Incumbents listen to their best customers, invest in their most profitable (sustaining)
    products, and *rationally* ignore small, low-margin emerging markets.
  - **Disruptors enter at the bottom** (or create a new market) with a product that's **worse on
    traditional metrics but better on a new dimension** the incumbent isn't tracking — convenience,
    price, simplicity, accessibility.
  - The disruptor improves until it's "good enough" on the traditional metric — and by then it's too
    late for the incumbent to respond.
  - **Sustaining vs. disruptive innovation** is the core distinction; incumbents reliably win the
    former, lose the latter.
- **Applies when:** evaluating a cheap/"inferior" new entrant — the question is *which dimension* it
  competes on, not whether it's currently worse than you.
- **Breaks when:** the theory is **over-applied** — not every cheap entrant is a disruptor; many are
  just bad products that stay bad (Christensen famously mis-predicted the iPhone as non-disruptive).
  It's strong on *why* incumbents fail, weak as a *prediction* tool (hard to tell ex-ante which
  low-end entrant has the improvement trajectory). And the prescription — "set up an autonomous unit"
  — frequently fails in practice. Pairs with Grove's inflection points
  ([[canon-grove-only-the-paranoid-survive]]) and Helmer's counter-positioning.
- **Practitioner counterpoint:** *(researched 2026-06-06 — HBR "Disruptive Innovation Explained" transcript, youtube.com/watch?v=qDrMAzCHFUU)*
  Christensen himself supplies the cleanest *targeting* heuristic, and the book-specific transcript
  preserves it verbatim: when Andy Grove pressed him on what disruption meant for Intel, Christensen
  refused to prescribe — *"if I'd have been suckered into telling Andy Grove what he should do, I'd
  have been killed because he knew so much more about microprocessors than I ever would know."* He
  handed over the *theory*, and Grove worked out the answer himself: *"Grove said, oh I get it… they
  had two companies coming at him from below and Intel needs to go down and not let them go up against
  us."* That's the founder's correct use of the book — a **lens you apply to your own market, not a
  forecast you outsource to the author.** The same transcript flags the over-application risk too:
  Christensen's confidence reads as *post-hoc* (mini-mills, PCs, smartphones all narrated after the
  fact), which is exactly why the r/strategy practitioner canon leans on operational trade-off books
  — Lafley/Martin's "**Playing to Win**" (102↑) and "**Bulletproof Problem Solving**" (87↑) — that
  force ex-ante where-to-play/how-to-win choices Christensen's taxonomy can't.

- **Researched layer** *(updated 2026-06-06)*
  - **Canonical explanation (sourced):** Christensen's mechanism is the *rational* trap. A disruptive
    innovation *"generally has to cause you to go after new markets, people who aren't your
    customers… something that is just so much more affordable and simple that your current customers
    can't buy it"* — so the incumbent's choice becomes *"should we make better products that we could
    sell for better profits to our best customers? Or maybe we ought to make worse products that none
    of our customers would buy that would ruin our margins"* (Clayton Christensen, HBR — "Disruptive
    Innovation Explained," youtube.com/watch?v=qDrMAzCHFUU). Listening to your best customers is the
    *mechanism* of failure, not a deviation from doing things right.
  - **Real application (mined):** the Oxford lecture works the cases in full detail. **Steel
    mini-mills** entered at *rebar* — *"a doggy dog commodity… their gross margins were only 7%"* — so
    *"the integrated mills were just relieved to get out of the rebar business"* and fled up-market
    chasing the *"12% gross margins"* a tier above, exactly as the disruptor wanted: *"in pursuit of
    profit, the giant is motivated to flee rather than fight you."* The auto case mirrors it — Toyota
    came in not with Lexuses but *"rusty little subcompacts in the 1960s called Coronas"* so cheap
    that *"the rebar of humanity, people we call college students, can own a car,"* then marched up to
    Lexus; *"Kia and Hyundai have stolen the bottom of the market from Toyota… why would they ever
    want to defend the lowest profit part of their product line when they have the privilege of
    competing in luxury cars against Mercedes?"* (Saïd Business School, Oxford,
    youtube.com/watch?v=rpkoCZ4vBSI).
  - **Where the corpus pushes back on the book:** the theory is *descriptively* gorgeous and
    *predictively* weak. Every case in the transcript is narrated in **hindsight** — even Christensen's
    cleanest win (Intel) works *because he refused to predict* and handed Grove a lens instead. The
    r/strategy practitioner canon routes around this gap by privileging trade-off frameworks that
    force a decision *before* the outcome is known: "**Playing to Win**" (102↑) and "**Bulletproof
    Problem Solving**" (87↑) top the list, while *Innovator's Dilemma* itself isn't the thread people
    reach for — the "explains everything, predicts nothing" worry, made concrete.
  - **Sources in corpus:** Harvard Business Review — "Disruptive Innovation Explained"
    (youtube.com/watch?v=qDrMAzCHFUU) · Saïd Business School, Oxford — "Clayton Christensen: Disruptive
    innovation" (youtube.com/watch?v=rpkoCZ4vBSI) · Startup Grind — "Clayton Christensen on How to
