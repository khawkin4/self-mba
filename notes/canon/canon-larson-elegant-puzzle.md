# Note · Larson — *An Elegant Puzzle*

> Canon · Cluster: Org Design & Mechanisms · Maps to **Course 09** (Leadership & OB), **E1** (Leading at Scale)

- **Source:** Will Larson, *An Elegant Puzzle: Systems of Engineering Management* (2019)
- **Core claim:** Engineering organizations are **systems with throughput constraints** — manage
  them by state, not by motivation. Organizational debt compounds like financial debt.
- **Framework:**
  - **Four team states:** *falling behind* → *treading water* → *repaying debt* → *innovating*. You
    manage each differently.
  - **Key counterintuitive rule:** you **can't fix a falling-behind team by adding goals** — you fix
    it by **adding people or cutting scope**. (Adding goals to an overloaded team makes it worse.)
  - Practical systems: team sizing, managing migrations, organizational debt, when to centralize vs.
    federate.
- **Applies when:** a team is underwater and leadership's instinct is to "set clearer priorities" —
  Larson says that's the wrong lever; change the load, not the exhortation.
- **Breaks when:** it's written from a **high-growth, well-funded tech** context — "just add
  headcount" assumes you *can* hire, which fails in constrained or non-tech orgs where cutting scope
  is the only lever. The four-state model is clean but real teams sit in several states at once. And
  the systems lens, like Meadows', can under-weight when the bottleneck is genuinely one person.
- **Practitioner counterpoint:** *(researched 2026-06-05 — r/ExperiencedDevs + r/managers corpus + Insight Partners author talk)*
  Larson's cut-scope lever is exactly what the top-voted r/ExperiencedDevs essay "**You should really
  consider letting some plates hit the floor**" (2672↑) argues from the floor: when leadership downsizes
  and "the consequences of those decisions often fall on the employees," the move isn't to work harder
  but to *deliberately drop work* — "you don't have to let the stress from those decisions drown you."
  **But the corpus also exposes the lever practitioners can't pull:** in "**I'm giving up; Becoming a yes
  man**" (1668↑) an engineer documents that org-debt repayment is *unrewarded* — "it doesn't matter that
  spending 20% more now means every quarter for the next 3 years we spend 10% less… none of it ever
  matters," only "brand new features" get credit. Larson names the state correctly; the incentive system
  often won't let an IC act on it.
- **Researched layer** *(2026-06-05)*
  - **Canonical explanation (sourced):** Larson lays out the four-state model in his own words — the
    worst state is *falling behind*, where *"each day you wake up and there's more tickets for you than
    the day before because you simply can't process the work at the rate that it's coming in,"* and the
    only move is to *"make brutal choices about what to do… think about what can I do today that will
    create capacity to succeed tomorrow"* (Insight Partners, "Insights From the Author of *An Elegant
    Puzzle*"). Critically he warns: *"if you optimize for the short-term when you're already falling
    behind… you're literally doomed"* — capacity, not effort, is the constraint. His sizing companion is
    equally blunt: *"divide the entire headcount by eight, that's the number of teams… divide the number
    of teams by six, that's the number of managers… I've never found any way that works better than this"*
    (the "rule of 6 and 8").
  - **Real application (mined):** Larson's own Uber case — joining an infra team where *"each day we got
    a request to provision a couple more micro services… we were just further behind every single
    morning… we had to make some really hard choices about not supporting people who desperately needed
    support."* That is the four-state model applied: stop adding goals, cut the load, buy back capacity.
  - **Where the corpus pushes back on the book:** practitioners confirm the *diagnosis* but flag a
    *political* gap. The r/ExperiencedDevs "let some plates hit the floor" essay (2672↑) shows cutting
    scope is psychologically hard precisely because management's downsizing "bets" push the overload back
    onto ICs; and "I'm giving up; Becoming a yes man" (1668↑) shows the debt-repayment quadrant is often
    *unrewarded* by promotion systems that only credit "brand new features." Larson's lever is correct;
    whether an EM or IC is *allowed* to pull it is the missing variable.
