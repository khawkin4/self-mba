# Note · Dixit & Nalebuff — *Thinking Strategically*

> Canon · Cluster: Game Theory & Strategic Interaction · Maps to **Course 03** (Micro & Strategy Foundations), **Course 04** (Competitive Strategy)

- **Source:** Avinash Dixit & Barry Nalebuff, *Thinking Strategically* (1991)
- **Core claim:** Every strategic interaction means **anticipating what others will do in response to
  what you do** — applied game theory for non-mathematicians.
- **Framework — the core tools:**
  - **Dominant strategy** — a move that's best regardless of what others do (rare, powerful when it
    exists).
  - **Nash equilibrium** — a stable state where no one gains by *unilaterally* deviating (not
    necessarily good for anyone — see the prisoner's dilemma).
  - **Backward induction** — solve from the end state backward to find what to do now ("look forward,
    reason back").
  - **Credible commitment** — make threats/promises believable by *limiting your own options*
    (Schelling's idea, [[canon-schelling-strategy-of-conflict]]).
  - **Repeated games change everything** — when you'll interact again, cooperation becomes rational
    ([[canon-axelrod-evolution-of-cooperation]]).
- **Applies when:** pricing wars, negotiations, market entry, capacity decisions — any move whose
  payoff depends on rivals' reactions.
- **Breaks when:** clean game theory assumes **rational, payoff-maximizing players with known
  payoffs** — real people are predictably irrational ([[canon-ariely-predictably-irrational]]) and
  the *real* payoff matrix is usually unknown. Many games have **multiple equilibria** and the theory
  can't say which one occurs (that's where Schelling's focal points come in). It's a *framing* tool —
  brilliant for structuring the interaction, weak at producing a single number.
- **Practitioner counterpoint:** (fill via `research` → r/strategy, r/consulting) — the usable habit:
  before any competitive move, **write the rival's best response** and assume they'll play it. Most
  strategy errors are ignoring the second move.

### Rep
Take one competitive move you're considering. Draw the 2×2 payoff grid (you × them). Use **backward
induction**: what's *their* best response, and does your move still win once they make it?
