# Note · Dixit & Nalebuff — *Thinking Strategically*

> Canon · Cluster: Game Theory & Strategic Interaction · Maps to **Course 03** (Micro & Strategy Foundations), **Course 04** (Competitive Strategy)

- **Source:** Avinash Dixit & Barry Nalebuff, *Thinking Strategically* (1991)
- **Core claim:** Every strategic interaction means **anticipating what others will do in response to
  what you do** — applied game theory for non-mathematicians.
- **Framework — the core tools:**
  - **Dominant strategy** — a move that's best regardless of what others do (rare, powerful when it
    exists).
  - **Nash equilibrium** — a stable state where no one gains by *unilaterally* deviating (not
    necessarily good for anyone — see the prisoner's dilemma).
  - **Backward induction** — solve from the end state backward to find what to do now ("look forward,
    reason back").
  - **Credible commitment** — make threats/promises believable by *limiting your own options*
    (Schelling's idea, [[canon-schelling-strategy-of-conflict]]).
  - **Repeated games change everything** — when you'll interact again, cooperation becomes rational
    ([[canon-axelrod-evolution-of-cooperation]]).
- **Applies when:** pricing wars, negotiations, market entry, capacity decisions — any move whose
  payoff depends on rivals' reactions.
- **Breaks when:** clean game theory assumes **rational, payoff-maximizing players with known
  payoffs** — real people are predictably irrational ([[canon-ariely-predictably-irrational]]) and
  the *real* payoff matrix is usually unknown. Many games have **multiple equilibria** and the theory
  can't say which one occurs (that's where Schelling's focal points come in). It's a *framing* tool —
  brilliant for structuring the interaction, weak at producing a single number.
- **Practitioner counterpoint:** *(researched 2026-06-05 — r/GAMETHEORY corpus + Dixit/Distinctive Voices transcripts)*
  The usable habit holds — before any competitive move, **write the rival's best response** and assume
  they'll play it; most strategy errors are ignoring the second move. But the corpus exposes a sharper
  gap: r/GAMETHEORY is dominated by **textbook puzzles** (Monty Hall 44↑, "infeasible strategies in a
  centipede game" 44↑, dominance-vs-domination confusion 42↑) — not deals people actually closed. The
  most-upvoted *applied* attempts are people teaching themselves the practicality, e.g. a **"2025
  Government Shutdown Extended Form Game Theory Help"** post (208↑) from someone "**very new to game
  theory… trying to learn its practicality**," and a plain **"What would I be able to do if I learn game
  theory?"** thread (28↑). The recurring pain point mirrors the book's own warning: the framework is
  easy to *read* and hard to *deploy live* — which is why the top tool-post is **"Game Theory Arcade"**
  (31↑), a lab for "learning core game-theory ideas by **actually playing them rather than just reading
  about them**… 'rational' one-shot decisions often perform badly over repeated interactions."
- **Researched layer** *(2026-06-05)*
  - **Canonical explanation (sourced):** Dixit frames the whole discipline as the *"systematic analysis
    of strategic interactions"* where *"what you get out of the interaction depends on what everybody
    else is doing… people are aware of this and try and take advantage of what they can do to pursue
    their best interests"* (Distinctive Voices — Avinash Dixit, "The Art of Strategy"). The 5-Minute
    Book Summary distills the operating method to one line: strategic thinking is *"anticipating the
    actions and reactions of others… looking ahead and reasoning backward, a process known as backward
    induction"* — exactly the note's "look forward, reason back."
  - **Real application (mined):** Dixit's own go-to illustration of **credible commitment** is mundane,
    not military — *"you make a commitment by putting the alarm clock on the other side of the room so
    that you have to get out of bed"* (Distinctive Voices). It's a game *against your own future self*:
    you win by **limiting your own options**, the same mechanism that makes a price floor or a burned
    bridge work against a rival. (His richer worked case is **Dr. Strangelove** — *"the best movie
    that's nothing but game theory in all of its 90 minutes"* — a doomsday device as a commitment that
    backfires precisely because the other side didn't *know* about it, i.e. an uncommunicated threat is
    no threat.)
  - **Where the corpus pushes back on the book:** the live r/GAMETHEORY corpus is almost entirely
    **closed-form puzzles** (Monty Hall, centipede games, exam problems), not boardroom moves — the
    practitioner translation the book promises is thin in the wild, and newcomers openly ask "**what
    would I be able to do if I learn game theory?**" (28↑). The other reality-check: the **"Game Theory
    Arcade"** post (31↑) reports that **"'rational' one-shot decisions often perform badly over repeated
    interactions"** — confirming the note's "repeated games change everything" caveat is where the clean
    theory bends toward cooperation.

### Rep
Take one competitive move you're considering. Draw the 2×2 payoff grid (you × them). Use **backward
induction**: what's *their* best response, and does your move still win once they make it?
