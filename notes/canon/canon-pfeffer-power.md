# Note · Pfeffer — *Power*

> Canon · Cluster: Power & Organizational Politics · Maps to **Course 09** (Leadership & OB), **E1** (Leading at Scale)

- **Source:** Jeffrey Pfeffer, *Power: Why Some People Have It — and Others Don't* (2010)
- **Core claim:** Organizational power **doesn't come from being right.** It comes from controlling
  resources, building alliances, managing information, and understanding who actually decides. People
  who ignore power dynamics don't transcend them — they just **lose to people who don't.**
- **Framework:**
  - **Sources of power:** control of resources, position in the information/communication network,
    alliances, reputation, and personal qualities (will, energy, focus, ambition, political skill).
  - **Build power deliberately:** get into a department/role *near the power*, build a personal brand,
    network *before* you need it, ask for things, and don't wait to be recognized on merit.
  - Performance ≠ advancement; **visibility + relationships with decision-makers** often matter more.
- **Applies when:** you need to *get things done* in any real organization — Pfeffer's uncomfortable
  honesty is that political skill is a prerequisite, not a dirty word.
- **Breaks when:** the playbook can curdle into **pure careerism** that erodes trust and, at scale,
  the org itself — power *acquired* is not the same as power *deployed well* (the values question
  Pfeffer brackets out). It also reads as cynical-by-design; in genuinely high-trust, mission-driven
  teams, naked power-seeking backfires and Carnegie's "make others feel valued"
  ([[canon-carnegie-win-friends]]) outperforms. And Munger's lens applies: understand the
  *incentives* before you assume someone's power play is what it looks like
  ([[canon-munger-poor-charlies-almanack]]).
- **Practitioner counterpoint:** *(researched 2026-06-06 — Stanford GSB Pfeffer "Power" book talk, youtube.com/watch?v=0eFln_mdXGY)*
  Pfeffer's own framing is blunter than the book's tidy summary: *"the biggest barrier I think for
  us having power is of course ourselves — we are often times not willing to do what it takes or to
  make the tradeoffs required to have power."* He names the specific self-defeats: we *"worry
  excessively about what others, particularly peers, think"* — yet *"your direct hierarchical peers
  are also your competitors"* under any up-or-out. And the deepest trap is the just-world reflex:
  students tell him *"these people will get it in the end, so I don't have anything to learn from
  them,"* and he counters that *"because we believe in a just world we often times don't pay
  attention to the more interesting people in the organization,"* leaving us *"less vigilant and
  strategic than required."* The cynical extreme is Greene ([[canon-greene-48-laws-of-power]]) —
  whose own summary admits *"a lot of it's about defense… knowing that there are malevolent,
  aggressive, toxic people out there."*

- **Researched layer** *(updated 2026-06-06)*
  - **Canonical explanation (sourced):** Pfeffer's core mechanism is that power is *learnable and
    earned, not deserved* — and that most people refuse to learn it because it offends their sense
    of fairness. The first practical move is diagnostic: *"diagnose who has power — not just the
    formal power but the informal power; oftentimes it's an assistant or a secretary or people who…
    aren't that high up in the organization chart"* — then *"figure out how to get closer to the
    people with power and get those people on their side"* (Stanford GSB, "Why Cultivating Power is
    the Secret to Success"). Power *"is in fact all around them, kind of like air and water and
    gravity."* The contrarian career move follows directly: *"why would you go to the same firms
    that hire all of the best and brightest from all the same schools that you're graduating from?
    Go find the niche"* (Stanford GSB, "Power: Why Some People Have It").
  - **Real application (mined):** the concrete playbook is mundane and tactical — do *"small tasks
    that make those other individuals' lives easier,"* network and build social relationships,
    **flatter** (*"one of the best ways to make people think well of themselves is to flatter them"*),
    and **find unexploited resources** (*"organizations have budgets that will permit you to put
    together a speaker series… dinners or lunches… that put yourself in the center of a series of
    relationships"*). The hardest discipline is reallocating time: *"if you spend all your time with
    your current close friends, how do you ever make new ones?"* — the useful contacts are the
    **weak ties** who carry *"non-redundant information,"* not the roommates you already ski with.
  - **Where the corpus pushes back on the book:** Pfeffer is candid that the playbook has a real
    price. On authenticity he is provocative: *"your job as a leader is not to be authentic or
    genuine… your job is to be true to what the situation requires of you"* — you must *"sometimes
    display emotions that you're not really feeling… but it's what success requires"* (Stanford GSB,
    "Why Cultivating Power"). And on the personal cost: *"there's an enormous price for having
    power… most successful people that I know have to work very, very hard,"* trading the people you
    *want* to be with for the people you *need* to be with. This is exactly the careerism the note's
    *Breaks-when* warns about — power *acquired* is not power *deployed well*, and the Greene strand
    shows how fast "take the credit" curdles.
