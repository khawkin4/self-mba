# Note · Pfeffer — *Power*

> Canon · Cluster: Power & Organizational Politics · Maps to **Course 09** (Leadership & OB), **E1** (Leading at Scale)

- **Source:** Jeffrey Pfeffer, *Power: Why Some People Have It — and Others Don't* (2010)
- **Core claim:** Organizational power **doesn't come from being right.** It comes from controlling
  resources, building alliances, managing information, and understanding who actually decides. People
  who ignore power dynamics don't transcend them — they just **lose to people who don't.**
- **Framework:**
  - **Sources of power:** control of resources, position in the information/communication network,
    alliances, reputation, and personal qualities (will, energy, focus, ambition, political skill).
  - **Build power deliberately:** get into a department/role *near the power*, build a personal brand,
    network *before* you need it, ask for things, and don't wait to be recognized on merit.
  - Performance ≠ advancement; **visibility + relationships with decision-makers** often matter more.
- **Applies when:** you need to *get things done* in any real organization — Pfeffer's uncomfortable
  honesty is that political skill is a prerequisite, not a dirty word.
- **Breaks when:** the playbook can curdle into **pure careerism** that erodes trust and, at scale,
  the org itself — power *acquired* is not the same as power *deployed well* (the values question
  Pfeffer brackets out). It also reads as cynical-by-design; in genuinely high-trust, mission-driven
  teams, naked power-seeking backfires and Carnegie's "make others feel valued"
  ([[canon-carnegie-win-friends]]) outperforms. And Munger's lens applies: understand the
  *incentives* before you assume someone's power play is what it looks like
  ([[canon-munger-poor-charlies-almanack]]).
- **Practitioner counterpoint:** *(researched 2026-06-05 — Stanford GSB Pfeffer transcript corpus; r/cscareerquestions pull returned empty, flagged for re-pull)*
  Pfeffer's own framing is blunter than the book's tidy summary: *"why don't people have power…
  the biggest barrier I think for us having power is of course ourselves — we are often times not
  willing to do what it takes or to make the tradeoffs required"* (Stanford GSB). The recurring
  self-defeat he names is the just-world reflex — students tell him *"these people will get it in
  the end, so I don't have anything to learn from them,"* and he counters that believing the world
  is fair makes you *"less vigilant and strategic than required."* The hard truth people relearn:
  doing great work and waiting to be noticed loses to those who manage visibility and relationships.
  The cynical extreme is Greene ([[canon-greene-48-laws-of-power]]) — whose own summary admits
  *"a lot of it's about defense… knowing that there are malevolent, aggressive, toxic people out
  there."*
- **Researched layer** *(2026-06-05)* *(corpus thin — reddit pull empty, flagged for re-pull)*
  - **Canonical explanation (sourced):** Pfeffer's core mechanism is that power is *learnable and
    earned, not deserved.* The first practical move is diagnostic: *"diagnose who has power — not
    just the formal power but the informal power; oftentimes it's an assistant or a secretary or
    people who… aren't that high up in the organization chart"* — then *"figure out how to get
    closer to the people with power and get those people on their side"* (Stanford GSB, "Why
    Cultivating Power is the Secret to Success"). Power *"is in fact all around them, kind of like
    air and water and gravity."*
  - **Real application (mined):** the concrete playbook Pfeffer gives is mundane and tactical —
    do *"small tasks that make those other individuals' lives easier,"* network and build social
    relationships, **flatter** (*"people like to think well of themselves… one of the best ways to
    make people think well of themselves is to flatter them"*), and **find unexploited resources**:
    *"organizations have budgets that will permit you to put together a speaker series that will let
    you meet people… dinners or lunches… that put yourself in the center of a series of
    relationships."* In the fireside chat he compresses this into rules — *act and speak with power,
    build a strong personal brand* (*"I was a troublemaker then, I still am — that's my brand"*),
    *network relentlessly,* and *use your power* once you have it.
  - **Where the corpus pushes back on the book:** the same transcript that sells power also carries
    the cost — Pfeffer's later work finds *"the workplace is the fifth leading cause of death in the
    United States, responsible for 120,000 excess deaths a year."* The power playbook is morally
    neutral by design; the corpus's own author concedes power *acquired* and power *deployed humanely*
    are different problems, and the Greene strand shows how fast "get others to do the work and take
    the credit" curdles into the careerism the note warns about.
