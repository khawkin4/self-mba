# Note · Brandenburger & Nalebuff — *Co-opetition*

> Canon · Cluster: Game Theory & Strategic Interaction · Maps to **Course 04** (Competitive Strategy)

- **Source:** Adam Brandenburger & Barry Nalebuff, *Co-opetition* (1996)
- **Core claim:** Business is **not zero-sum.** You simultaneously **compete and cooperate** within a
  **Value Net**, and the highest-leverage move is often to **change the game itself**, not to win the
  existing one.
- **Framework:**
  - **The Value Net** — four players around you: **customers, suppliers, competitors, and
    complementors** (someone whose product makes yours more valuable — hot dogs & buns, hardware &
    software). Most strategy ignores the complementor axis.
  - **PARTS** — the five levers to change the game: **P**layers, **A**dded value, **R**ules,
    **T**actics, **S**cope.
  - Key move: **adding/strengthening a complementor** can beat fighting a competitor — grow the pie
    before fighting over slices.
- **Applies when:** mapping an ecosystem and looking for non-obvious allies — platform plays,
  standards, bundling, partnerships — rather than head-to-head rivalry.
- **Breaks when:** "co-opete" relationships are **unstable** — today's complementor is tomorrow's
  competitor once they **envelop** your space (the platform tension in
  [[canon-cusumano-business-of-platforms]]). "Change the game" is easy to say, hard to do — most
  firms lack the market power to rewrite rules. And the friendly "grow the pie" framing can lull you
  into underrating a partner who's quietly accumulating bargaining power
  ([[canon-porter-competitive-strategy]]).
- **Practitioner counterpoint:** *(researched 2026-06-05 — r/GameTheory + Veritasium/Dixit-Nalebuff transcripts)*
  The book's central wager — that business is **not zero-sum** — is exactly the conclusion the
  cooperation literature keeps reaching the hard way. Veritasium's tournament write-up ("This game
  theory problem will change the way you see the world," 25k-word transcript) lands on it bluntly:
  *"most of life is not zero sum. To win, you don't need to get your reward from the other player…
  Cooperation pays even among rivals."* **But the recurring practitioner pain point is application,
  not the idea:** r/GameTheory's "**What would I be able to do if I learn game theory?**" (28↑) is a
  textbook user asking what it's actually *for*, and the puzzle-heavy feed there (Monty Hall, "the
  Sole button" coordination trap at 33↑, clone-prisoner's-dilemma at 37↑) shows the gap Co-opetition
  also has to cross — the durable move is still **ask "who gains from my success?"**, invest in
  complementors, and watch for the day the complementor's incentives flip.
- **Researched layer** *(2026-06-05)*
  - **Canonical explanation (sourced):** The Value Net stands on a non-zero-sum foundation, and the
    corpus states that foundation cleanly — *"In games like chess or poker… one person's gain is
    necessarily another person's loss, so these games are zero sum. But most of life is not zero
    sum… you can get [your reward] from the banker"* (Veritasium, "This game theory problem will
    change the way you see the world"). That "banker" is Co-opetition's enlarged pie: you grow total
    value *before* fighting over slices, which is why **adding a complementor can beat fighting a
    competitor.**
  - **Real application (mined):** Axelrod's iterated tournament is the mined case for *how* you
    sustain co-opetition. Veritasium distills the winning posture into four traits — *"they were all
    nice, which just means they are not the first to defect"* plus **forgiving, retaliatory, and
    clear** — and notes *"the worst performing nice strategy still far outscored the best performing
    nasty one."* That is the operating manual for a complementor relationship: cooperate by default,
    punish defection, forgive fast. Dixit & Nalebuff's *Art of Strategy* summary (5-Minute Book
    Summary) frames the same choice — players *"must decide whether to compete or cooperate… where
    cooperation can lead to better outcomes for all involved but where the temptation to defect can
    undermine collective success"* — which is precisely the instability the note's "breaks-when"
    flags.
  - **Where the corpus pushes back on the book:** the practitioner reality-check is that "change the
    game" is intuitive but operationally vague. r/GameTheory skews to clean math puzzles, not messy
    Value-Net mapping; the "Game Theory Arcade" post (31↑) admits *"'rational' one-shot decisions
    often perform badly over repeated interactions"* — i.e. the payoff only shows up if the game
    truly repeats, and many real complementor deals are one-shot or get **enveloped** before the
    cooperative payoff compounds. The friendly "grow the pie" framing can also mask a partner quietly
    banking bargaining power.
