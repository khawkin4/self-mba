# Note · Drucker — *The Effective Executive*

> Canon · Cluster: Org Design & Mechanisms · Maps to **Course 09** (Leadership & OB), **E11** (Personal OS)

- **Source:** Peter Drucker, *The Effective Executive* (1967)
- **Core claim:** **Effectiveness is a habit, not a talent** — a set of practices anyone can learn.
  The scarcest resource isn't intelligence; it's *usable, contiguous time*.
- **Framework — the five practices:**
  1. **Know where your time goes** — track it; you'll be surprised (and appalled).
  2. **Focus on contribution** — ask what *you* can contribute, not what you need to do.
  3. **Build on strengths** — yours and others'; don't waste effort fixing weaknesses.
  4. **Concentrate** — on the few major areas where superior performance yields outstanding results;
     **first things first, and one at a time.**
  5. **Make effective decisions** — few but fundamental, based on the *right question*, not consensus.
  - Underappreciated core: executives have **almost no usable time blocks**; reclaiming contiguous
    time is the first task.
- **Applies when:** you feel busy but not productive — Drucker's diagnosis is that you've never
  measured where your time actually goes or decided what *only you* can contribute.
- **Breaks when:** "build on strengths, ignore weaknesses" can rationalize neglecting a *fatal* gap
  (a brilliant exec who can't be trusted). "Few fundamental decisions" assumes a stable environment;
  in fast-moving domains, many small reversible bets beat few big ones (Bezos's two-way doors,
  [[canon-bezos-shareholder-letters]]). And "focus on contribution" presumes you correctly know what
  the org most needs — a diagnosis that can be wrong.

  exercise is the single most-cited "actually changed how I work" takeaway across decades.
