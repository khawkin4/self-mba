# Note · Helmer — *7 Powers*

> Canon · Cluster: Landscape & Competitive Awareness · Maps to **Course 04** (Competitive Strategy), **Course 12** (Capstone)

- **Source:** Hamilton Helmer, *7 Powers: The Foundations of Business Strategy* (2016)
- **Core claim:** There are **exactly seven** sources of *durable* competitive advantage. Most
  claimed "advantages" don't survive the test — if you can't name which power you have or are
  building, you probably don't have one.
- **Framework — the 7 Powers:**
  1. **Scale economies** — unit cost falls with volume.
  2. **Network economies** — value rises with number of users.
  3. **Counter-positioning** — you adopt a model the incumbent *can't* copy without damaging their
     existing business (the deepest and most overlooked power).
  4. **Switching costs** — customers face loss/friction from leaving.
  5. **Branding** — a *justified* perception of quality that supports pricing power.
  6. **Cornered resource** — exclusive access to a valuable input (a patent, a key person, a deposit).
  7. **Process power** — embedded organizational capability that can't be copied quickly (e.g. Toyota).
  - A real power = a **Benefit** (it improves your economics) **that persists** (a Barrier the
    competitor can't or won't cross). Helmer's rigor is the *Barrier* test.
- **Applies when:** stress-testing any strategy or moat claim — "how will we win" (from
  [[canon-lafley-martin-playing-to-win]]) must resolve to *one of these seven* or it's a wish.
- **Breaks when:** the taxonomy is a **static classification, not a playbook** — it tells you what a
  power *is*, not how to *originate* one (Helmer's "invention" stage is thin). Powers can also
  erode (yesterday's network effect undone by multi-homing) — durability is relative, not absolute.
  And counter-positioning, the prized power, is the hardest to recognize before it's obvious.
  Sharper successor to Porter's generic strategies ([[canon-porter-competitive-strategy]]).
- **Practitioner counterpoint:** *(researched 2026-06-05 — r/strategy corpus + Lenny's Podcast/Business Architect transcripts)*
  The corpus confirms the discipline's real value is **forcing you to name the specific barrier** —
  Helmer's own framing on Lenny's Podcast is the practitioner antidote to lazy moat claims: *"in a
  lot of decks it's like 'oh we have the most amazing team, we move the fastest'… you're on a
  treadmill, and if you stop running that treadmill you get creamed — but it's not power. The things
  that drive operational excellence can be mimicked."* **But r/strategy practitioners push back on
  *applying* it:** the most-upvoted threads aren't about taxonomy at all — "**Most organizations
  don't need a new strategy**" (45↑) argues the real failure mode is *execution and alignment*, not
  a missing power ("the strategy isn't broken — the communication and ownership are"), and an MBA-bound
  poster asking "**Which of these books is good for corporate strategy?**" (34↑) gets pointed back to
  Porter, not Helmer. The taxonomy wins arguments; it doesn't run the offsite.
- **Researched layer** *(2026-06-05)*
  - **Canonical explanation (sourced):** Helmer reduces strategy to one equation — *"power requires
    a benefit and a barrier… [Buffett] is taking care of the benefit part by saying a castle — you
    have to have a pretty good understanding of why it's a castle and not a shack"* (Lenny's Podcast).
    The Barrier is the whole game: a benefit competitors can copy is just a treadmill. The Business
    Architect explainer sharpens the same point — without a structural barrier, *"competitive
    arbitrage"* relentlessly copies your innovation *"until your profit margins completely vanish."*
  - **Real application (mined):** Helmer's own worked example — **Netflix's scale economies**: content
    is *"a very large fixed cost, about 50% of their cost structure… they spread that fixed cost over
    more subscribers, so their cost per subscriber is less"* than a smaller rival's. Contrast the
    *non*-power: when **Blockbuster** finally launched mail-order DVDs, *"you couldn't tell it
    different from Netflix — they just copied it,"* proving UI/operational cleverness was mimicable,
    not a moat. He also stresses the powers are **sequenced**: nearly every startup *"starts with
    counter-positioning,"* the one refuge from an incumbent who already has the capabilities to
    extend into your space.
  - **Where the corpus pushes back on the book:** practitioners treat 7 Powers as a *diagnostic
    checklist*, not an operating plan. r/strategy's recurring complaint is that org performance
    problems are misdiagnosed as strategy problems ("Most organizations don't need a new strategy,"
    45↑) — and the book is silent on the *origination* and execution work that actually builds a
    barrier, exactly the "invention stage is thin" gap noted above.

### Rep
Take one company you admire. Name its single strongest power from the seven — and the *specific
barrier* that stops competitors from copying it. If you can't name the barrier, it's not a power.
