# Note · Lafley & Martin — *Playing to Win*

> Canon · Cluster: Strategy · Maps to **Course 04** (Competitive Strategy), **Course 12** (Capstone)

- **Source:** A.G. Lafley & Roger Martin, *Playing to Win* (2013)
- **Core claim:** Strategy is **choice** — and above all the choice of *where you won't play*.
  The default failure mode is "playing to play": participating everywhere with no theory of winning.
- **Framework — the strategy cascade (5 nested choices):**
  1. **Winning aspiration** — what does winning look like?
  2. **Where to play** — which markets, segments, channels, geographies?
  3. **How to win** — the value proposition that lets you win *there* specifically.
  4. **Capabilities** — what must you be able to do, distinctively?
  5. **Management systems** — what supports and measures the above?
  - Choices must **nest coherently**: each level's choices fit inside the level above.
- **Applies when:** you need to convert ambition into operational choice, at any org level —
  the cascade works for a company, a function, or a single team.
- **Breaks when:** the cascade is clean on paper but assumes you *know* where you can win — in a
  genuinely new market (disruption, see [[canon-christensen-innovators-dilemma]]) the "how to win"
  is unknowable in advance and must be discovered by experiment. Also: "where to win" needs a real
  source of advantage behind it, or it's just a wish — pressure-test "how to win" against
  [[canon-helmer-7-powers]]. A cascade with no diagnosis underneath is fluff
  ([[canon-rumelt-good-strategy]]).
- **Practitioner counterpoint:** *(researched 2026-06-05 — r/strategy corpus + Lenny's Podcast / ArtCenter transcripts)*
  Practitioners credit the cascade's *operational* edge over abstraction: a r/strategy "**Playing to
  Win: How Strategy Really Works (one paragraph review)**" thread (102↑) singles out *"how simple and
  operational the core framework is — choosing where to play and how to win, and then forcing real
  trade-offs instead of vague 'we want to be the best in everything' thinking."* **But the recurring
  reality-check is that most orgs misdiagnose:** a "**Most organizations don't need a new strategy**"
  thread (45↑) argues the strategy is usually fine — *"the communication and operating rhythm"* (i.e.
  the cascade's box #5, management systems) is what's actually broken. Martin himself names the deeper
  failure: *"I have never met this mythical beast called a great natural strategist. Great strategists
  have all one thing in common — they just practice"* (Lenny's Podcast).

- **Researched layer** *(2026-06-05)*
  - **Canonical explanation (sourced):** The cascade exists to kill "playing to play." Martin frames
    it as five linked questions — *"What's your winning aspiration? Where to play? How can you win?
    What capabilities do you have to have that your competitors don't? And then, what enabling
    management systems do you have to put in place?"* (Lenny's Podcast). The mechanism is **focus**:
    *"You're not going to play everywhere, in every product, at every vertical stage around the world"*
    — and *"where to play is always a choice… it's extremely hard to win if you haven't defined very
    carefully where you're going to play"* (ArtCenter College of Design).
  - **Real application (mined):** Martin's worked example is **Intuit**: Quicken users kept calling
    support saying *"it doesn't have an accounts receivable and accounts payable package,"* and reps
    answered *"well, it's for people, not for businesses."* Intuit re-drew its *where-to-play* to
    *include* small-business software — *"and what came out of that? QuickBooks"* (ArtCenter). The
    "how to win" half is binary in his telling: *"you have to be either differentiated or low cost —
    there's no way to protect yourself if you're not one of those two"* (Lenny's Podcast).
  - **Where the corpus pushes back on the book:** the top r/strategy review concedes the cascade is
    *"more of a clean executive framework than a deep exploration of strategy in messy, dynamic
    environments"* (102↑) — i.e. it assumes you can name where you'll win, which a genuinely new
    market won't let you do. And the "Most organizations don't need a new strategy" thread (45↑)
    relocates the real failure to **alignment and operating rhythm**, not the five boxes themselves —
    a clean cascade on the wall changes nothing if KPIs *"are tracked, but not reviewed."*
