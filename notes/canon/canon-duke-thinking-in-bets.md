# Note · Duke — *Thinking in Bets*

> Canon · Cluster: Risk, Uncertainty & Fragility · Maps to **Course 07** (Data & Decision-Making), **E2**, **E11**

- **Source:** Annie Duke, *Thinking in Bets: Making Smarter Decisions When You Don't Have All the
  Facts* (2018). Duke is a former professional poker player with a background in cognitive psychology.
- **Core claim:** Life is **poker, not chess**. Outcomes come from two things, **decision quality and
  luck**, and with hidden information you can't read one from the other on a single result. So treat
  every decision as a **bet**: a probability, a stake, and a payoff. Then judge the bet, not how it
  turned out.
- **Framework:**
  - **Resulting** — treating the outcome as a perfect signal of decision quality. A good decision can
    lose and a bad one can win. This is the book's central error to unlearn.
  - **"Wanna bet?"** — the question that turns a belief into a confidence level. Saying "I'm 70%"
    instead of "I'm sure" makes you consider the 30% where you're wrong.
  - **Sort outcomes into skill vs. luck** before learning from them. Blaming bad luck for your losses
    and crediting skill for your wins is self-serving bias, and it stops you from learning either way.
  - **Truth-seeking group** — a small group with explicit rules: reward accuracy over agreement,
    welcome dissent, and update on evidence. Duke borrows Merton's **CUDOS** norms from science
    (share the data, judge claims on their merit regardless of source, have no stake in the
    conclusion, and stay skeptical).
  - **Mental time travel** — tools that bring your future self into the decision. **Pre-mortem**
    (assume it failed and explain why), **backcasting** (assume it succeeded and work back), **10-10-10**
    (how will this feel in 10 minutes, months, years?), and **Ulysses contracts** (commit in advance
    so your future self can't back out).
- **Applies when:** decisions repeat often enough that process matters more than any one result, and
  luck is large enough to fool you: hiring, investing, product bets, sales forecasts, project portfolios.
- **Breaks when:**
  - **"Bad luck" becomes a permanent alibi.** Separating skill from luck can turn into the very
    self-serving bias the book warns about. One result says little, but a *string* of bad outcomes is
    evidence about the process ([[canon-mauboussin-success-equation]] on how many results you need
    before you can tell).
  - **It offers no stake.** The book makes you state a probability but says little about **how much to
    bet** on it. A well-calibrated 60% placed with too much leverage still ruins you
    ([bet-sizing-conviction](../bet-sizing-conviction.md); Kelly as the formal answer).
  - **Poker has known odds; business doesn't.** The 82% in a poker hand is computable. A company's
    "70%" is an estimate, sometimes with no reference class to check it against
    ([[canon-taleb-black-swan]]). Expected-value thinking needs a cap on ruin alongside it.
  - **Organizations punish bad outcomes anyway.** A truth-seeking group needs incentives that don't
    reward resulting; most performance reviews do exactly that ([[canon-jensen-meckling-agency-theory]]).
- **Practitioner counterpoint:** Reality largely agrees, and the strongest evidence comes from
  markets. In the Elm Wealth experiment, people given the next day's newspaper still lost money,
  while the five professional traders profited by betting in proportion to their confidence
  ([bet-sizing-conviction](../bet-sizing-conviction.md)). The pushback from practitioners is about
  **novel bets**: a podcast host asks how to put a probability on a startup "no one's ever walked."
  Duke's answer is that almost nothing is fully without precedent, so start from the closest reference
  class and adjust. That is Tetlock's outside view ([[canon-tetlock-superforecasting]]).

- **Researched layer** *(added 2026-10-04)*
  - **Canonical explanation (sourced):** Duke defines resulting as *"using the outcome quality as a
    perfect signal for deriving decision quality"* (Big Think). Her standard example is **Super Bowl
    XLIX**: Pete Carroll called a pass on the one-yard line, it was intercepted, and he was called an
    idiot. Imagine the pass caught and the same headlines praise his genius. Whether the ball was
    caught *"does not actually change whether the decision was a good one."* With Mark Manson she
    states the two-factor model: luck and decision quality are what determine how life turns out,
    because decisions *"change the distribution of possible outcomes"* available to you.
  - **Real application (mined):** Duke says to write the reasoning down for high-stakes calls only:
    written out, you're *"less likely to make a mistake"* and it's *"easier to spot"* one (Odds on
    Open). She then adds a pre-mortem, and is explicit that it's for big decisions, *"not for ordering
    off a menu."* For the truth-seeking group, a book-summary podcast reduces it to three ground rules:
    reward accurate assessments over confirming beliefs, welcome dissent, and agree to update on new
    evidence (Growth in Minutes). Her own example of the group in action is mentor **Erik Seidel**
    refusing to listen to her bad-luck stories (Innovation Show).
  - **Where the corpus pushes back on the book:** the Odds on Open host presses on bets with no track
    record or extreme variance, like a new kind of startup, where the expected-value inputs are guesses.
    Duke answers with reference classes, but the gap is real, and it's exactly where the Taleb notes
    begin. A second gap: across all six transcripts, *how much* to commit barely comes up. "Thinking in
    bets" gets reduced to "state a probability," and the stake half of the bet drops out.

### Rep
Pick one outcome from the last month, good or bad. Write two lines: **what you knew and decided at
the time** and **what was luck**. If the answer to the second line is "everything bad," you're
resulting in reverse. For your next real decision, state it as a full bet: **odds, stake, and what
would make you lose.**
