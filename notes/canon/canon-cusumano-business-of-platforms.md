# Note · Cusumano, Gawer & Yoffie — *The Business of Platforms*

> Canon · Cluster: Network Effects & Platform Strategy · Maps to **Course 04** (Competitive Strategy), **E10** (Digital & AI Transformation)

- **Source:** Michael Cusumano, Annabelle Gawer & David Yoffie, *The Business of Platforms* (2019)
- **Core claim:** Not all platforms are alike — and the durable ones manage a few brutal strategic
  tensions deliberately.
- **Framework:**
  - **Two types:** **Innovation platforms** (others build *on top* of you — iOS, AWS, Salesforce) vs.
    **transaction platforms** (you facilitate *exchange* — Uber, Airbnb, marketplaces). The most
    durable are **hybrids**.
  - **The hard strategic choices:**
    - **How open?** — too open loses control/quality; too closed limits growth.
    - **How to price each side?** — usually *subsidize the harder-to-attract side*
      ([[canon-hagiu-wright-multi-sided-platforms]]).
    - **Multi-homing** — users on multiple platforms weaken your network effects; raise switching
      costs or exclusivity to fight it.
    - **Platform envelopment** — when (and whether) to compete with your own ecosystem partners; it
      *always* creates tension and can poison the ecosystem.
- **Applies when:** running or investing in a platform that must decide openness, pricing, and how to
  treat its own complementors.
- **Breaks when:** the strategic-choice menu is clean but **executing it is political and dynamic** —
  the "compete with your ecosystem?" call (Sherlocking) buys short-term revenue at long-term trust
  cost, and the book can't tell you the threshold. Also written near the **techlash/antitrust**
  inflection — the "envelop everything" playbook now collides with regulation in ways the framework
  underweights. Complementor relationships are the unstable co-opetition of
  [[canon-brandenburger-nalebuff-coopetition]] at scale.
- **Practitioner counterpoint:** *(researched 2026-06-05 — Talks at Google / NFX transcript corpus; r/startups thin)*
  Operators reframe Cusumano's "manage the tensions" as a *sequencing* problem: you can't envelop,
  price, or fight multi-homing on a network that doesn't exist yet. Andrew Chen (*The Cold Start
  Problem*, Talks at Google) insists the early network is hand-built, not designed — the founders who
  won "sat down, and they did the work… **they manually did it, and then they found ways to
  operationalize it.**" NFX sharpens the *why it's worth it*: of four defensibilities (scale, switching
  costs, brand, network effects), the network effect is **"the greatest of them all… because it's native
  to the digital environment"** — "the more users you get… the more ahead you'll get of your
  competitors, so the faster you go the faster you go." The practitioner caution the book underplays:
  **viral growth is not a network effect** ("a viral effect is about acquisition… they're actually very
  different things") — cheap acquisition can mask a network that never reaches the tipping point.
- **Researched layer** *(2026-06-05)*
  - **Canonical explanation (sourced):** the engine under both platform types is **demand-side
    economies of scale** — *"more users leads to more value for existing users… so we call this strong
    demand-side economies of scale"* (David Halliday, "2-Sided Markets"). Where a traditional firm
    profits on a markup over cost of goods, a transaction platform "**never has a traditional cost of
    goods sold… [it] exists to facilitate that transaction,**" and at scale these markets tip toward
    **"winner-take-all… markets where barriers to entry grow dramatically as the incumbent companies
    are growing."** That tipping dynamic is exactly why Cusumano's openness/pricing/multi-homing
    choices are existential rather than cosmetic.
  - **Real application (mined):** the Airbnb cold-start, told from the demand side — early on
    "**no pictures, no inventory, no booking. You literally would just email them**" (Andrew Chen).
    The fix was a hand-built **atomic network** ("the smallest network that can stand on its own"),
    repeated until **"the tipping point, which is when you're able to create these networks on demand…
    and then escape velocity."** The Halliday transcript names the flip side: as 2011–12 Airbnb filled
    up, *"that raises price and… decreases the overall availability"* — early subsidy and abundance
    erode once the market clears, the practical face of Cusumano's "how to price each side."
  - **Where the corpus pushes back on the book:** the transcripts argue the binding constraint isn't
    *choosing* among strategic tensions — it's **reaching critical mass at all**. Defensibility is
    earned by manual, un-scalable early work and by not mistaking **viral acquisition for a genuine
    network effect** (NFX). Cusumano's clean two-type taxonomy says little about the messy,
    operations-heavy zero-to-tipping-point phase where most platforms actually die.
