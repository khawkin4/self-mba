# Note · Mankiw — *Principles of Economics*

> Canon · Cluster: Economics & Incentive Design · Maps to **Course 03** (Micro & Strategy Foundations)

- **Source:** N. Gregory Mankiw, *Principles of Economics* (1998) — the standard intro text
- **Core claim:** A handful of principles explain most economic behavior; for operators, **two are
  transformative: opportunity cost and marginal thinking.**
- **Framework — the ten principles (compressed):**
  - *How people decide:* people face **tradeoffs**; the cost of something is **what you give up**
    (opportunity cost); rational people **think at the margin**; people **respond to incentives.**
  - *How people interact:* **trade** can make everyone better off; **markets** usually organize
    activity well; **governments** can sometimes improve outcomes (externalities, public goods,
    market power).
  - *How the economy works:* living standards depend on **productivity**; printing money causes
    **inflation**; short-run **inflation–unemployment** tradeoff.
  - **The two that change how you decide:** **opportunity cost** (the real cost of any choice is the
    next-best alternative forgone) and **marginal thinking** (decide at the *edge* — the next unit —
    not the average).
- **Applies when:** any resource-allocation or pricing decision — and as the literacy floor under the
  whole program (Course 03's backbone).
- **Breaks when:** the **rational-actor / efficient-market** scaffolding is exactly what the
  behavioral shelf demolishes — people are **predictably irrational** ([[canon-ariely-predictably-irrational]],
  [[canon-kahneman-thinking-fast-and-slow]]), markets have **information asymmetry**
  ([[canon-akerlof-market-for-lemons]]) and **agency problems**
  ([[canon-jensen-meckling-agency-theory]]). Treat Mankiw as the *baseline model* whose failures the
  rest of the canon maps — useful precisely because reality deviates from it in patterned ways.
- **Practitioner counterpoint:** *(researched 2026-06-05 — r/AskEconomics + Marginal Revolution University transcripts corpus)*
  The two ideas operators use daily are **"what am I giving up?"** (opportunity cost) and **"what's
  the marginal unit worth?"** — but the corpus shows the *interesting* questions are exactly where
  Mankiw's baseline breaks. A top r/AskEconomics thread — "**Why can the US government successfully
  run a massive grocery chain for the military (commissaries), but municipal-run grocery stores for
  the public often fail?**" (1293↑) — is a pure incentives/subsidy puzzle the ten-principles frame
  *names* but doesn't resolve. "**Are SNAP benefits essentially subsidies for corporations who don't
  pay a living wage?**" (1399↑) is an incidence question dressed as a moral one. And the half-joking
  "**If I had \$500B, bought a single piece of cake from my friend for half a trillion dollars, then
  cleaned his glasses in exchange for half a trillion, would the nation's economy increase by one
  trillion?**" (992↑) is a clean test of whether learners actually grasp value-added vs. transactions.
  The teaching corpus confirms the operator value isn't the model's elegance but its *failure modes*:
  MRU's principal-agent video frames it as "**conflicting incentives and you don't have the
  information to know a good deal from a bad deal**" — i.e. the incentive principle is only useful
  once you add information asymmetry. Munger's "think at the margin" and incentives overlap
  ([[canon-munger-poor-charlies-almanack]]).
- **Researched layer** *(2026-06-05)*
  - **Canonical explanation (sourced):** Mankiw's fourth principle — *people respond to incentives* —
    is only operationally useful once you specify *who knows what*. The corpus's principal-agent
    transcript states the mechanism cleanly: a problem *"can arise when the incentives of two parties
    in a transaction are not aligned… he can lie to charge more"* and, crucially, *"the principal-agent
    problem usually arises when there is asymmetric information — when one party in a transaction has
    more and better information than the other"* (Marginal Revolution University, "What Is the
    Principal-Agent Problem?"). The market-for-lemons transcript shows the same baseline failing:
    *"the sellers know the quality of their car, but… the buyers can't tell which used cars are lemons"*
    → high-quality sellers exit until *"the market collapses"* in an adverse-selection death spiral
    (MRU, "Asymmetric Information and Used Cars").
  - **Real application (mined):** the r/AskEconomics commissary thread (1293↑) asks why a
    federally-run grocery chain (DECA, ~23% savings to military families) works while municipal
    food-desert stores fail — an opportunity-cost-and-subsidy comparison where the answer is *who
    bears the cost*, not whether markets "usually organize activity well." The corpus's own teaching
    examples are decision-grade: the mechanic who *"recommend\[s\] more service than is necessary"* so
    you *"pass on those repairs for fear of being ripped off"* — Mankiw's efficiency vanishing the
    moment information is one-sided.
  - **Where the corpus pushes back on the book:** practitioners and educators treat the ten principles
    as a *floor*, not a model of reality. Business School 101's agency-theory video opens by assuming
    *"individuals are generally egoists who act in their own self-interest"* and that *"agents have
    access to more information"* — exactly the rational-actor scaffolding the behavioral and
    information shelves dismantle. The recurring r/AskEconomics pattern (SNAP-as-corporate-subsidy,
    the "\$500B cake" GDP riddle) is laypeople intuiting that **incidence and value-added** — not the
    headline principle — are where the real reasoning lives.

### Rep
Take a decision you're weighing. Write its **opportunity cost** (the best thing you'd give up), then
reframe it **at the margin** (is the *next* unit of effort/spend worth its *next* unit of return?).
