# Note · Fisher & Ury — *Getting to Yes*

> Canon · Cluster: Negotiation & Influence · Maps to **Course 08** (Negotiation), **E7** (Exec Presence & Comms)

- **Source:** Roger Fisher & William Ury, *Getting to Yes* (1981, Harvard Negotiation Project)
- **Core claim:** **Principled negotiation** beats positional bargaining. Most negotiations have
  *compatible interests* hiding behind *incompatible positions*.
- **Framework — the four principles + BATNA:**
  1. **Separate the people from the problem** — attack the issue, not each other.
  2. **Focus on interests, not positions** — a position is *what* they say they want; an interest is
     *why*. Trade on the why.
  3. **Generate options for mutual gain** *before* deciding — expand the pie first.
  4. **Insist on objective criteria** — anchor on a fair standard, not a willpower contest.
  - **BATNA** (Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement) — the single most important idea: your
    power = how good your walk-away alternative is. Improve your BATNA before you negotiate.
- **Applies when:** the negotiation is repeated, relationship-preserving, and both sides can
  rationally see mutual gain — partnerships, internal deals, vendor terms.
- **Breaks when:** it assumes **rational, good-faith** counterparties — against a hardball or
  emotional adversary, "focus on interests" gets steamrolled. Voss's whole critique is that people
  *aren't* rational and the emotional dynamics drive the outcome
  ([[canon-voss-never-split-the-difference]]). "Objective criteria" presumes a shared standard
  exists; often the fight *is* over whose standard counts. Read the two together for the full
  picture.
- **Practitioner counterpoint:** (fill via `research` → r/negotiation, r/salary) — the consensus:
  **BATNA is the part that actually wins** — most "negotiation" outcomes are decided before the
  conversation, by who has the better alternative.
