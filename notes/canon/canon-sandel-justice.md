# Note · Sandel — *Justice*

> Canon · Cluster: Ethics & Judgment · Maps to **Course 11** (Global Strategy & Responsible Business), **E4** (Governance & The Board)

- **Source:** Michael Sandel, *Justice: What's the Right Thing to Do?* (2009)
- **Core claim:** There are **three major approaches to justice**, each powerful and each with a blind
  spot — and the practical skill is **recognizing which framework you're implicitly using**, and when
  to shift.
- **Framework — the three lenses:**
  1. **Maximizing welfare (utilitarianism)** — the greatest good for the greatest number.
     *Blind spot:* can justify sacrificing the individual for aggregate benefit; reduces all values
     to one scale.
  2. **Respecting freedom (libertarianism / Kantian rights)** — individual rights and consent above
     collective outcomes. *Blind spot:* can tolerate vast inequality so long as it arose from free
     exchange.
  3. **Cultivating virtue (communitarianism / Aristotelian)** — what promotes human flourishing and
     the common good. *Blind spot:* whose definition of virtue prevails?
  - Most real arguments are people **talking past each other from different lenses** without naming
    them.
- **Applies when:** ESG, governance, responsible-business tradeoffs, fairness debates, policy — any
  decision where "the right thing" is contested and stakeholders argue from different moral premises.
- **Breaks when:** the survey **diagnoses without deciding** — it sharpens *which* framework you're
  using but **refuses to adjudicate** between them, so it can leave you articulate and still stuck (a
  sophisticated relativism). It's also **deliberative and slow** — useless in the fast,
  recognition-primed moment ([[canon-klein-sources-of-power]]); it's a tool for *set-piece* ethical
  reasoning. And utilitarian "maximize welfare" quietly underwrites a lot of business logic
  (cost-benefit, [[canon-mankiw-principles-of-economics]]) — worth seeing the lens you default to.
- **Practitioner counterpoint:** *(researched 2026-06-05 — r/Ethics corpus + Harvard "Justice" transcripts)*
  The corpus is a live demonstration of Sandel's thesis: the most-upvoted r/Ethics threads are
  **unacknowledged framework clashes**, not disagreements about facts. A 1,144↑ post —
  "**the debate around abortions shows how bad most people are at assessing and discussing ethical
  dilemmas**" — diagnoses exactly this: *"any debate around the topic is abysmal, with everyone just
  making oversimplified, politicized propaganda statements… almost no one seems to be able to admit
  that [it] is a very complex and difficult ethical dilemma."* A 399↑ thread —
  "**I hate the phrase 'There's no ethical consumption under capitalism'**" — is a rights-vs-welfare
  collision dressed as a slogan, with the OP arguing ethics is *"a spectrum of harm reduction"*
  against an absolutist "all-or-nothing" frame. **The usable move:** name which lens each party is
  arguing from (welfare / rights / virtue) before arguing the case. Badaracco's right-vs-right is the
  *personal-scale* version ([[canon-badaracco-defining-moments]]).
- **Researched layer** *(2026-06-05)*
  - **Canonical explanation (sourced):** Sandel's whole course turns on a single distinction that
    maps onto two of the three lenses. From the opening trolley lecture: *"consequentialist moral
    reasoning locates morality in the consequences of an act, in the state of the world that will
    result from the thing you do,"* whereas *"categorical moral reasoning locates morality in certain
    absolute moral requirements, in certain categorical duties and rights, regardless of the
    consequences"* (Harvard University, "The Moral Side of Murder"). The skill isn't picking one —
    it's noticing **which one you're using** when your intuition flips between cases.
  - **Real application (mined):** the trolley poll itself is the mechanism in miniature — the *same*
    person votes to divert the trolley (welfare: "**better that five should live even if one must
    die**") but refuses to push the fat man off the bridge (a categorical bar on using a person as a
    means). Sandel: *"people weren't so sure about consequentialist moral reasoning when people
    hesitated to push the fat man over the bridge."* The r/Ethics "eating dog vs. cow" thread (595↑)
    runs the same experiment on the reader — once culture is stripped out, people discover they were
    arguing from a virtue/tradition lens, not a welfare one.
  - **Where the corpus pushes back on the book:** Rawls' answer to the impasse — choose principles
    *"behind the veil of ignorance,"* where you'd reject utilitarianism because *"we will each want
    to be respected with dignity… even if we turn out to be a member of a minority"* (Harvard, "What's
    a Fair Start?"). But the practitioner corpus shows the limit Sandel himself flags: naming the
    lenses sharpens the argument without ending it. The abortion thread's despair —
    *"everyone is 100% sure that they are right"* — is the lived version of the note's "diagnoses
    without deciding" critique. For *operational* ethics the corpus prefers a decision model: a
    Global Ethics Solutions transcript splits dilemmas into *"situational"* (judgment/temptation) vs.
    *"not-so-situational,"* arguing the latter *"require a decision-making model that allows you to
    clarify the situation and offer constructive alternatives"* — structure Sandel deliberately
    withholds.
