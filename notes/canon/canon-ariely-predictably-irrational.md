# Note · Ariely — *Predictably Irrational*

> Canon · Cluster: Behavioral Design & Decision Science · Maps to **Course 07** (Data & Decision-Making), **Course 05** (Marketing & Brand)

- **Source:** Dan Ariely, *Predictably Irrational* (2008)
- **Core claim:** Our irrationality is **systematic and exploitable** — Ariely supplies experimental
  evidence for specific, repeatable distortions that pricing and product design routinely use.
- **Framework — the named effects:**
  - **Decoy effect** — adding a clearly inferior third option makes a target option look better
    (the *Economist* subscription classic).
  - **The power of FREE** — "free" isn't a price, it's an emotional trigger; people over-choose it.
  - **Anchoring in pricing** — the first number seen becomes the reference for everything after.
  - **Endowment effect** — people overvalue what they already own.
  - **Social norms vs. market norms** — introduce money into a social-norm relationship (favors,
    goodwill) and you *can't go back* (pay your mother-in-law for Thanksgiving dinner…).
- **Applies when:** setting prices, designing menus/tiers, or understanding why customers (and you)
  behave "irrationally" — and where to ethically use, or defend against, these levers.
- **Breaks when:** many lab effects have **shaky real-world / replication** standing — the *direction*
  is usually right but the *size* shrinks outside the lab; don't build a business on one cute effect.
  The social-vs-market-norms insight is the deepest and most durable; the decoy/free effects are
  tactical and **erode once customers wise up** (over-used decoys read as manipulation). Cousin of
  Kahneman's biases and Cialdini's levers, with weaker external validity than either claims
  ([[canon-kahneman-thinking-fast-and-slow]], [[canon-cialdini-influence]]).
- **Practitioner counterpoint:** (fill via `research` → r/marketing, r/PPC) — pricing folks use
  anchoring + decoys constantly; the durable strategic one is **social vs. market norms** — why
  paying people can *destroy* goodwill-driven behavior.
