# Note · Meadows — *Thinking in Systems*

> Canon · Cluster: Mental Models & Thinking · Maps to **Course 07** (Data & Decision-Making), **Course 06** (Operations)

- **Source:** Donella Meadows, *Thinking in Systems: A Primer* (2008)
- **Core claim:** A system is interconnected elements organized to achieve something. Most people
  intervene at the **wrong leverage point** — pushing on flows when the *structure* of the feedback
  loop is the real driver. Most org problems are **structural, not personal.**
- **Framework:**
  - **Stocks** (accumulations), **flows** (rates of change), **feedback loops** (reinforcing or
    balancing), **delays** (why systems oscillate and overshoot).
  - **Leverage points (least → most powerful):** parameters/numbers (weak) → buffer & stock sizes →
    feedback loop strength → **rules & incentive structures** → information flows → **goals** →
    **paradigm/purpose of the system** (strongest).
  - Key heuristic: **replace the person but keep the system → same behavior.**
- **Applies when:** a problem keeps recurring despite fixes — a sign you're tuning parameters when
  the loop structure or the system's *goal* is what needs changing.
- **Breaks when:** the high-leverage interventions (changing goals/paradigm) are exactly the
  hardest to execute and most resisted — the framework tells you *where* to push, not *how* to win
  the political fight to do it. Systems maps can also become elaborate procrastination ("paralysis
  by diagram"). And boundaries are chosen, not given — draw them wrong and the model misleads.
- **Practitioner counterpoint:** *(researched 2026-06-05 — r/systemsthinking corpus + Meadows/Senge transcripts)*
  The book's "structure over person" claim has real teeth in the field: an r/systemsthinking thread
  on belief and culture (83↑) argues *"why facts don't change minds… structure does,"* and the
  Senge summary in the corpus states the rule flatly — *"different people in the same structure tend
  to produce qualitatively similar results… while it's easier to find someone or something to blame,
  the problem usually lies in how the system was set up in the first place"* (Readitfor.me). **But the
  loudest practitioner pain point is execution, not insight:** the top tension thread, *"systems
  thinking mindset tensions"* (133↑), names the trap precisely — you become *"so aware the Event-level
  solutions don't last but Pattern-level and Structure-level solutions are much harder to achieve that
  you are caught between the urgent but ephemeral and the slow-burn but everlasting."* Knowing the
  high-leverage point doesn't buy you the political capital or patience to push on it.
- **Researched layer** *(2026-06-05)*
  - **Canonical explanation (sourced):** Meadows ranks leverage points *least → most* powerful, and
    the corpus walk-through of Ch. 6 confirms the punchline — the weak knobs are numbers/parameters,
    while *"she identifies paradigm shifts as the most important leverage point,"* above goals,
    self-organization, rules and information flows (Ashley Hodgson, "Thinking in Systems, Ch. 6:
    Leverage Points"). The deeper claim is structural: per the Senge summary, *"the structure of the
    system influences the behavior more often than not… different people in the same structure tend to
    produce qualitatively similar results"* (Readitfor.me) — i.e. replace the person, keep the loop,
    get the same outcome.
  - **Real application (mined):** the **beer game** is the corpus's cleanest demonstration of *delays*
    and feedback driving oscillation — a four-stage supply chain where *"orders move in the opposite
    direction… from retailer to the manufacturer,"* and because each tier can only see its own back
    orders, small demand wobbles amplify into the bullwhip swings as the structure, not any one
    player, that produces the chaos (Management Whizz / Niko J, "The Beer Game"). r/systemsthinking's
    *"examples of system thinking applied in real life?"* (172↑) reaches for the same everyday handle —
    *"Parenting is one of the oldest examples of systems thinking applied."*
  - **Where the corpus pushes back on the book:** practitioners flag two failure modes. First, the
    *urgent-vs-structural* trap — you see that event-level fixes don't last but structure-level fixes
    are slow and politically expensive, and freeze between them ("mindset tensions," 133↑). Second, the
    field attracts *grand-theory inflation*: a popular thread asks whether there's *"a
    disproportionately high number of kooky 'grand theories of everything' in this space"* (86↑) — the
    same "paralysis by diagram" the note warns about, where mapping substitutes for moving.

### Rep
Pick a recurring problem at work. Sketch the stock, the flows, and the feedback loop. Ask: am I
fixing a *number* when the *loop* (or the system's goal) is the cause?
