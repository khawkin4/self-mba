# Note · Greene — *The 48 Laws of Power*

> Canon · Cluster: Power & Organizational Politics · Maps to **Course 09** (Leadership & OB)

- **Source:** Robert Greene, *The 48 Laws of Power* (1998)
- **Core claim:** Distills historical case studies into blunt principles about **how power actually
  operates** — controversial precisely because it describes power as it *is*, not as we wish it were.
- **Framework — representative laws:**
  - Never outshine the master · Conceal your intentions · Always say less than necessary · Make other
    people come to you · Win through actions, never through argument · Get others to do the work but
    take the credit · Keep people dependent on you · Use absence to increase respect.
  - The throughline: power is relational, perception-driven, and often *indirect*.
  - **Best used as a diagnostic** — recognizing these moves being run *on you* — rather than a
    prescription for how to behave.
- **Applies when:** decoding manipulative or political behavior around you — naming the move strips
  its power. A defensive lens.
- **Breaks when:** taken as a **how-to, it's corrosive and self-defeating** — "conceal intentions,"
  "take the credit" build short-term advantage and long-term distrust that destroys the alliances
  power actually rests on (Pfeffer's *sustainable* power needs reputation,
  [[canon-pfeffer-power]]). It's amoral by construction and **survivorship-biased** (Greene picks
  history's winners and back-fits a law). In high-trust modern orgs it's frequently the *wrong*
  strategy — Carnegie's sincerity ([[canon-carnegie-win-friends]]) and Radical Candor
  ([[canon-scott-radical-candor]]) out-compound it.
- **Practitioner counterpoint:** (fill via `research` → r/managers, r/cscareerquestions) — common
  verdict: invaluable for *spotting* office politics, dangerous as a personal operating system —
  people who *live* by it get isolated.
