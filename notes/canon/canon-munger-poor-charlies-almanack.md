# Note · Munger — *Poor Charlie's Almanack*

> Canon · Cluster: Mental Models & Thinking · Maps to **Course 07** (Data & Decision-Making), **E11** (Personal OS)

- **Source:** Charlie Munger, *Poor Charlie's Almanack* (ed. Peter Kaufman, 2005)
- **Core claim:** You need a **latticework of mental models** from many disciplines — psychology,
  physics, biology, economics, math, engineering — applied *in combination*. No single model is
  sufficient; "to a man with a hammer, every problem is a nail."
- **Framework — the highest-yield models:**
  - **Incentives** — people respond to incentives, and you're usually wrong about what the *real*
    incentives are. ("Never ask a barber if you need a haircut.")
  - **Inversion** — don't ask how to succeed; ask how you'd *fail*, then avoid that.
  - **Second-order thinking** — *and then what?* — consequences past the obvious first one.
  - **Availability / psychology of misjudgment** — overweighting what's vivid and recent.
- **Applies when:** any consequential judgment — Munger's discipline is to run a problem through
  several disciplinary lenses before committing.
- **Breaks when:** the latticework rewards breadth but a *little* knowledge of many fields can breed
  overconfident dilettantism — models misapplied outside their domain mislead. Inversion and
  incentive-analysis are powerful but not *generative*: they tell you what to avoid, not what to
  build. And "psychology of misjudgment" is easy to see in others, hard in yourself
  ([[canon-kahneman-thinking-fast-and-slow]] gives the rigorous version).
- **Practitioner counterpoint:** (fill via `research` → r/SecurityAnalysis, investing threads) —
  investors note Munger's real edge was *temperament* (patience, avoiding stupidity) more than any
  single model — "it's not about being smart, it's about being consistently not-dumb."
