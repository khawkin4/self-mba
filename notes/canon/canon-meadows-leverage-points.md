# Note · Meadows — *Leverage Points*

> Canon · Cluster: Design Thinking & Problem-Solving · Maps to **Course 07** (Data & Decision-Making), **Course 06** (Operations)

- **Source:** Donella Meadows, *Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System* (essay, 1999;
  expanded from her systems work)
- **Core claim:** There are **twelve places to intervene in a system, ranked from least to most
  effective** — and **most interventions happen at the bottom** of the list (tweaking numbers), where
  they barely matter.
- **Framework — the 12 leverage points (least → most powerful):**
  12. Constants, parameters, numbers (taxes, standards) — *weakest.*
  11. Buffer sizes · 10. Stock-and-flow structure · 9. Lengths of delays · 8. Strength of balancing
  loops · 7. Gain of reinforcing loops · 6. Information flows (who has access) · 5. **Rules**
  (incentives, constraints) · 4. **Self-organization** (power to add/change structure) · 3.
  **Goals** of the system · 2. **Paradigm** — the shared assumptions the system arises from · 1. The
  **power to transcend paradigms.**
  - Big takeaway: **the highest-leverage interventions challenge the *goal* and the *paradigm***,
    which is why they're rare and usually come from **outsiders** — and why number-tweaking feels
    productive but rarely moves the system.
- **Applies when:** choosing *where* to intervene in any persistent systemic problem — policy, org
  design, a stubborn metric — and checking you're not just adjusting parameters.
- **Breaks when:** **leverage and *feasibility* run opposite** — the highest points (goals,
  paradigms) are the **hardest to move and most resisted**, so "aim high on the list" can mean **aim
  at the impossible** while the achievable parameter tweak goes undone. Meadows herself cautioned the
  ranking isn't mechanical — push the wrong high-leverage point in the wrong direction and you do
  *more* damage, faster. Pair with Rumelt's crux (importance × *solvability*,
  [[canon-rumelt-the-crux]]) to balance leverage against tractability.
- **Practitioner counterpoint:** *(researched 2026-06-06 — Ashley Hodgson, "Thinking in Systems, Ch. 6: Leverage Points in Systems")* A
  book-specific chapter walkthrough confirms the load-bearing claim that the *rules* and who controls
  them sit near the top of the ladder: *"if you want to understand the deepest malfunctions of systems
  pay attention to the rules and who has the power over them."* The same source sharpens Meadows'
  warning on the goals lever — the *stated* goal is often not the real one: a CEO is held accountable
  to maximize profits, but *"profits are a requirement to stay in the game… as long as you're making
  profit… you can have these other goals that sort of structure people's incentive inside the
  system."* Practitioner takeaway that survives the re-pull: when you reach for a number/parameter fix,
  **ask whether the *rule*, the *goal*, or the *paradigm* is the real lever** — and ask *who has power
  over the rules*, because that is where the deepest malfunctions hide. The foundation is her
  *Thinking in Systems* ([[canon-meadows-thinking-in-systems]]).

- **Researched layer** *(updated 2026-06-06)*
  - **Canonical explanation (sourced):** Hodgson walks the ladder top-down and names the apex: *"she
    identifies Paradigm shifts as the most important leverage point"* (Ashley Hodgson, "Thinking in
    Systems, Ch. 6: Leverage Points in Systems"). Her worked example is taxation — the *paradigm* is
    "what does the population view as being a fair tax"; that shared notion of fairness *"in people's
    heads as the way the world works… that is a really important thing driving the system."* The
    leverage *rises* as you climb from parameters (#12) through rules (#5), self-organization (#4),
    goals (#3) toward the paradigm (#2) and the power to transcend it (#1).
  - **Real application (mined):** The transcript gives Meadows' own playbook for actually moving a
    paradigm — not arguing harder but **placing change agents and ignoring resistance**: *"work with
    active change agents,"* *"put people with this new paradigm shift in key positions, influencer
    positions,"* and **"Target the vast middle of people who might be open to the Paradigm Shift
    rather than focusing energy on the few who resist it."** In the wild, r/consulting's *"Most women
    in consulting are gone by their 30s. Why?"* (1,439↑) is a textbook high-leverage diagnosis: the OP
    notes *"the system pushed her out right when she should be hitting her stride"* — i.e. the fix
    isn't a parameter (one more maternity-policy tweak) but the firm's **rules and paradigm** about
    what a partner-track career must look like.
  - **Where the corpus pushes back on the book:** Hodgson surfaces the caution the earlier thin pull
    missed — Meadows' *"transcending paradigms"* (#1) reads less as an action and more as *"a warning
    not to get too embedded in your own paradigms even if they're good because of course they can have
    unintended consequences,"* and she flags that *"having the whole population transcend paradigms is
    probably not something that's going to happen."* That tempers any "just aim at the top of the
    ladder" reading: the highest points are the least feasible (see **Breaks when** above). The
    r/consulting thread underlines the same gap from the field — naming the systemic lever is easy;
    the comments show how hard it is to actually shift a firm's entrenched paradigm.

### Rep
Take an intervention you're planning. Where does it sit on the 12 — a *parameter* (low) or a *rule /
goal / paradigm* (high)? If low, ask what higher-leverage point you're avoiding, and why.
