# Note · Shapiro & Varian — *Information Rules*

> Canon · Cluster: Network Effects & Platform Strategy · Maps to **Course 04** (Competitive Strategy), **E10** (Digital & AI Transformation)

- **Source:** Carl Shapiro & Hal Varian, *Information Rules* (1999)
- **Core claim:** **Information goods have high fixed costs and ~zero marginal costs** — fundamentally
  different economics that demand different strategy. Written in 1999, still the clearest framework
  for digital competition.
- **Framework:**
  - **Price by value, not cost** — with zero marginal cost, cost-plus pricing is meaningless.
  - **Versioning** — sell the same information at different quality/feature levels to segment by
    willingness-to-pay.
  - **Bundling** — aggregating information goods raises total willingness-to-pay (and is a moat).
  - **Lock-in & switching costs** — technology choices create path dependence; **measure the total
    switching cost** of your customers (and your own vendor lock-in). The clearest treatment of this
    anywhere; Helmer's "switching costs" power ([[canon-helmer-7-powers]]).
  - **Standards wars** — network effects decide *which* technology wins; **it's about adoption, not
    quality** (VHS over Betamax). Tactics: build alliances, pre-announce, ensure backward
    compatibility.
- **Applies when:** any software/data/media business, or any product where switching costs and
  standards determine who wins.
- **Breaks when:** the *principles* are timeless but some *tactics* predate the platform/SaaS/
  open-source era — subscription, freemium, ad-funded, and API-ecosystem models reshaped versioning
  and lock-in beyond the book's 1999 examples (and aggressive lock-in now invites
  regulatory/antitrust blowback). Strong network effects can also flip fast under multi-homing
  ([[canon-cusumano-business-of-platforms]]).
- **Practitioner counterpoint:** *(researched 2026-06-05 — NFX / Talks at Google / David Halliday platform-strategy transcripts; r/startups corpus thin)*
  Modern operators rename Shapiro-Varian's **lock-in** as **"embedding"** and treat it as one of only
  a handful of durable moats: NFX's *"What Are Network Effects?"* counts *"only four defensibilities
  left… scale… embedding… brand… [and] the network effect,"* with **embedding** illustrated exactly as
  the book's switching-cost chapter would — *"people are embedding their software into enterprise
  behaviors, it's very hard to rip them out, and so Oracle, you just charge more and more every year
  and there's not much you can do about it."* The book's **standards-war** lesson — adoption, not
  quality, decides — survives intact as the network-effect "tipping point": *"they start off low, but
  when they reach a tipping point… every new incremental person increases the rate of return to
  everyone else."* (The r/startups pull was off-topic — generic idea-validation and co-founder posts,
  no versioning/lock-in discussion — flagged for a targeted r/SaaS re-pull.)

- **Researched layer** *(2026-06-05)*
  - **Canonical explanation (sourced):** The book's lock-in/switching-cost engine shows up almost
    verbatim in practitioner language as **"embedding."** NFX frames it as a top-tier moat:
    *"embedding is used by companies like Oracle or Workday — people are embedding their software into
    enterprise behaviors, it's very hard to rip them out, and so Oracle, you just charge more and more
    every year and there's not much you can do about it"* (NFX, "What Are Network Effects?"). That is
    Shapiro-Varian's *"measure the total switching cost of your customers"* restated as a defensibility.
  - **Real application (mined):** The **standards-war / network-effects** mechanism — *which* technology
    wins is about adoption, not quality — appears as the "tipping point": *"they start off low, but when
    they reach a tipping point they start up and… every new incremental person increases the rate of
    return to everyone else"* (NFX). Andrew Chen frames the same endgame as building **the moat**: *"how
    do you ultimately build the defensibility in your product such that you can compete against new
    startups and new entrants"* (Talks at Google, *The Cold Start Problem*).
  - **Where the corpus pushes back on the book:** the book's *"price by value"* edge erodes once a
    two-sided platform stops subsidizing a side. Halliday's first-hand reversal — *"booking on Airbnb
    was fantastic… I had so many great trips early on and never had one since"* — is the
    practitioner reality-check: zero-marginal-cost pricing power gets competed away and re-captured by
    the *platform*, not the original buyer, as cross-side network effects and economies of scale
    consolidate. Lock-in also now *invites* the antitrust the book's 1999 era did not price in.
