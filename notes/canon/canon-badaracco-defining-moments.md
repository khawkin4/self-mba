# Note · Badaracco — *Defining Moments*

> Canon · Cluster: Ethics & Judgment · Maps to **Course 09** (Leadership & OB), **E11** (Personal Operating System)

- **Source:** Joseph Badaracco, *Defining Moments* (1997)
- **Core claim:** The **hardest ethical decisions aren't right vs. wrong** — they're **right vs.
  right**: competing values, competing loyalties, competing visions of who you want to be. These
  decisions **reveal and shape character simultaneously.**
- **Framework — the questions (note: identity, not analysis):**
  - **What do my deep feelings tell me?** (intuition as data, not noise)
  - **Which option passes the "newspaper test"** — could I defend this publicly, on the front page?
  - **Which option best expresses who I am and who I want to become?**
  - **Which will I be most proud of over time?**
  - Right-vs-right conflicts come in recognizable forms: truth vs. loyalty, individual vs. community,
    short-term vs. long-term, justice vs. mercy.
- **Applies when:** a genuine **values dilemma** — not "do the right thing vs. the profitable thing,"
  but two *defensible* goods that can't both be honored.
- **Breaks when:** the **identity/feelings framing can rationalize self-interest** — "which option
  expresses who I am" is dangerously close to "which option I already wanted," and the newspaper test
  rewards what's *defensible* over what's *right* (a skilled rationalizer passes it easily). It offers
  **little for power-asymmetric** situations where the "right vs. right" framing obscures a real
  victim. Pair with a principled lens (Sandel's three theories, [[canon-sandel-justice]]) so the
  decision isn't purely introspective. Horowitz's "what you do is who you are" is the cultural-scale
  echo ([[canon-horowitz-what-you-do-is-who-you-are]]).
- **Practitioner counterpoint:** *(researched 2026-06-06 — Badaracco lecture "Leadership & Ethics," youtube.com/watch?v=POyAnJFGzSg)*
  In his own words, Badaracco draws the line the note's core claim rests on: most scandals aren't
  interesting because the answer is obvious — *"the folks at Enron… did a lot of things that were
  wrong… it was clear what was right and what was wrong… for the vast majority of people there's very
  little value in talking about right and wrong."* The decisions that actually torment serious
  managers are the ones where *"their stomach is tightening… they're breathing a little faster… I call
  them right versus right problems."* His sharpest case is the *un*-told half of the Tylenol story:
  after J&J had decided to pull the product, the heads of the FDA and FBI asked James Burke to leave it
  on the shelves so copycats couldn't "be the tail that wags the dog" and so the US wouldn't "exceed
  the terrorists." Badaracco: Burke *"to this day still doesn't know what he would have done"* —
  *"an unambiguous conflict between right and right."* That is the book's wager in the author's voice,
  and it lands harder than any third-party gloss.

- **Researched layer** *(updated 2026-06-06)*
  - **Canonical explanation (sourced):** Badaracco's mechanism is that hard calls are *judgments of
    character a manager must make alone, without clean rules and with too little time.* In the
    audiobook preface he indicts the standard playbook — *"follow the law, serve the shareholders,
    consult the company credo… do the right thing"* — as *"abstract and very general… they float
    serenely above the messy, complicated, anxiety-laden political dilemmas that managers must
    sometimes resolve, typically with too little time and information"* (Badaracco, "Defining Moments"
    audiobook, youtube.com/watch?v=yjBbitgIgDI). His method follows directly: *"the most successful
    guidance involves asking questions, not giving answers"* — which is why the framework above is a
    set of identity questions, not a decision rule.
  - **Real application (mined):** Badaracco's recurring fieldwork example is the layoff-and-the-friend
    bind, *"told four different versions of this exact same story"* — a subordinate confides he's about
    to over-mortgage his family for a dream house, while you know his name is on the coming layoff list.
    As a corporate officer you owe *"a duty of confidentiality"*; as a friend you'd *"reach out and pull
    [him] back"*; if he's fishing, honesty says tell him. *"This is not right versus wrong, this is
    right versus right versus right."* The reddit corpus shows the same structure surviving outside
    business: r/Ethics's top dilemma thread on abortion (1,144↑) frames it as *"How do we wage
    individual freedom against the rights of another existence?"* — two defensible goods that can't both
    be honored, the poster's complaint being that everyone collapses it into right-vs-wrong.
  - **Where the corpus pushes back on the book:** the note's worry — that feelings-first framing can
    launder self-interest — shows up verbatim in training material: a Global Ethics Solutions video
    defines *"situational ethics"* as deciding *"based on what's right for you at the time of the
    crisis,"* i.e. exactly how rationalization operates. So the adjacent corpus prescribes *process and
    preparation* over pure introspection. And Sandel's *Justice* lectures in the same cluster (Rawls'
    difference principle, Kant on treating persons as ends) supply the **principled lens the note says
    to pair with** — evidence the right-vs-right method needs a theory of justice underneath it, not
    just identity.
