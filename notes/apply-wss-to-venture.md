# Apply rep: the Wall Street Skinny's capital-markets layer, run against two seed firms

> **What this is.** An Apply rep, like [[apply-stock-picking-nke]]. It takes the concepts distilled in
> [[wall-street-skinny-insights]] and tests each one against two real seed firms with very different shapes:
> **Primary Venture Partners** (scaled, specialist, operator-heavy; [[primary-venture-partners-insights]]) and
> **Harlem Capital** (emerging manager, mission-driven, lean; [[harlem-capital-vc-insights]]).
>
> **The question.** The Wall Street Skinny teaches banks, credit, rates and PE. Seed VC funds use no leverage and hold
> no bonds. Which of these forces still reach them, through what channel, and does the answer differ by firm shape?
>
> **Provenance.** Firm figures come from the two firm notes (transcripts plus press). Concepts come from the seven
> `wss-*` notes. **Every calculation below is this note's own**, built on the stated inputs, and labelled where an
> input is an assumption (fee rates, exit ownership). Neither firm has disclosed DPI, net TVPI, or fee terms in any
> source we hold.
>
> Maps to **Course 10** (Entrepreneurship & Venture), **Exec E2** (Capital Allocation), **Course 11** (Global Macro).

## Concept 1 · The distribution drought and DPI

**The WSS concept** ([[wss-private-markets-wealth]] §6): distributions as a share of NAV are at a post-GFC low. LPs
with no cash coming back can't recommit, so they rank managers on **DPI** (cash returned) rather than TVPI (marks).

**Through what channel it reaches seed VC:** the next fundraise. A seed fund's product is its *next* fund. When LPs
are cash-starved, they re-up with managers they know and cut first-time and small relationships.

**Primary.** Its public scorecard is a **markup metric**: Fund I's claimed 9 of 25 unicorns (36%). Named cash events
exist (Mirror ~$500M to Lululemon in 2020; an unnamed ~$800M sale at 20-something times invested capital in early 2026;
Alma's pending sale), but no DPI figure is on any tape. Primary raised its largest fund ($625M) *during* the drought.
That suggests its LPs ($100B+ institutions underwriting 15–20-year relationships) are paying for brand and access, and
accepting marks plus a few large exits as interim evidence.

**Harlem.** Harlem is the manager the drought hits by design. It is an emerging manager (Fund III would be its "third-fund
hump"), its LP base leans on individuals and family offices with less pacing discipline, and it raises into an
anti-DEI backlash on top. It has 12 exits on 80+ investments (PitchBook via press). If those exits are mostly small M&A,
they produce DPI but not headline marks, which is the right currency for this market but the wrong one for brand.

**With vs. without.** *With* a normal distribution cycle, both raise on schedule and the gap between them is size. *Without*
it, as now, the gap becomes survival: the scaled firm consolidates LP dollars and the emerging manager stretches its raise.

## Concept 2 · Rates set the LP's opportunity cost

**The WSS concept** ([[wss-credit-distressed]], [[wss-rates-macro]]): senior secured private credit yields roughly
9–11%, contractual, with quarterly cash from year one.

**This note's arithmetic.** Convert venture multiples to annual returns:

| Net multiple | Over 10 years | Over 12 years |
|---|---|---|
| 2.0x | 7.2% / yr | 5.9% / yr |
| 3.0x | 11.6% / yr | 9.6% / yr |
| 5.0x | 17.5% / yr | 14.4% / yr |

A **3x net fund held 12 years earns about the same annual return as private credit**. It takes on power-law dispersion,
a decade of illiquidity and zero cash for years to get it. At zero rates, 3x was a huge premium. At 5% base rates it
is roughly a wash.

**What follows:**
- LPs now underwrite only managers they believe can do **5x+**. That is why Primary pitches each partner book as
  "eight 5x funds" ([[primary-venture-partners-insights]] §2), and why its unicorn-rate bar (≥10% of seed bets) matters.
- Higher rates also lower startup valuations directly. Startups are the longest-duration equity there is, because all the
  cash flow is far out, so a higher discount rate hits them hardest. Growth-stage markdowns then flow down to seed as
  smaller, slower Series A rounds.

**With vs. without.** If rates fell back to near zero, credit would yield 5–6%, the venture premium would reopen, and
LP money would flow back into emerging managers first, since they are the higher-beta bet. Harlem gains most from that.

## Concept 3 · Fund size decides how much the exit window matters

**The WSS concept** ([[wss-capital-markets-ma]]): IPO pricing, float, lockups and acquirer affordability decide whether a
private mark becomes cash, and at what price.

**This note's arithmetic** (simplified: ignores fees, follow-ons and timing; assumes **~10% ownership at exit** after
dilution, as an illustrative input):

| | Exit value needed to return 1x | ...to return 3x gross |
|---|---|---|
| Primary Fund V ($625M) | **~$6.3B** aggregate | **~$19B** |
| Primary, one partner book (~$60M) | ~$0.6B | ~$1.8B (5x = $3B) |
| Harlem Fund III ($150M) | **~$1.5B** aggregate | **~$4.5B** |

**The insight.** Harlem can return a fund through a handful of **$300M–$1B strategic acquisitions**, the M&A market that
stays open in most cycles. Primary's fund-level math needs several **multi-billion outcomes**, which in practice means
IPOs or mega-acquisitions. Its partner-book framing brings each book back to solo-GP scale, but the fund still needs a few
companies to clear the IPO window. Svrluga says exactly this: the OpenAI, Anthropic and SpaceX IPOs will cool the
market if they trade poorly.

**Acquirer affordability applies too.** The Netflix/WBD lesson (a buyer can value a target highly and still walk because
the deal dilutes earnings) caps strategic bids for venture-backed companies when acquirers' P/Es fall. Harlem's
M&A-dependent path is exposed to *that*. Primary's IPO-dependent path is exposed to the IPO window.

## Concept 4 · Secondaries vs. Select: two answers to "what do we do with our winners?"

**The WSS concept** ([[wss-private-markets-wealth]] §5): LP-led secondaries and GP-led continuation funds manufacture
liquidity without an exit.

**Primary Select is the opposite tool.** It puts **new money into** the firm's winners at later rounds. A continuation
fund takes **money out** of them for existing LPs. Both solve the same problem: a seed fund's ownership in a breakout
gets diluted, and the GP wants to keep exposure. Select chooses concentration; a secondary sale chooses liquidity.

**Market size, from the channel** ([[wss-private-markets-wealth]] §5): $160bn of secondaries in 2024, ~$90bn LP-led and ~$70bn GP-led. Venture secondary discounts are not covered on the channel (n/d).

**The trade-off in numbers (illustrative).** A seed fund holds a stake marked at $100M. Selling 20% of it in a secondary
at a 30% discount to the last mark brings in **$14M of cash**: real DPI, delivered now. It gives up the upside on $20M of
marked value and books a $6M markdown against the mark. Under the drought, many LPs prefer the $14M.

**By firm:**
- **Primary** is the firm that most needs a liquidity answer, because Fund I is ~11.7 years old (past a standard 10-year
  term) and holds unicorn marks. The tapes never discuss secondaries (n/d). This is the most important open question in
  the comparison.
- **Harlem** uses SPVs for follow-ons, which pushes the concentration decision to separate investors and keeps the main
  fund's reserves smaller.

**Agency flag** ([[canon-jensen-meckling-agency-theory]]). When Select joins a round in a company Fund IV holds, that
round
sets Fund IV's mark. This is harmless when an outside lead prices the round. It becomes an LP-vs-LP conflict if Select
leads insider rounds. How Select's pricing is governed is not disclosed. The same structural tension shows up in
GP-led continuation funds, where the GP sits on both sides.

## Concept 5 · The holding period belongs to whoever funds you

**The WSS concept** ([[wss-trading-hedge-funds]]): redemptions, margin calls and stop-losses can end a correct trade
early. Situational Awareness was reportedly up ~80% when its book was sold.

**The venture version is slower but the same mechanism.** A fund has a contractual life of about 10 years plus 1–2
extensions. LP patience, not the company's timeline, sets the deadline. A winner still compounding in year 12 forces
the GP to choose among an extension vote, a secondary sale, or distributing illiquid shares.

- **Primary Fund I** (2015) is there now. Its ~$800M exit and the Alma sale arriving in 2026 fit a fund working through
  its tail.
- **Harlem Fund I** (2018) reaches the same point around 2028–29.
- **What protects a firm:** brand and multi-fund LP relationships buy extensions. A firm raising its next fund from the
  same LPs gets more patience on the old one. That is another way scale compounds.

## Concept 6 · AI economics changes what seed money buys

**The WSS concept** ([[wss-ai-economics]]): the AI build-out is descending a funding ladder (cash flow → bonds →
off-balance-sheet vehicles → vendor guarantees → chip-backed debt), and the binding constraint is power and grid
access, not capital. Application-layer software faces the "SaaS-pocalypse."

**Primary sits directly on the ladder.** It has an infrastructure and compute partner, semis exposure, and the ASIC
company Svrluga calls its most valuable (which matches Etched; see [[primary-venture-partners-insights]] §9). Those
outcomes depend on the same AI capex cycle the WSS notes describe:
- **If hyperscaler capex keeps rising,** chip startups get bought or listed at large values.
- **If the funding ladder breaks** (vendor-financing unwind, depreciation reset), the most valuable marks in the
  portfolio are the most cyclical.

Svrluga's own read fits this: higher loss rates, bigger outliers.

**The SaaS-pocalypse cuts the other way.** Primary's B2B, go-to-market and vertical-software books face falling seat-based
pricing. The specialist model is the hedge: partners in vertical AI and go-to-market should see the repricing early.

**Harlem.** The firm notes we hold don't break down Harlem's portfolio by sector, so its AI exposure is n/d. Structurally,
a $150M generalist fund can't follow compute companies into $50M+ rounds, so its AI exposure is likely to be
application-layer, which is more exposed to the SaaS-pocalypse and less exposed to the capex cycle.

## Concept 7 · The wealth channel: competition or a new LP pool?

**The WSS concept** ([[wss-private-markets-wealth]]): managers are pushing evergreen and semi-liquid funds through private
banks and advisor platforms to reach individuals, because institutions are tapped out.

- **Harlem** already raises from individuals and family offices. The big-platform push brings well-packaged products
  (quarterly liquidity sleeves, platform due diligence) to compete for that money. The opportunity is the reverse:
  platforms such as CAIS, and private-bank alternatives desks, need differentiated venture exposure to fill shelves.
- **Primary's** LP base is institutional, so the channel matters less. The one point of contact: if evergreen vehicles
  become a major buyer of venture *secondaries*, Primary's Fund I liquidity problem (Concept 4) gets a new buyer.

## Concept 8 · A cap table is a capital structure

**The WSS concept** ([[wss-credit-distressed]]): who gets paid first decides recovery; liability-management fights are
about jumping the queue.

**The venture translation** (liquidation preferences are taught in [[wss-private-markets-wealth]] §14b). Preferred stock rounds stack like debt tranches. Later rounds commonly carry liquidation
preferences senior to (or pari passu with) earlier ones, and a 1x non-participating preference returns cost before common.
In a sale below the last post-money, the seed preferred is effectively the **junior tranche**.

**Example (illustrative):** $60M raised in total, sold for $50M:
- Series B ($40M, senior) takes its $40M first.
- Series A ($15M) takes $10M.
- Seed ($5M) takes **zero**, despite having "owned 15%."

That's the cap-table version of a first-lien recovery falling while the junior tranche is wiped out.

- **Primary's Select creates an internal stack.** When Select buys Series A preferred in a company where Fund IV holds
  seed preferred, the firm's own vehicles sit at different ranks. In a downside sale they are on opposite sides of the
  waterfall.
- **Harlem's SPV investors** sit on the same internal-stack logic.
- **The venture version of an LME** is a **pay-to-play or down-round recap**: insiders who put up new money convert to a
  senior class, and non-participants get crammed down. The Caesars playbook in miniature.

## Concept 9 · Opposite payoff shapes, same economy

**The WSS concept**, read in reverse. Credit's upside is capped and its downside total, so it profits by **avoiding
losers**: covenants, collateral, recovery. Venture's downside is capped at 1x and its upside unbounded, so it profits by **not
missing winners**: ownership, reserves, access.

| Same shock | Credit response | Seed VC response |
|---|---|---|
| Rates up | Higher coupons; tighter terms | Lower valuations; LP money diverted to credit |
| Recession | Defaults, workouts, distressed buying | Lower entry prices; historically strong vintages |
| Liquidity drought | Gating in evergreen credit funds | DPI pressure; secondaries; extensions |

**The strategic read** ([[canon-taleb-antifragile]], [[canon-mauboussin-success-equation]]): the investors who profit
from a downturn are the ones still writing checks during it. That depends on having fresh capital at the bottom, which
is a fundraising question. So the firm with deep LP relationships (Primary) is structurally positioned to invest through
the bottom. The firm raising into it (Harlem) may be forced to slow down exactly when entry prices are best. **Fund
timing is the emerging manager's hidden risk.**

## The fee lever (the sharpest contrast, quantified)

[[primary-venture-partners-insights]] names this as the defining difference: both firms treat the management fee as
the strategic variable and spend it in opposite ways. **Illustrative arithmetic at an assumed 2% fee** (actual terms
undisclosed):

| | Annual fee pool | What it funds |
|---|---|---|
| Primary Fund V ($625M) | ~$12.5M/yr, plus tail fees from Fund IV and Select still running | 8 partners + 60–80 operators (recruiting, BDRs, strategic finance, incubation) |
| Harlem Fund III ($150M target) | ~$3.0M/yr | A small partner team, a media engine, an intern program |

Even with overlapping fees from earlier funds, ~$12.5M across ~80 people is thin, which implies Primary's model needs
**continuously larger funds** to support the platform. That is the risk named in its note: fee dependence. Harlem's
risk is the reverse: capacity. A $3M budget cannot staff coverage as the fund grows.

**With vs. without the platform.** Primary's claim is that operators raise the seed-to-A graduation rate (>90%, self-reported)
and the win rate enough to beat the drag of a larger fund. Without the platform, Primary is a large seed fund with a
harder return-the-fund problem (Concept 3). With it, the question is whether graduation rate turns into DPI, and no
disclosed number yet answers that.

## Verdict: where the WSS layer applies to seed VC

| Concept | Primary | Harlem | Channel |
|---|---|---|---|
| Distribution drought / DPI | Medium (brand buys patience) | **High** (emerging manager, Fund III) | Next fundraise |
| Rates as LP opportunity cost | Medium | **High** | LP allocation |
| Exit-window dependence | **High** (needs multi-$B exits) | Medium (M&A-sized exits work) | Exit value |
| Secondaries / liquidity | **High** (Fund I at ~11.7 yrs) | Medium (~8 yrs) | DPI |
| Holding period | **High** | Medium, rising | Fund life |
| AI funding ladder | **High** (compute and semis marks) | Low–medium (likely app layer; n/d) | Mark volatility |
| Wealth channel | Low | Medium (competition and opportunity) | LP base |
| Cap-table seniority | Medium (Select internal stack) | Medium (SPV stack) | Downside recovery |
| Counter-cyclical capacity | **Advantaged** | **Disadvantaged** | Fund timing |

**One sentence.** The capital-markets layer reaches seed VC through its **LPs and its exits**, not its portfolio's balance
sheets. Scale shifts the exposure from fundraising risk (Harlem) to exit-window and liquidity risk (Primary).

## Retrieval questions

1. *Why does a 5% base rate hurt emerging seed managers more than scaled ones?* It shrinks the premium of a 3x fund over
   private credit to roughly zero, so LPs keep only managers they believe can do 5x+, and they judge that from brand
   and history, which emerging managers lack.
2. *Why does Primary's fund size make it more dependent on the IPO window than Harlem?* At ~10% exit ownership, $625M
   needs ~$6.3B of exit value just to return capital. Harlem's $150M needs ~$1.5B, reachable through strategic M&A.
3. *How is Primary Select the opposite of a GP-led continuation fund?* Select adds new primary capital to winners;
   a continuation vehicle moves existing assets so old LPs can exit. One concentrates, the other provides liquidity.
4. *A seed fund "owns 15%" and gets zero in a $50M sale. How?* Liquidation preferences on later rounds sit senior.
   Below the last post-money, seed preferred is the junior tranche.
5. *What is the venture version of a liability-management exercise?* A pay-to-play or down-round recap, where
   participating insiders take senior paper and non-participants get crammed down.
6. *Why might the best time to invest be the worst time for Harlem?* Entry prices are lowest in a downturn, but that is
   when an emerging manager struggles to raise fresh capital. Fund timing is its hidden risk.
7. *What does Fund I's age tell you about Primary's next two years?* At ~11.7 years it is past term. Expect
   extensions, secondary sales or distributions in kind, and the 2026 exits fit a fund working through its tail.
8. *Which WSS concept most threatens Primary's most valuable marks?* The AI funding ladder ([[wss-ai-economics]]). Its
   compute and semis winners are worth most if hyperscaler capex keeps climbing, and are the most cyclical if it doesn't.
