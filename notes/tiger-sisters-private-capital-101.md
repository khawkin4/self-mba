## Private Equity — buying control, fixing, flipping

- **Three defining traits**: (1) **private ownership** — PE invests in companies *off* the public market; (2) **control** — they buy a **majority stake** (the whole company or most of it), not a few shares like a retail Robinhood buyer; (3) a **turnaround mindset** — "buy the company, increase the value, and sell it for a profit." Mental model: **flipping a house** — see the potential, renovate, flip for more.
- **PE vs VC**: PE targets **larger, more developed companies that are "fixer uppers"** — bloated, inefficient, but turnaround-able. VC takes "the big bets, the earlier companies that are much riskier." Familiar PE-touched brands: Burger King, Neiman Marcus, Dollar General, Dunkin' Donuts, Dell.
- **How PE firms make money — "2 and 20"**:
  - **Management fee ≈ 2%** of the fund, annually, just to manage it. On a **$1B fund that's $20M/year** regardless of performance.
  - **Carried interest ≈ 20%** of the profits on successful deals — "that's where the magic happens." Carry is the real upside.
- **Where the fund's money comes from (three sources)**:
  - **LPs (limited partners)** — the investors: endowments, **pensions**, high-net-worth individuals, other funds.
  - **The PE firm itself** — a slice of its own money so it has **"skin in the game"**; LPs *want* to see this so the managers (who collect the 2%) are also exposed to downside.
  - **Debt** — which is the engine of the LBO.

### LBO mechanics (leverage = doing more with less of your own money)
- A **leveraged buyout (LBO)**: the PE firm **borrows money to buy the company**, then uses the *acquired company's own profits* to pay down that debt. House analogy: fix up the house, rent it out, use the **rent to pay the mortgage**.
- **Why leverage is powerful (the math)**: buy a **$1M house with $100K of your own cash + $900K of debt**; improve it and make $100K → you've already earned **~2x on the cash you actually put in.** Small equity, big swing.
- **When it burns the house down — Toys R Us**: a **$6.6B LBO in 2005**, of which **$5.2B was debt.** The company couldn't be turned around; it spent its cash **"servicing its debt"** instead of investing to compete with the rising Amazon. **Bankrupt by 2017; ~30,000+ jobs lost.** Debt is the lever that can multiply returns *or* "really burn down the house."

### Why PE gets dragged — the bad-rep levers
- **Layoffs / restructuring** — the strongest, fastest lever to cut costs and boost profit; hits real employees.
- **Short-term over long-term** — squeezing profits "artificially," risking the company's long-run health.
- **Asset stripping** — selling what the company owns (e.g., expensive manufacturing machines) for a quick cash/profit bump, often at the cost of the company's future.
- **Industry sensitivity** — when PE owns **hospitals, healthcare, education**, the mandate to "return money to the shareholder" collides with other stakeholders → big **headline risk** ("the evil company that laid off 30% of hospital workers").
- **The dirty-secret exit**: the most common PE exit is **selling to *another, bigger* PE firm.** Grow the company to a target size/profitability, then pass it up the chain — "most of the exits of these PE firms is selling to another PE firm that takes on the company when it has grown to a certain size."
- **But not all PE is bad** — **Blackstone bought Hilton**, improved and expanded it globally, took it public again, and made **~$14B without mass layoffs.** Outcome depends on the firm's size, industry, and style.
- **If your employer/brand gets acquired**: expect **efficiency moves** (cost cuts, tighter budgets), **new outside leadership** (PE firms install operators they trust to execute their plan), and **restructuring/layoffs.** As a customer: possibly higher prices, leaner offerings, worse quality (the hosts' "buy the vintage Zimmermann now" instinct) — though sometimes genuinely *better* operations, since "cutting the bloat" is what PE claims to be good at.
- **Verdict**: PE as a finance mechanism "isn't inherently evil." The discipline is to **follow the money to the incentives** — know what the firm is optimizing for, especially when it touches your salad chain, hospital, or favorite brand.

## Venture Capital — buying equity in big bets, governed by the power law

- **What it is**: **high-risk, high-reward equity investing.** VCs **give** money to startups (mostly early-stage) **in exchange for ownership** — "they're not loaning the money," they're **buying equity.** If the startup takes off, they win big; if it fails, they lose it all.
- **Stages & check sizes** (stage ≈ company maturity + raise size):
  - **Pre-seed** — just an idea, often **no revenue, no traction, no product-market fit.**
  - **Seed** — early. (Example: **Airbnb's 2009 seed ≈ $600K**; IPO'd 11 years later in 2020 around a **~$100B** valuation.)
  - **Series A ≈ $3M–$15M**; **Series B / C and beyond** — larger checks, growth mode.
  - **Growth equity** — the most mature bucket (usually after Series E): predictable revenues, more "stable," lower risk.
- **Why VCs specialize**: by **stage** (you evaluate a Series A very differently from a Series E) and/or by **industry/vertical** (e.g., healthcare, **B2B SaaS** — business-to-business software-as-a-service, like Salesforce; loved by VCs because it's "super scalable"). Later stages tend to demand deeper industry expertise. *(AI used to be a vertical; now it's a horizontal — "every single company is using AI now.")*
- **Tools to look this up**: **CrunchBase** and **PitchBook** — for valuations, last round, and investor lists.

### How VCs evaluate startups — Team, Market, Traction
- **Team (often the most important, especially early)** — with no revenue to analyze, you "bet on the team" and their track record. Pedigree **signals** training (an ex-Meta/Google engineer "sets a higher bar"). Red flags: **no technical co-founder** (you'll burn money hiring instead of building); **people risk** — most later-stage failures are "people problems," and a **married/dating founder pair** is a risk if the relationship breaks (counter-example: **Canva**, founded by a couple → massive success).
- **Market — the TAM trap**: VCs need a **billion-dollar-plus outcome**, which requires a large **TAM (total addressable market).** But TAM is "so overused it's almost a joke" — a non-defensible "$10 trillion, we solve problems for all humans" TAM **works against** the founder. What actually matters: of that TAM, **how many people realistically *convert* into paying customers.**
- **Traction** — "is your product working?" Evidence of **product-market fit**: downloads, month-over-month revenue, people actually paying.

### Why VC is a power game (signaling)
- VC "isn't just about money — it's about **signaling, social proof, access, and warm intros.**" Reputation as "clubby" — "who you know" over pure merit.
- **Brand-name signal cascade**: landing a **Sequoia / a16z / Lightspeed** check is a strong signal that makes **all the other funds pile in.** A strong **cap table** (capitalization table — the list of who owns the company) is itself a signal that smart money "sees something."
- **Accelerators as signal machines**: getting into **Y Combinator** is itself a signal of belief; **Demo Day** exposes founders to every major VC at once.
- **Investor fit > the check** (Shark Tank intuition): you pick an investor for the *specific expertise* they bring, not just the money. Founders **back-channel** to vet investors — YC even has an internal "Yelp/burn book" where founders warn each other (e.g., the investor who ran five meetings, issued a term sheet, then ghosted).

### The dark side — structural misalignment + the power law
- **The power-law portfolio**: a VC fund holds **dozens-to-hundreds of startups** and *expects* the **vast majority to go to zero** ("a nothing burger"), banking on a tiny few to return **100x–1,000x** (the unicorns). The math *requires* outliers.
- **The structural misalignment** (the load-bearing insight): because the fund needs **100x outcomes**, a **3x or 5x company — life-changing for the founder — is "a loser," "an egg," a zero to the fund.** So VCs push founders toward **swing-for-the-fences moves with a small chance of a moonshot**, even when steadier growth would serve the founder better. *"They are trying to optimize for their bottom line, which might not be the same decision that you would want to make for your own company as a founder."* Even good-faith VC advice is filtered through their incentive, not the founder's.
- **The pressure**: VC money "is not free." It carries the expectation of **explosive growth** → the "grow at all costs / spend now, figure out the business later" mentality (WeWork, Quibi). Some investors are "on the ass of the founder" with bi-weekly/monthly updates.
- **Practitioner advice**: HBS/GSB founder retrospectives repeatedly cite **taking funding too early — or at all — as a top mistake.** Once you take it, "you have an overlord." Choose investors as people (handsoff "smart capital" vs. heavy-touch).
- **Borrowable VC frameworks for life**: **portfolio approach** (don't bet everything on one outcome), **conviction over consensus** (contrarian bets), and **optionality** (VCs bake pro-rata rights into contracts; you keep your own options open — job searches, school apps with reach/target/safety tiers).

## Family Offices — patient capital with aligned incentives

- **What it is**: a **dedicated team managing all aspects of an ultra-high-net-worth family's financial life** — investment management, **tax planning, estate planning, philanthropy, legacy** — explicitly aiming at **"peace of mind" and overall well-being**, not just accumulating, growing, or even preserving wealth. **Ultra-high-net-worth** here ≈ **$100M average client**; Bessemer Trust runs **~$200B AUM**. (~two-thirds of clients are **first-generation wealth creators.**)
- **The fiduciary edge — aligned incentives**: Bessemer is **privately owned and in one line of business**, so "we really **sit on the same side of the table** as our clients... We're not selling them things or pushing products." Contrast: a **public** money manager's "ultimate responsibility is to the shareholder," and many push in-house funds they earn a **basis point** on — a built-in conflict with true **fiduciary duty.** Right alignment → "you can then think really clearly, how can I best help my client?" *(This is the agency problem made concrete — see [[canon-jensen-meckling-agency-theory]].)*
- **Start with the *why*, not the assets**: "What is the **purpose** of the wealth?" Preserve it? Grow it for **philanthropy**? Pass it to the **next generation** (and which **values** with it)? The purpose **drives** structure, tax strategy, and asset allocation. Most common answer: **preservation** ("we worked very hard") — but pure preservation fails because **inflation erodes wealth**, so even a preserve-only goal needs a growth strategy to keep **purchasing power.**
- **Time horizon → illiquid alternatives**: because UHNW families **don't need to spend the money soon** and have a **much longer time horizon** (and certainty around liquidity), they can hold **illiquid alternatives — private equity, venture capital** — "where you're not expecting to get your money back for 5 to 10+ years." These are typically **closed to retail investors** (high minimums) and unsuitable for people early in their careers with uncertain liquidity needs. Bessemer has worked to give even **small accounts (e.g., a grandchild's)** access to top-manager **private-market** exposure.
- **The "family office of one" (DIY translation)**: most of the principles scale down. Build a **mini family office** for yourself (or with a partner): hold a **regular dialogue about goals and the "why"**; **automate** allocation of each paycheck into spend / save / give buckets (treat **saving as a bill you pay yourself**); set a **recurring calendar reminder** (yearly/quarterly) to review. Pairs with a higher-yield home for cash so it's "working," not idle. (Built out as a beginner lesson in [[unit-10-personal-finance-operating-system]].)
- **Soft-skill power moves (from Holly)**: **listening well and asking good questions** is how you accumulate information ("information is power") — and you can **disagree by asking a question** ("did you think about X?") rather than calling someone out, which keeps you aligned and surfaces better ideas. **Inclusive culture isn't fuzzy** — the best idea often comes from the newest person, so psychological safety is an *idea-generation* engine.
