The sibling note covers the allocator's seat through an **emerging-manager mission fund**: Harlem started as a $25k

## 1 · Why "scaled seed": the strategy and its origin

- **Positioning as a Goldilocks middle.** Svrluga frames founders' choice as a boutique seed firm (2–3 generalist
  partners, thin resources) versus a multi-stage platform that will promise resources but has misaligned incentives on
  a $5M round out of a $5B fund. A seed check is option value to a mega-fund, which may back competitors and index
  across a category. Primary claims the middle: stage-dedicated, but staffed like a platform. He calls it the
  "preeminent institutional-grade seed firm."
- **The barbell he is arguing against.** At the 2025 Summit he cited the consensus view that venture is splitting into
  mega-platforms and niche players, with the middle as a death zone. His figures: ~58% of all VC dollars raised the prior
  year went to nine firms, and first-time funds fell ~60%. In 2022 he invoked the JCPenney analogy (stuck between
  boutiques and Walmart) for undifferentiated mid-size firms. Primary's answer is to be a *specialist* platform, not a
  scaled generalist.
- **Seed as a big enough market** (Sun's "billion-dollar seed fund" manifesto, written ~2019–20). US seed went from
  ~$3B to ~$15B a year over ten years (his figures; the Harlem note quotes $5B to $20B). The arithmetic: put ~60% of $1B
  into seed over 3 years and you write ~$200M of seed checks a year, which is ~1.3% of the market. At $280M (Fund IV) it
  was ~$60M a year, under 1%. His conclusion is that "too big for seed" is a myth when measured against the market.
- **Only ~150 seed deals a year matter.** Of ~5,000 US seed deals, ~3% historically become unicorns, so ~150 drive
  returns. Thousands of firms now compete for them in an efficient market, where a founder who changes a LinkedIn title
  to "stealth" gets dozens of inbound messages. Scale, in this view, is how you win your share of those 150.
- **Origin: NYC as the first wedge.** In 2013–14 the biggest NYC tech exit was Tumblr ($1B). Sun's thesis was that NYC
  had always attracted talent, but that talent was not working on startups until the financial crisis pushed ~200K
  finance workers out. A Midas-list VC told him a NYC-only seed fund was "insane." Primary invested almost only in NYC
  for 5–6 years. Svrluga: "we defined a battle we could win." Narrowing made the story credible to LPs and made the
  operating model possible: every portfolio company was within 20–30 minutes of the office.
- **Earning the right to stretch.** The geographic alpha disappeared around 2019–20. Sun says they stopped seeing and
  winning every NYC deal they wanted, because markets are more efficient than people think. They moved from a
  geographic edge to a structural one (specialists plus platform). By ~2024 most deals in the current fund were outside
  NYC: Bay Area, Chicago, Boston, Israel.
- **Svrluga's general lesson:** pick what you want to be known for and say no to whole customer segments. "Give
  yourself the advice you would give a portfolio company."

**Applies when** the seed market is large and growing, and founders value stage-dedicated help over brand-name multi-stage
optionality. **Breaks when** seed volume shrinks (a post-bubble contraction) or multi-stage firms credibly commit
resources at seed, which collapses the middle.

**Worked math, as Svrluga gave it:**
- **2020, $100M fund (Fund II).** Take $15–20M off the top for fees and costs, leaving ~$80–90M invested. If a third
  goes to zero and a third returns ~2x, the remaining third (~$30M) must produce ~$270–300M by itself. So winners need
  roughly **10x**. The house rule: no deal unless it can return the whole fund on its own. At 10–15% ownership over time,
  that means a **$700M to $1.5B** outcome.
- **2022, $150M fund (Fund III).** First checks ran **$0.5M to $4–5M, sweet spot $3–3.5M**. The bar fell to "can return
  half the fund in one shot." That means $75–80M of proceeds, so a $250M exit would need 30% ownership, which no founder
  will sell. Target markets are therefore $2B+, and bigger still for capital-intensive businesses, because the next
  investor writing a $50M check may need a $10B market. Ownership target: **15–20% at entry**. Pricing: underwrite a **3x
  step-up** to the next round (pay $20M post, expect $60M+ pre).
- **Why syndicates broke down.** Splitting a $4M round three ways leaves a $1.3M check. Even a 20x on that returns $26M,
  which is not enough for a $155M fund. Every growing firm needs bigger checks, so the old Primary/First Round/Lerer
  three-way co-investing ended.
- **Sun's per-partner reframe.** The Floodgate-era rule ("each deal can return the fund") assumed 2 partners on a
  $30–40M fund. With 8 check-writers, each partner controls ~12% of the fund. The right test is whether each partner's
  ~$50M book can return 5x+. His figures: $280M ÷ 6 ≈ $47M; ~$450M ÷ 8 ≈ $52–53M. That is roughly a solo GP's fund, but
  with ~10 staff behind each partner. He pitches it to LPs as "eight 5x funds."
- **Sun's unicorn-rate rule.** At current prices you need about **10% of seed bets to become unicorns** for a 3x+ net
  fund. Decacorns are only ~5% of ~1,500 private unicorns (~70–80), so you cannot count on one. Check: Fund I's claimed
  9/25 is **36%** (Svrluga said "38% or 40%"), more than three times that bar. Sun, in 2025, said "half a dozen" of 25;
  on Harlem's channel he said 8 unicorns from ~35 deals across Funds I–II.
- **Shuman's incubation lever on the same math.** At 10–15% ownership you need a unicorn rate above 10% to return the
  fund. Incubation buys higher ownership, which he says lowers the bar to about **7.5%**, or lets a $1B exit do the
  work of a $2B one. That is the financial case for the ~30% of deals that are homegrown.
- **Svrluga's scaling warning: AUM per partner, not AUM.** When AUM per partner grows faster than average round size at
  your stage, returns fall. Partners drift later, become less price-sensitive, and hunt for places to put $15M to work.
  Adding check-writers without a way to keep decision quality up also degrades returns. Primary's answer is to hold
  capital per partner roughly flat (about $50–70M, adjusted for bigger seed rounds), add specialist partners, and let
  the platform lift each partner's hit rate.

**Applies when** ownership stays at 10–20% and each partner's book is roughly solo-GP sized. **Breaks when** round sizes
inflate faster than books ("outrageous $50M seeds"), which forces either lower ownership or drift into later rounds, or
when headline fund growth outruns the number of credible check-writers.

## 3 · Primary Select: an opportunity fund, not a continuation vehicle

- **What it is.** A separate pool ($150M in 2022 per press; "$225M" alongside Fund V per Svrluga) that writes larger
  follow-on checks into **existing portfolio companies only**. Sun: no new names. Press says follow-ons run up to Series A;
  Svrluga in 2026 described "later rounds of our best performing companies."
- **What it is *not*.** A GP-led continuation vehicle (see [[wss-private-markets-wealth]] §5) moves assets the GP
  already owns out of an old fund into a new vehicle. A secondary buyer prices them, and existing LPs can cash out. Its
  purpose is liquidity and extending the hold. Select does the reverse: it puts **new primary capital into new financing
  rounds** of companies the seed fund still holds. No asset changes hands between Primary vehicles, and no LP gets
  liquidity. It is a reserves strategy carved out into its own vehicle.
- **Sun's case for it:** two products with different risk/return profiles. The core seed fund is the "high beta product":
  high conviction, high loss rate, multiple-driven. Select concentrates capital behind breakouts. It gets lower multiples
  but better IRR. Its claimed edge is **access** (pro-rata rights, board seats, founder relationships) plus **insight**
  (inside information on which companies the market is underpricing).
- **The reserves lesson behind it** (Sun, "How to Start a VC Fund"). Early on they copied the conventional **1:1
  reserves** ratio without asking why. They then watched follow-on rounds get crowded and repriced, and concluded they
  should have bought more at entry, at roughly a quarter of the price. They changed course mid-Fund I. Splitting out
  Select lets the seed fund spend more on initial ownership while the follow-on decision gets its own capital and its own
  LP underwriting.
- **Inference (mine, not on tape):** the obvious conflict to watch is cross-fund pricing. Fund A's portfolio is marked by
  the round that Fund B (Select) helps fill. That is benign when an outside lead sets the price and Select only takes
  pro-rata. It is a real agency issue if Select leads insider rounds. The transcripts do not say how Select's pricing is
  governed. **n/d.**

**Applies when** the GP has genuine inside information and an outside lead prices the round. **Breaks when** Select
becomes a way to defend marks on struggling companies, or when the access edge weakens because hot companies cut
pro-rata.

## 4 · Sector specialization: "no place for generalists"

- **How the model emerged (Svrluga).** Around 2019 the firm needed two things at once. Strong internal hires needed a
  checkbook or they would leave (one did). And the competitor he feared most was the niche micro-fund that does nothing
  but one sector all day. Winning a fintech deal against a fintech-only investor was harder than beating a generalist,
  even though he had two fintech-infrastructure unicorns in Fund I. So the firm specialized at the level of each
  individual partner.
- **The roster on tape:** Sam Toole (healthcare), Emily Man (fintech/financial services; the one external partner hire,
  from Redpoint), Tobias Citron (cybersecurity), Brian Schechter (compute and infrastructure software; runs Primary
  Labs), Jason Shuman (consumer/fintech/proptech/marketplaces in 2021–22), and Cassie Young (go-to-market; oversees the impact team). The Fund V video adds semiconductors/AI
  infrastructure. Count: seven partners (late 2025); "eight partner check-writers" (Sun, 2025); eight sector partners
  (press).
- **The job definition.** Each specialist's goal is to be one of the two or three best seed investors in their sector in
  the world. Svrluga says that goal is impossible if you also have to stay inside NYC, which is why specialization forced
  geographic expansion.
- **Governance: a "miniature fund of funds."** Each partner runs a fairly independent book. Svrluga and Sun share the CIO
  role and a technical IC, but Sun says the IC works as a veto that has **never been used**. They let partners do deals
  they would personally have voted against; some of those became the biggest winners.
- **Why specialists underwrite better (Sun).** Series A investors are now sector-specialized. A seed firm is in an
  "origination business" and has to know what its buyer will want. Emily Man came from the A-round buyer side and knew
  which fintech models A investors avoid (e.g., lending), which suits Sun's internal target of getting 80% of seed bets
  to an A.
- **Partner pipeline.** Most partners were promoted from within. Trust built over 3–5 years of watching someone work
  beats six months of dinners. Svrluga: "You have to lose money before you can learn how to make money" — give people
  the checkbook before you are sure they are ready, and tolerate the losses that build negative pattern recognition.
- **Contrast inside the sources:** Index's Martin Mignot defended a generalist partnership. Index staffs each deal with
  a team based on relationship, proximity and expertise, and its eight fund-returners came from seven different
  partners. Specialization is one valid answer, not the only one.

**Applies when** downstream investors are sector-specialized and the sector has enough deal volume to fill a partner's
book. **Breaks when** a sector cools (Primary largely exited consumer after 6–8 bad years) and a
specialist's book gets stuck in a dead category, or when the best deals cut across categories.

## 5 · The impact team as product: cost, funding, and whether it shows up in returns

- **Headcount trajectory (on tape):** 18 people (Q1 2020, already far larger than peers' 4–5) → 32–33,
  more than half on impact (2022) → ~50, about a third investors (2023–24; Sam Toole: 13 investors and 30+ on the
  portfolio) → 60, investment team under a third (2025–26) → 75–80 by end of 2026. Fund V video: "scaling to an
  80-person team."
- **What the team does.** It started with talent (one person on day one). Then it added strategic finance (models,
  KPIs, data-room prep for the A; separate from fund admin), then go-to-market and market development. The **People &
  Networks** group, led by Rebecca Price, has 9–10 full-time recruiters. They placed a 160th hire by mid-year 2025 and
  were on track for ~180–190 (Sun); the Harlem note says 170 the prior year. Recruiters report cold-outreach connect rates
  **3–6x** higher than at brand-name startups. The **market development** group is a half-dozen to 8 BDR-style
  associates who book customer meetings for portfolio companies; the Fund V video claims 1,300 meetings and $30M of
  qualified pipeline; Shuman cites 1,000+ sales meetings set up in a year. Cassie Young (ex-CRO of a ~$200M ARR SaaS
  company) oversees the whole function, with three former executives running its pillars (~35 people, ~2025).
- **Service ratios on purpose (Shuman).** The impact team has grown faster than the portfolio, because one market-dev
  rep can serve maybe 5–10 companies a month before quality drops. He contrasts this with platform teams nominally
  serving 1,200 companies. Primary avoids the word "platform" for that reason. Cadence: a monthly line-by-line review
  of every portfolio company with the impact team, built around each company's one or two "glass balls" (the things
  it cannot drop), plus Slack channels for hires landed and customer wins. By 2026 the go-to-market practice (run by
  Jason Gelman) had added a go-to-market engineering team for portfolio companies (Cassie Young).
- **How it is paid for.** Mostly from management fees, which is the point. Sun says a fee base on ~$1B AUM makes running
  a firm "very profitable." If they cut half the team, LPs would barely notice, and the partners would keep the money.
  Instead: "let's put the fees on the field." Svrluga describes years of sacrificed current compensation. Sun cites
  Bezos's "your margin is my opportunity." The stated alignment logic is to earn money from carry, not fees, so the
  partners do not become dependent on fees and turn risk-averse (host Nihal Mehta's gloss, which Sun endorsed).
- **What portfolio companies pay.** Recruiting is the only charged service: about **20–25% of the cost** of an outside
  recruiter, run at a loss. They charge so companies take it seriously. Customer development is mostly free. Nothing is
  mandatory, and companies can fire Primary and get their money back (Svrluga says nobody ever has).
- **The KPI pivot.** Once the firm was ~60% non-investors, the impact team drifted toward measuring NPS, the natural
  metric for a professional-services group. Leadership pulled it back to the only metric that matters to LPs, **MOIC**.
  Recruiters now explain their work in terms of value created for the deal partner's companies, and the team withdraws
  resources from companies that are not working.
- **Avoiding adverse selection.** Svrluga: build the capabilities to attract the founders who need them least.
  Compensating for a weak founder with services is a losing strategy. Utilization is highest among *experienced*
  repeat founders.
- **Does it show up in returns?** The evidence offered is all self-reported:
  - **Seed-to-Series-A graduation.** "90% raise an outside-led A" (2021); "over 90% vs 25–30% industry" (Sun). Sun
    says this, not picking skill, drives the unicorn hit rate: he could not predict unicorns a decade out, but he could
    underwrite to the A.
  - **Fund ranks.** Fund I "top 5%" (Svrluga) vs "top 1%" (Sun); "two top-5% global funds" (Shuman, 2022). Svrluga
    said in 2022 that Funds I and II each held multiple companies valued above the entire fund.
  - **Size vs returns.** Sun argues that fund size matters less than stage: small funds look better in rankings because
    early-stage returns are widely dispersed. He says the prior fund, already top-5% by seed-fund size, is on track to
    be Primary's best. Shuman's version is that the partners would not divert money from their own pockets without seeing
    a return. Both are assertions, not evidence.
  - **No counterfactual.** There is no matched comparison of supported vs unsupported companies. Founder selection and
    service intensity are confounded. The impact team's sourcing value (recruiters asking candidates who will found
    next) is another unpriced channel.
- **Cultural cost.** It is an operating company with HR issues, budgets, KPI screens on the walls and pod plans. Svrluga
  says name-brand VCs tell him they would not want his job.

**Applies when** fees are large enough to fund senior operators, and portfolio companies are at the stage where
recruiting and first pipeline are the binding constraints. **Breaks when** fees shrink (a smaller fund or a fee holiday),
when growth-stage investors do not take over and Primary ends up serving B-stage companies, or when the team
optimizes service metrics instead of MOIC.

## 6 · Sourcing and the NYC ecosystem machine

- **The shift in sourcing channels.** In ~2015–16, 30–50% of deals came from other investors' referrals. By 2021 it was
  ~10% (Shuman). Early on the "hitch your wagon" strategy meant regular check-ins with the ~10 established NYC firms
  (First Round, Greycroft, Lerer) to stay in their deal flow. Sun says a new fund today needs its own sourcing edge.
  He has also argued there is no longer proprietary deal flow: nearly every deal has multiple term sheets, so the
  skill that matters is winning. ~2021 KPI: a **90% win-rate target** on issued term sheets, running mid-to-high 80s.
  The win pitch brings the whole team (around 22 people at the time) with a customized deck naming candidate hires and
  target customers.
- **Talent tracking before ideas exist.** Specialists track the best people in their sectors, watch **vesting
  schedules**, and ask who would back whom with personal money. Sun asks strong operators for "the three best people you
  ever worked with" and builds a network of high-signal people inside NYC's scaled startups (Warby Parker and similar).
  Shuman knew one Dandy co-founder for 8–9 years before backing him. Svrluga: if you wait for deals to be shopped,
  "you've already lost."
- **Incubation as sourcing and cost basis.** It started on the side of Sun's desk in Fund I, and was hidden from LPs
  because it looked odd. It became ~**30% of deals**, run by a dozen-person team (Primary Labs, led by Schechter) that
  spins out 4+ companies a year. LPs now cite it as a reason to invest. The model hands strong operators an idea and a
  check on the day they quit. The first two incubations were worth over $1B combined (2022). Incubation seed rounds
  averaged more than $5M at more than a $20M valuation even in the 2022 bear market. Sun compares it to YC: proprietary
  deal flow at a low cost basis. Variant: Coast's founder got a desk at Primary before he had an idea, with a promise
  that Primary would put in the first money.
- **Ecosystem assets as top of funnel.** The NYC Summit started to fix a brand problem: a Sand Hill investor had asked
  one of their founders who Primary was. They solved it by offering West Coast VCs stage time. Svrluga's 2023 welcome
  dates the first edition to 2017 (150 people) and put the fifth at ~2,000. In 2025 he said 2016, nine editions and
  ~3,000; Sun said ~2,000 invite-only in its ninth year. Specialists also run buyer-facing content: Tobias Citron's CISO
  interview series tells founders how security buyers evaluate startups. Also: NYC Tech Awards (Kevin Ryan the first Hall of Fame inductee), NYC Founders
  Guide, a 15-person Founders Fellowship, a Future Founders program, and the Factor nonprofit (first cohort of 20, placing
  underrepresented talent into startups; Harlem Capital is a named partner). Recruiters double as sourcing scouts.
- **Volume and selectivity:**
  - **2020:** ~1,000 opportunities a year, 250–400 looked at closely, 7–10 investments.
  - **~2024 (Sun):** each partner sees ~100 *funded* seed deals a year and does 2–3. Firm-wide, more than 900 closed seed
    deals were reviewed and 9 term sheets issued (under 1%).
  - **Pipeline metric.** Primary counts deals that actually raised, not decks received, to grade a partner's coverage.
  - **NYC coverage.** It saw ~70% of NYC seed deals over three years.
- **Scaled research tools.** An internal expert network of ~3,500 people gives a partner deal context within 24–48
  hours. Marketing "air cover" (outbound tech, events and content production) runs on demand. Sun also co-founded
  **Altus**, a private-company research firm incubated with an ex-BCG partner as CEO. His model is the pre-meeting
  research Tiger Global bought from Bain, sold as "market beta" to all investors. He cites PitchBook's growth from $30M to
  $700M ARR as the market-size precedent.
- **The NYC scorecard (Sun):** 3 private unicorns ~10 years ago vs ~150 now (~200 by his 2025 Bloomberg count), which he says is more than the next ten
  markets combined (Miami has 6). There have been 50+ NYC tech IPOs since 2019, and NYC tech employs 500–600K people vs
  ~1M in the Bay Area. NYC now does ~1,000 seed deals a year to the Bay Area's ~1,200, and took 15% of VC dollars vs 23%
  for the Bay Area. Svrluga's B2B argument: no other market gives you as many customers within reach (finance, media,
  advertising, pharma, CPG). His 2023 Summit figures: 1 in 17 NYC workers is in tech vs 1 in 4 in the Bay Area, and NYC
  has ~70% more Fortune 500 headquarters across twice as many industries.

**Applies when** the firm can build sourcing that compounds (talent graphs, incubation, events) instead of relying on
referrals. **Breaks when** the geography is fully covered by every multi-stage firm (true of NYC since ~2019), so the
edge has to move from where you invest to what you bring.

## 7 · The seed-to-A bar: what founders must prove

- **Underwrite to the Series A, not to the unicorn** (Sun). Seed capital buys 18–24 months of runway. Work out what A
  investors in that category require (customers, revenue, product state, team), underwrite the team's ability to hit it,
  then use the impact team to help them get there. Raise at least six months before cash-out (Shuman).
- **Concrete A thresholds (Shuman, late 2022):** a 3x markup at the A is the target. For SaaS that usually means
  **$1–2M ARR** with a clear path to tripling within a year. Consumer subscription companies now need more like **$5–10M
  ARR**. Unit economics should be profitable after the A. If you raised at a $1B peak valuation and the market halved, you
  need 2x+ growth just to grow back into your price.
- **When Primary doubles down (Svrluga):** before revenue gets large, once product-market fit shows. His example: $200K
  revenue in July grew to $2.5M+ run-rate nine months later, with 50 customers. The test is whether downstream investors
  will look at the company and want to pile in.
- **Founder screens, consistent across speakers.** The founder matters most; Svrluga says he has relearned this every
  year for 25 years. His "three unnatural sales": recruiting employees into a likely failure, raising money from
  investors who expect to lose it, and getting a customer to bet their job and mortgage on a startup. Sun's version is
  "wait for great": would you bet against this person at anything, even French cooking? Sam Toole looks for rate of
  learning. Shuman checks how fast a founder answers email during a raise, and what they learned between month 0 and
  month 12. Non-obvious deals need numbers "popping off the page" (Sun). Sun's weighting (~2021): founder 80–90%,
  market 10–20%. Tailwind markets are the "wet sponge" versus a rock. If the firm loves the founder but disagrees with
  the business model, it passes and stays close in case of a pivot. He also tells founders to spend at least half their
  energy on distribution, not the 90/10 product/distribution split that worked for him in 1997.
- **The "future equity story" (Shuman, ~2025).** Between rounds, the deal partner and the founder write down what the
  company should look like to Series A investors 8–14 months out, and the milestones to get there. Shuman pressure-tests
  these narratives with ~40 later-stage firms because the AI-era goalposts keep moving.
- **Failure modes Primary tries to prevent:**
  - **Bad first hires.** Two wrong hires at a two-founder company can cost a year, and reaching the A with no product
    shipped is fatal.
  - **Late leadership build-out.** Founders settle for "good" hires; Primary shows them a great exemplar they cannot yet
    hire, to calibrate.
  - **Doing too much.** Insecure founders try 20 things to land 2 and land none.
- **Boards:** ~50% of seed rounds now get a formal board. Svrluga says governance fights at seed mean "you've already
  lost." What matters is a quarterly step-back with goals (OKRs or anything else), one-page pre-reads, and under 90
  minutes. Tactical product and branding debates belong in working sessions, not board meetings.
- **Where the playbook does not fit (Sam Toole):** healthcare care-delivery companies often cannot show results within
  18–24 months. They may need to raise more up front, or from alternative capital. Primary avoids FDA-path companies.

**Applies when** A investors have legible, category-specific bars the seed firm can reverse-engineer. **Breaks when** A
rounds become momentum-priced (2021) or freeze (2022), so the target moves mid-runway, or in capital-intensive
categories where 18–24 months cannot reach the proof point.

## 8 · Markups vs DPI, exits, the LP base, and the post-2021 market

- **Exits named on tape (Fund I):** Mirror, ~$500M to Lululemon (2020), the first material exit. An unnamed consumer
  company sold for ~$800M at roughly 20-something times invested capital (early 2026). Alma, announced sale to Spring
  Health (pending, early 2026). Eight Fund I companies passed $100M revenue. Other names: Alloy, Vestwell (fintech
  infrastructure), Jet.com, Latch (SPAC), K Health, Slice (>$100M net revenue), and Dandy (Fund II; pivoted from
  aligners into dental labs; 700+ people). Press adds "two more exits above $500M." **No DPI figure appears on any
  tape.**
- **Markups vs DPI.** The firm's public metric is the unicorn count, which is a markup metric. Svrluga's 2022 claim that
  several companies were valued above the fund size is also about marks. The DPI discipline in this note comes from
  outside voices (see [[harlem-capital-vc-insights]] §13, Lerer's "3-fund hump"). One honest data point: Svrluga's
  survival bar is that average VC firms return less than the S&P 500. **Secondaries: not discussed on any tape (n/d).**
- **Loss-rate drift (by date):** "a third to zero" (2020, 2022); "25–50%" per wire (2021); "40, 50, 60%" and "60% of
  the time" (2026). This matches his AI-era view in §9 of higher failure rates with bigger outliers.
- **LP base:** foreign sovereign wealth funds, university endowments and West Coast foundations (Bloomberg 2021).
  Vintage, a fund-of-funds, was the first institutional LP (Vintage summit). Fund I's tipping point was a single ~$2M
  family-office commitment. Today's largest LPs are $100B+ institutions with $30–50M per fund family. Svrluga says they
  want $500M+ over time and underwrite a 15–20-year relationship. Fund I was raised under the old High Peaks name because
  the largest LP could get "fund three of the old thing" through its committee more easily than a new brand; they
  renamed after the first capital call.
- **LP fundraising craft (Sun and Svrluga):**
  - **The LP memo.** Give the LP a differentiated "why you win" story it can write into its memo. Do what you said you
    would do; strategy drift is a common LP complaint.
  - **GP referrals.** Ask well-regarded co-investors for LP introductions, and brief those GPs on your strategy (Sun did
    this with Thrive's Josh Kushner).
  - **Tourist LPs.** Sun spoke to ~100 LPs in one year and treats LPs who are new to venture or "switching strategy" as a
    red flag.
  - **Allocation squeeze (Mar Hershenson).** Seed managers are squeezed because ~80% of many LPs' venture budget goes to
    multi-stage firms. Sun saw LPs correcting back toward early stage (~2023).
- **Market-cycle reads:**
  - **Mid-2022 (Svrluga):** public markets had corrected and growth investors had repriced, but seed "hasn't gotten the
    memo." He predicted seed repricing within ~6 months.
  - **2021, speed as a weapon (Sun):** using fast closes to win deals was dangerous, because the diligence that matters
    is time with the founder.
  - **2025–26 (Svrluga):** pricing doubles week to week. The OpenAI, Anthropic and SpaceX IPOs will cool the market if
    they trade poorly and heat it if they trade well.
  - **Brand compounding (Sun, quoting an LP):** venture is the one asset class that is not a random walk, because brand
    compounds.

**Applies when** LPs underwrite multi-fund relationships and accept marks as interim evidence. **Breaks when** the
distribution drought ([[wss-private-markets-wealth]] §6) forces LPs to rank managers on DPI, which the unicorn count does
not show.

## 9 · AI's effect on seed: competition, capital intensity, compute bets

- **Opportunity (Svrluga, 2026):** every industry is being turned over by startups, and large software incumbents
  cannot reinvent themselves fast enough. **"Software as work"** opens markets that were hard to sell into. Example: sell
  a landscaper or electrician software that comes with agents to run it, instead of a scheduling tool nobody wants to
  learn.
- **Threat:** the most competition he has seen in any market. Every "off the beaten path" idea has five rivals.
  Incumbency cycles are compressing, so founders must build for model capabilities **12–24 months out** or a newer
  startup will do to them what they are doing to incumbents. Founder quality matters ~"10 times" more. He sees no
  general shift to winner-take-all, except where a hard-to-copy data advantage exists.
- **Portfolio response:** Fund V will hold more companies than any prior fund. The reason is more check-writers, not
  bigger checks. His view: failure rates go up and outliers get bigger, so more shots on goal make sense.
- **Capital intensity and the stack (Schechter):** value will accrue across the whole stack: compute, models (DeepSeek
  reopened the model layer), middleware and applications. Vertical AI now delivers services, not just software. Hence
  specialization by layer and sector, and the Fund V semiconductors/AI-infrastructure partner.
- **The compute bet.** Svrluga said the firm's most valuable company (2025) was a chip company started by two Harvard
  sophomores building an ASIC. It had ~$1.25B of pre-orders 2.5 years in, before shipping. He called it an "utterly
  absurd proposition" that was likely to lose money. He did not name it on tape; the description matches Etched, as the
  brief anticipates. He used it to argue that the best venture deals are often the ones most likely to fail.
- **AI-substitution diligence (Cassie Young, 2026).** She now passes on companies she would have backed a year ago when
  buyers on her reference calls say they would try the product but could probably build it themselves with Claude. Her concern is that ARR
  traction arrives but nothing defensible remains afterward. Point solutions are most exposed. Her test is whether the
  domain needs purpose-built, deterministic accuracy (fund administration, supply-chain operations research) and
  whether the company has a credible path from point solution to platform. Go-to-market tools must hit one of her PRIME
  levers (productivity, retention, investment efficiency, momentum, expense reduction) or she will not invest. At seed
  she tolerates ugly burn multiples if there is a plan to bring them down. Revenue per employee now appears in Series A
  board decks, where it never used to. Both founders frame AI as infrastructure ("like electricity"), not a sector.
  Sun's caveat: today's bigger rounds and prices only work if AI produces bigger exits.
- **Consumer AI:** apart from OpenAI, consumer AI has not happened yet, which is backwards relative to past platform
  shifts. Mignot's explanation is that ChatGPT absorbs most use cases; he expects room in high-liability verticals.
- **AI inside the firm (Sun, Harlem's channel):** a four-person "Primary OS" team builds sourcing agents that watch
  LinkedIn, GitHub, papers, competitions and other VCs' interest. The go-to-market team builds a working
  customer-sourcing app for each finalist during the win process, e.g., mining Google reviews to rank home-services
  prospects. Sun's point is that a subscale firm cannot afford that team.
- **Round inflation (Shuman, ~2025):** AI-era founders treat the next three years as a gold rush and raise much larger
  early rounds, "because they can." Multi-stage firms write seed checks as option bets. His analogy: if a $35M house and
  a $2M house are both on fire, you save the big one first.

**Applies when** AI lowers the cost of building, which raises the number of companies and the size of outliers.
**Breaks when** AI raises capital needs at seed (compute, $50M+ seeds) beyond a ~$50M partner book, or when competition
compresses outcomes so the bigger-outlier assumption fails.

## 10 · How Ben Sun describes starting a fund

- **Pre-history.** Merrill Lynch tech banking (around the Netscape IPO), then founded Community Connect (AsianAvenue,
  BlackPlanet) in 1996–97. He bootstrapped it for two years, raised ~$20M, survived the dot-com crash, reached
  profitability with ~130–150 staff, and sold in early 2008 (Bear Stearns, his banker, collapsed mid-deal). His first
  backer, Bob Goldhammer, invested on the person, not the business. That shaped Sun's "betting on people betting on
  themselves" view.
- **Angel phase as the track record.** At the end of 2009 he set aside "a couple million" and did ~10 deals in 4–5
  years, often joining the board or co-founding. Four became unicorns, including Coupang (pickup basketball at
  Stuyvesant; ~$50M via SPVs and over $1B returned at IPO; market cap quoted at $30–75B depending on date). By ~2023 his
  lifetime record was 40 deals and 8 unicorns. The incubation habit started here, under an entity called Launch Time.
  He spun up a Groupon-style deals business for parents with two engineers and a PM, sold deals to merchants himself,
  and got distribution through Diapers.com via Marc Lore. Coupang came out of explaining that model to Bom Kim.
- **The founder record he invests from.** At Community Connect, ad revenue could not sustain the business after 2000. He
  added paid dating profiles on top of the free social network in 2001, reaching ~$20M revenue and ~$6M EBITDA. The
  company sold to Radio One in 2008, and he says seed investors made ~30x.
- **Why institutionalize:** he could not scale as one person, it was lonely, and the platform he wanted needed
  management fees. Svrluga wrote a public blog post listing seven partner attributes, held 125 coffee meetings, and asked
  Sun about six times. They wrote down shared core values first. Their "how" and "what" differ constantly, but the "why"
  does not.
- **Division of labor:** co-CEOs for about five years. At ~20 people Sun handed the CEO role to Svrluga. Sun now does
  partner mentoring, "R&D" (incubation and experiments), and LP fundraising; he led the latest raise. Succession is
  planned 6–10 years out, and Svrluga says the "vast majority" of firm economics is already held by people other than the
  two founders.
- **His advice to new GPs:** think from first principles rather than copying norms (the 1:1 reserves mistake). Run
  experiments even when LPs do not understand them yet (incubation). Start with a sourcing edge. Know your "why me."
  Svrluga adds: you do not have to compete for every deal.

**Applies when** the would-be GP has a founder-grade or angel-grade track record and a structural idea LPs can memo.
**Breaks when** the "edge" is only a temporary market inefficiency, like NYC in 2015. Sun says that kind of alpha
disappears fast.

**The sharpest contrast:** both firms treat the management fee as the strategic variable, and spend it in opposite
ways. Harlem keeps headcount low and gets leverage from media and community (scalable, near-zero marginal cost).

beat the drag of a bigger fund. Harlem's risk is capacity: a few partners cannot cover a growing fund. Primary's risk is
fee dependence: the model needs fee-scale AUM, which requires either strong DPI or a fundraising market that funds
"largest seed firm" stories.

## Retrieval questions

1. **Why did Primary limit itself to NYC for its first 5–6 years?** It made a credible, winnable story for LPs and let
   the services model work at short distances. Svrluga: "we defined a battle we could win."
2. **Svrluga's 2020 return-the-fund arithmetic for a $100M fund?** Net out $15–20M of fees, assume a third to zero and a
   third at 2x, and the last ~$30M must make ~$300M, so ~10x winners. Every deal must be able to return the fund.
3. **How does Primary Select differ from a GP-led continuation vehicle?** Select puts new capital into new rounds of
   current portfolio companies (no new names). A CV moves existing assets into a new vehicle to give LPs liquidity.
4. **What is Sun's per-partner reframe of fund size?** Divide the fund by its check-writers: ~$50M each, like a solo GP
   with ~10 staff. Judge each book on 5x+, not whether every deal can return the whole fund.
5. **What unicorn rate does Sun say a seed fund needs?** About 10% of seed bets for a 3x+ net fund, since only ~5% of
   unicorns become decacorns. Fund I's claimed 9/25 is 36%. Shuman: higher ownership via incubation cuts the bar to
   ~7.5%.
6. **How is the impact team funded, and what do companies pay?** Mostly management fees ("put the fees on the field").
   Only recruiting is charged, at ~20–25% of outside cost and run at a loss.
7. **What KPI correction did the impact team go through?** It drifted toward NPS, a service metric, and was reoriented to
   MOIC on the deal partners' companies, with resources pulled from companies that are not working.
8. **Shuman's Series A bars (late 2022)?** A 3x step-up. For SaaS, $1–2M ARR with a path to tripling. For consumer
   subscription, more like $5–10M ARR. Profitable unit economics after the A.
