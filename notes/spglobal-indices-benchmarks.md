# Note · S&P Global indices & benchmarks — a licensing toll booth whose moat is regulatory status and the venues that list its products, not the arithmetic of the index

> Market-structure / strategy note · Maps to **Course 04** (Competitive Strategy: toll-booth economics, complements, coopetition with MSCI and the exchanges), **Course 03** (Microeconomics: network effects in liquid ecosystems, two-sided licensing), **E4** (Corporate Governance: benchmark governance, the index committee, regulator settlements), **E9** (Geopolitics & Macro: EU benchmark regulation, passive flows) · Written 2026-10-25 · Part of [[spglobal-hub]]; companions [[spglobal-digital-assets]], [[spglobal-ihs-markit-merger]], [[spglobal-pricing-fixed-income]]

- **Source:** S&P Global press and IR corpus, `_ingest/raw/spglobal/articles.jsonl` (492 items, Jan 2019 – Oct 2026). Used here: about 40 S&P Dow Jones Indices (S&P DJI) releases, the Investor Day 2022 and 2025 decks, the annual-report letters, and earnings releases to Q1 2023. Earnings releases after Q1 2023 are stubs, so the division numbers after that come from the investor-day decks and letters. Routine *set to join S&P 500* rebalancing notices (363 items) were indexed but not read. Tradeweb-side facts come from `_ingest/raw/tradeweb/articles-2019-2026.jsonl`.
- **Core claim:** S&P DJI earns roughly a **70% adjusted margin** (S&P's figures: 69.2% in 2019, 69.9% in 2021, 71.8% in Q1 2023). It does so by charging rent on three things the index math does not explain:
  1. **Regulatory standing.** It was the first benchmark administrator endorsed under the EU Benchmark Regulation (BMR) in 2019. It runs an annual IOSCO review (the 12th was in 2025).
  2. **Venue partnerships that turn an index into a liquid product.** These include CME, which owns 27% of S&P DJI and lists the futures; Cboe, which runs the VIX and DSPX; B3; and, from 2026, Hyperliquid.
  3. **A governance brand.** The index committee says no to exceptions, as in the 2026 MegaCap decision.

  The IHS Markit merger added the credit leg: iBoxx, CDX and iTraxx. After that, the business mostly adds new *places to license*: direct indexing, private loans, private equity and on-chain markets. Rival vendors build indices too, so the moat is shared standards (GICS with MSCI) and liquidity that compounds, not index-building skill. The index business has had one SEC finding (2021, VIX futures) and no reported outage of trust.

## 2. How the toll booth works

| Layer | What S&P DJI owns | Who owns the trade | How S&P gets paid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Index IP and brand | S&P 500, the Dow, iBoxx, CDX/iTraxx, S&P GSCI, GICS (half, with MSCI) | — | Data subscriptions |
| Passive wrappers | — | ETF and mutual-fund issuers | Asset-linked fees (bps of AUM). "Tremendous runway" from active-to-passive (Investor Day 2022) |
| Listed derivatives | — | CME (E-mini futures), Cboe (SPX options, VIX, DSPX), B3 | Per-contract exchange-traded derivative fees, which rise with volatility |
| OTC credit | — | Dealers and SEFs (Tradeweb's SEF for iBoxx TRS) | Licences on CDX/iTraxx and iBoxx TRS |
| New wrappers | — | Brooklyn (direct indexing), Centrifuge, Dinari, Trade[XYZ] on Hyperliquid, Derivnex on Canton | Licence fees, terms undisclosed |

S&P's own description of the core logic: "Our indices underlie the most liquid financial products in the market for both equity and credit through broad ecosystems of complementary instruments" (Investor Day 2022 deck). The index is the shared reference point. The venues and issuers supply the liquidity, and that liquidity makes the reference harder to replace.

## 3. How the narrative moved

1. **2019–2020: independent, and first through the regulator.** Matturri on the EU BMR endorsement: "We are proud to be the first benchmark administrator to be granted this endorsement." Matturri on ESG: "Our philosophy as an independent index provider is to offer choice to investors." The emphasis is on compliance and independence.
2. **2020–2022: add the credit leg.** The April 2020 collaboration presents IHS Markit's indices as ones that "underpin some of the world's most liquid fixed income financial products, including credit default swaps, total return swaps, exchange-traded funds, futures and options". The merger makes them S&P's own.
3. **2021–2025: governance as defence.** After the SEC order, every IOSCO release repeats the control language. Draper (2025): "maintain a robust control framework that protects the independence of our index governance process".
4. **2024–2026: licence everywhere.** Direct indexing ("Direct indexing is part of an ongoing market evolution", Brandon Hass, 2024), private loans and private equity, then on-chain. Clay (2026) on private credit: "As private markets continue to mature, the need for trusted data and benchmarks is becoming more pronounced."
5. **2025: an exchange executive takes over.** Clay came from Cboe's derivatives and Data Vantage businesses. Cheung's announcement: "Her forward-thinking mindset, customer-centric approach and extensive leadership experience are vital for S&P DJI's future." *Inference:* the board chose someone who knows how an index becomes a listed product, rather than someone who builds indices.

## 4. What the claims don't prove

1. **"Independent" is a governance claim, not a financial one.** CME owns 27% of S&P DJI, earns money on S&P 500 futures, and was a 50/50 partner in OSTTRA until 2025. The IOSCO review checks process separation. It cannot remove the incentive to grow listed-product volume.
2. **The 2021 SEC order sits awkwardly with the integrity language.** The finding was about an *undisclosed* mechanism on one volatility product on one day (Volmageddon). It was negligence, neither admitted nor denied, and $9M. The release said S&P DJI "is committed to transparency and the integrity of its benchmark determination process" in the same document. The corpus contains no later disclosure of what changed.
3. **Revenue concentration is hidden in aggregates.** "Financial Exchanges & Data" is 26% of FY2024 revenue, but the corpus never splits out CME versus Cboe versus others. Asset-linked fees move with equity prices: the 2022 ETF AUM fall of 12% reached revenue directly. The ~70% margin is partly the S&P 500's own beta.
4. **"$27.7 trillion in assets indexed or benchmarked" mixes two things.** Most of it is *benchmarked* (active funds measured against an index), which earns little, not *indexed* (passive money paying basis points). The corpus does not separate them.
5. **The MegaCap refusal is principled but untested.** S&P DJI says exceptions "should not be granted solely based on market capitalization". The cost appears only if a very large IPO stays out of the S&P 500 for 12 months while rival indices include it.
6. **GICS is co-owned with a direct competitor.** Each GICS release is a joint S&P–MSCI press release. Consultations get dropped for "lack of market consensus", which is a reminder that users, not the administrators, set the limits.
7. **Platts showed the limit of administrator power in commodities too.** It reversed a Dated Brent change within about three weeks in March 2021 after market pushback, and then made Brent decisions jointly with ICE, the venue.

## 5. Strategic read (inference — not stated by S&P Global)

- **The moat is the complement, not the index.** Anyone can calculate a cap-weighted US index; CRSP and others do. Rivals cannot copy the open interest in E-mini futures, SPX options, VIX and CDX. Each venue partner is also a hostage: CME and Cboe have every reason to keep the S&P 500 central. This is [[canon-brandenburger-nalebuff-coopetition]] in its cleanest form.
- **Regulation as a barrier to entry.** BMR administrator status, IOSCO adherence and the Dutch AFM entity are fixed costs that a crypto-native or AI-native index start-up must also pay. Note that Kaiko keeps its own BMR registration ([[spglobal-digital-assets]]), so the barrier is real but not exclusive.
- **Hiring Clay is a bet on the derivatives venue side.** The new CEO previously licensed the VIX methodology *to* S&P DJI from Cboe in 2024. *Inference:* expect more S&P-licensed listed and perpetual products, and more exclusive-licence deals like DSPX.
- **Credit is the growth leg that equity cannot provide.** Leveraged loans (UBS), private loans (Lincoln), CLO IG indices (2026), and iBoxx tokenized on Canton all extend the same licensing model to fixed income, where the reference is less settled. This links to [[spglobal-pricing-fixed-income]]: the evaluated prices from Market Intelligence feed index valuations ("leveraging S&P Global Market Intelligence's industry leading reference and pricing data for loans").
- **Tradeweb is a distribution venue, not a rival.** The first fully electronic iBoxx TRS traded on Tradeweb's SEF (2021-03-26, Tradeweb corpus; see [[tradeweb-derivatives-regulation]]). Tradeweb's own benchmark business is *closing prices*, where FTSE Russell (LSEG) is the partner, not S&P ([[tradeweb-rates-em]]). The two firms meet at the credit index, not the price.

## 6. Canon lenses

- **Coopetition** ([[canon-brandenburger-nalebuff-coopetition]]): MSCI is a GICS co-owner and an index rival. CME is a shareholder, a venue, and the OSTTRA partner it exited with. Cboe is a licensee, a calculation agent, and the source of the CEO.
- **Power** ([[canon-helmer-7-powers]]): *network economies* (liquidity begets liquidity across futures, options and ETFs), *switching costs* (mandates and performance history tied to the benchmark) and a *cornered resource* (the S&P 500 and Dow trademarks).
- **Information rules** ([[canon-shapiro-varian-information-rules]]): GICS is a standard whose value comes from adoption. Co-owning it with MSCI avoids a standards war that both would lose.
- **Platforms** ([[canon-hagiu-wright-multi-sided-platforms]]): S&P DJI is a licensing platform. Issuers and venues on one side, investors on the other, with the index as the interface.
- **Five forces** ([[canon-porter-competitive-strategy]]): the main threat is *buyer power* from large passive issuers, who can switch to self-indexing or cheaper vendors as ETF fees compress. Investor Day 2025 lists "Fee compression at passive managers" as a market force.

## 7. Practitioner counterpoint

- **For S&P:** the S&P 500's role cannot be challenged in any time frame that matters, and a decade of margins near 70% says the toll is accepted. The SEC finding was narrow. Annual IOSCO reviews and BMR registration are real external checks.
- **Against:** large ETF issuers have moved assets to cheaper benchmarks before. Direct indexing and AI-built portfolios (S&P's own 3AI index, 2025) make a custom index cheap to produce. The exchange-traded derivative revenue depends on volatility, which S&P neither controls nor discloses separately after 2023.
- **Both:** the real economic question is how much of the "$27.7T" pays basis points. Treat the headline as reach, not revenue.

## 8. Open questions to track

1. **CME's 27%.** Does S&P ever buy it out, or does CME sell? Either would change the incentive picture in §4.1.
2. **Clay's first moves.** More exclusive listed-contract licences (DSPX-style)? An S&P 500 perpetual on a US-regulated venue?
3. **MegaCap test.** The first large IPO that fails the 12-month seasoning rule. Does S&P hold the line, and do rival indices take the flows?
4. **GICS AI consultation (results Nov 2026).** How are foundation-model developers classified, and do S&P and MSCI agree?
5. **Fee compression.** Indices' FY2025 growth (S&P cites $1.8B TTM revenue) versus the "10%+" Investor Day 2022 target, once full earnings PDFs are pulled.
6. **TXSE.** Does a new listing venue ever win an S&P 500 constituent?
