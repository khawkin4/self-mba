# Note · Thomson Reuters capital allocation — a controlled company that returned most of the trading-floor money, bought revenue at ~8–10× sales, and is now funding returns with debt

> Corporate-finance / capital-allocation note · Maps to **E2** (Capital Allocation — the CEO/CFO toolkit), **E3** (M&A, Deals & Restructuring — prices, premiums, carve-outs, JV with minimum-return support), **E4** (Corporate Governance — the controlling shareholder and the float cap), **Course 02** (Corporate Finance & Valuation — return of capital vs. buyback, accretion, leverage), **E8** (Investor Relations — how use of proceeds is labelled) · Written 2026-10-25 · Part of [[thomsonreuters-hub]]; companions [[thomsonreuters-refinitiv-lseg-exit]] (where the money came from) and [[thomsonreuters-ai-strategy]] (what the deals were for)

- **Source:** TR press releases and IR documents 2019–2026 (`_ingest/raw/thomsonreuters/articles.jsonl`): every quarterly results release (32), buyback/NCIB and return-of-capital releases, debt releases (2019, 2020, 2025, 2026), every acquisition and divestiture release, the 2021 Investor Day transcript (CFO Q&A on buybacks and Woodbridge), the Casetext call slides (Jun 2023), the KKR print releases (Jul, Oct 2026). No 10-K/40-F, proxy or annual report was read. Deal multiples below are derived from TR's stated revenue expectations, not disclosed by TR.
- **Core claim:** Between 2018 and 2026 TR had two windfalls: ~$17B of F&R cash (2018) and ≈$8.3B of LSEG share sales (2021–24). Most of both went back to shareholders: **$10B in 2018–19**, then a **$2B buyback (2022–23)**, a **$2.2B return of capital (2023)**, **$1B buybacks in 2023–24 and 2025** and a **$1.2B package in 2026**. Acquisitions ran alongside. Roughly **$4B** of disclosed prices went on tax software, e-invoicing and legal AI (SurePrep $500M, Casetext $650M, Pagero ~$800M, SafeSend $600M, plus undisclosed AI tuck-ins), at about **8–10× forward revenue** where revenue was given. Non-core assets were sold (Spain legal, Elite, FindLaw, 51% of Global Print). Two things shape the toolkit:
  - **Woodbridge** (the Thomson family) owns ~**66–70%**, so open-market buybacks are capped by the float. TR therefore uses **Canadian return-of-capital-plus-share-consolidation** transactions that Woodbridge pre-commits to vote for.
  - The LSEG money ran out in 2024. Since then returns are funded from FCF and debt: net debt/EBITDA went from **0.4× (end-2024) to 0.9× (mid-2026)**, and **US$1.3B + C$1.0B** of notes were issued in Sep 2026.

**The print deal, decoded (derived):**
- $500M for 51% implies ~$980M for 100% of a business with 2025 revenue of $490M and adjusted EBITDA of $185M. That is **~5.3× EBITDA**, before the royalty that TR takes back.
- KKR gets a minimum-return backstop, so part of the decline risk stays with TR.
- TR says the deal is ~neutral to margin and adds **60–70bp to organic growth**, because Print (−5% to −7% organic) leaves the growth calculation.
- This is a cosmetic gain to the growth rate *and* a real transfer of a declining asset, but with a put-like obligation attached. Read the minimum-return clause as debt-like.

## 5. Balance sheet and the controller

- **Woodbridge's float cap.** At the 2021 Investor Day, Eastwood said Woodbridge owned 66%, and "to have sufficient public float, we think we need about 70% would be the cap". So open-market buybacks that push Woodbridge above ~70% are off the table, and big returns have to be **pro-rata**. Woodbridge's holdings at Dec 2024: 300.5M shares (~66.8%), and ~69.7% via Thomson Investments Limited.
- **Why return of capital + consolidation?**
  - A Canadian return of capital is tax-efficient for Canadian holders, with an opt-out for taxable non-Canadians.
  - The paired reverse split keeps the share count and per-share metrics as if a buyback had happened, *without changing Woodbridge's percentage*.
  - TR has used it three times (2018, 2023, 2026). It needs a two-thirds shareholder vote and Ontario court approval, and Woodbridge pre-commits each time.
- **Leverage path** (company-defined net debt / adj. EBITDA): 2.1× (Mar 2020, against a 2.5× target) → **0.4× (Dec 2024)** → 0.6× (Dec 2025) → 0.8× (Mar 2026) → **0.9× (Jun 2026)**. Cash fell from $1.97B to $0.58B. Sep 2026: US$800M 5.100% 2028s + US$500M 5.750% 2033s, plus C$1.0B of private placements, to refinance commercial paper.
- **2025 debt housekeeping:** existing notes were swapped into TR Finance LLC paper "to optimize the Thomson Reuters group capital structure and align revenue generation to indebtedness" (~89–96% acceptance on most series).
- **Ventures:** a $100M corporate VC fund (2021), then a **$150M Fund II** (Feb 2025). Several investees later became acquisitions or partners (Materia, Noetica).

## 6. What the claims don't prove

1. **"funded with LSEG sale proceeds" is a label, not a ledger.** The 2026 $605M return of capital is described as the May 2024 LSEG proceeds, yet the Q1 2026 release raised the net-interest outlook to $180–190M (from $150–160M) because of the programme. The cash was spent long ago, and the return is debt-funded in substance.
2. **No deal is graded afterwards.** TR never reports SurePrep, Casetext, Pagero or SafeSend revenue after closing. "Revenue growth accretive" is the only promise, and it can't be checked from releases.
3. **Multiples are only derivable where TR gives revenue** (SurePrep, SafeSend). For Casetext and Pagero, the two AI-strategic deals, the corpus is silent on revenue.
4. **The buyback record looks good because the stock re-rated.** Average prices rose from $49 to $166, so earlier programmes look cheap in hindsight. TR never states an intrinsic-value or return threshold for buybacks; the stated aim was to hold ~500M shares (2020) and then "long-term shareholder value".
5. **The print deal's growth uplift is mechanical.** Removing a −5% business raises reported organic growth by 60–70bp with no change in any customer.

## 7. Strategic read (inference — not TR's words)

- **The hierarchy:**
  1. Dividends, raised 10% a year as a signal of confidence.
  2. Pro-rata returns of capital whenever a lump sum arrives.
  3. Buybacks within the float cap.
  4. Acquisitions that are **adjacent workflow software at growth multiples**, not transformational deals.
  5. Small venture stakes as a pipeline.

  The pattern is consistent from 2019 to 2026. Hasker's 2021 promise of "rigor" held: no deal above ~$800M in eight years, despite "as much as $15 billion" of capacity.
- **Was the capacity wasted?** The $15B figure assumed LSEG at 2021 prices. Most of it went back to holders: ~$7B+ of buybacks and $2.8B of returns of capital. That is the conservative answer to *what to do with a windfall when your controller doesn't need liquidity but values dividends*. The cost is that TR never used its balance sheet to *buy* a step-change AI asset. Its largest legal-AI deal, Casetext, cost $650M. Harvey and the frontier labs were funded by others. Whether that was discipline or a missed window is the open question of the AI era (see [[thomsonreuters-ai-strategy]]).
- **Leverage is the new lever.** With LSEG gone, Print sold and organic growth at ~8%, TR has started levering up (0.4× → 0.9×) to keep paying out. That is fine at 0.9×. The question is whether a larger AI deal would take it toward the old 2.5× target.

## 8. Canon lenses

- **The outsider CEO test** ([[canon-bezos-shareholder-letters]], [[canon-munger-poor-charlies-almanack]]): judge capital allocation by per-share value. TR's share count fell ~13% in 3.5 years while FCF rose from $1.3B to ~$2.1B (2026 guide). FCF per share rose ~80% from 2020 ($1.33B / ~497M ≈ $2.68) to the 2026 guide (~$2.1B / 433M ≈ $4.85; derived), which is the honest scorecard. The letters TR publishes are press releases, so the reasoning has to be inferred.
- **Agency costs and the controller** ([[canon-jensen-meckling-agency-theory]]): a 66–70% holder lowers some agency costs (long horizon, no activists) but constrains tools (no float-shrinking buybacks). It also makes every big return a negotiation with one holder. The return-of-capital structure is the governance workaround.
- **Margin of safety in M&A** ([[canon-klarman-margin-of-safety]]): paying ~8–10× revenue for >20%-growth tax software is reasonable only if retention and cross-sell hold. TR's 90%+ retention base is the margin of safety it's leaning on.
- **Focus** ([[canon-rumelt-good-strategy]]): the divestitures (Spain, Elite, FindLaw, Print) all move revenue toward the Big 3 segments, which went from ~80% of revenue (2020) to 83–85% (2026).

## 9. Practitioner counterpoint

- **Bull:** a disciplined, shareholder-friendly record. Dividend growth with a 33-year streak, buybacks at rising but justified prices, small deals bought at sensible growth multiples, and a clean exit from non-core assets. Organic growth roughly doubled while all this happened.
- **Bear:** the company had the biggest war chest in professional information (2021–24) during the biggest technology shift in its markets, and mostly gave the money back. The AI moat it now advertises rests on content and partners' models, not on anything it bought at scale. Rising leverage to fund returns, plus a minimum-return promise to KKR, are early signs that the easy money is gone.
- **Synthesis:** TR allocated capital like a **mature compounder**, not like an incumbent facing disruption. That is right if the content moat holds ([[thomsonreuters-ai-strategy]]) and wrong if it doesn't.
