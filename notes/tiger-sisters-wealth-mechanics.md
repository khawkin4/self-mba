# Wealth-Building Deal Mechanics — de León & Codie Sanchez (Tiger Sisters)

> **Provenance:** Synthesized from two Tiger Sisters episodes — "How a Self-Made Billionaire Built Wealth From Nothing" (Fernando de León, Forbes 10/10 self-made, ~$2.8B) and "How to Buy a Business With $0 (ft. Codie Sanchez)." Pulled 2026-06-29. Rendered in my own words with brief attributed quotes — not reproductions. **What it adds over the canon:** concrete no-capital deal structures the textbooks gloss over — rent-as-royalty, zero-down options + rezoning, seller financing as the *default* (~60% of small sales), and sweat/expertise-for-equity — plus the seller-psychology scripts that actually close them. Maps to Courses 02 (Finance/Dealmaking) & 10 (Entrepreneurship/Acquisition).

---

## The core thesis: own assets, not income — and you don't need capital to start

- **"Company ownership is the most hidden secret in American capitalism."** (de León) The wealth move is owning an *asset* that compounds and amortizes, not selling time. "You're never too young to own an asset."
- **The capital objection is a self-imposed constraint, not a real one.** de León: when you have nothing, "find maneuvers and tactics for yourself that aren't about capital" — idea, insight, persuasion, charisma, salesmanship — and "maneuver around the opportunity and find value and extract it."
- Codie reframes the same point in deal terms: **"The problem is never that you don't have enough money, it's you don't have enough knowledge... so fill the knowledge gap."** The "you must use your own capital" story is a *lie pushed by the people who profit from you believing it* — PE firms and large corporates who want the deals for themselves. (PE is "the most profitable asset class in the world... over any 10-year rolling period.")
- The mental model both share: a good boring business is **"like a bond"** (Codie) — it just keeps paying you over time. Don't chase the hockey-stick; buy the consistent checks. Connects to [[unit-09-capital-light-businesses]].

## de León Mechanic #1 — Rent-as-royalty (the Chick-fil-A math)

The signature structure. You can't buy into a great private operator, so you **own the real estate they operate from** and convert your rent into a synthetic royalty on their revenue.

- He couldn't invest in Chick-fil-A directly (family-owned, locked in a multi-generational trust). So he owned the *land* — a San Angelo, TX site he'd bought cheap from a bank after a borrower defaulted — and built the store to lease back to them.
- **The math he ran out loud:** if Chick-fil-A sells **$2M of chicken** at that site and pays him **$200,000/yr rent**, then *"for every 10 pieces of chicken they sold, one was being sold on my behalf."* He effectively owned **1/10 (10%) of that site's total revenue production** — as a landlord, not an equity holder.
- **"Whoever leased it from me was effectively my partner on some portion that came to me through rent."** He repeated the structure with **Starbucks, 7-Eleven, Raising Cane's, Chase Bank, CVS.**
- **Why it's antifragile:** rent is contractual and senior to the tenant's profit — you collect even in a bad year, with none of the operating risk. The brand's quality becomes *your* upside (you ride their per-site sales growth via percentage-rent logic) while you sit in the safest part of the capital stack. See [[canon-taleb-antifragile]].
- **The opening that let him win these:** the big real-estate players thought small single-tenant builds were *"beneath me."* de León: *"I'll do whatever it takes."* The contrarian edge was simply *doing the unglamorous deal everyone else skipped* — a [[canon-thiel-zero-to-one]] secret hiding in plain sight.

## de León Mechanic #2 — Zero-down option + rezoning + sweat equity

How he built subdivisions in Dallas at 24 with no money to buy land.

- **The option contract is the lever.** *"What the option contract was — a piece of paper."* He didn't buy the land; he optioned the *right* to buy it (or got the owner to **contribute it into a partnership** for a share of the upside instead of cash).
- **Manufacture value before you pay for it.** While holding the cheap option, he went to the city and got the **zoning changed (agricultural → residential)** through persuasion. The rezoning *itself* raised the land's value — which then unlocked **financing**, minimizing the capital he had to put in. This is value creation arbitraged into leverage: change the asset's permitted use → reprice → borrow against the new price.
- **Sweat/relationship equity to fill the labor gap.** He convinced engineers to do the work *without being paid upfront* ("But you're 24, why am I listening to you?" → "Come on, man, let's do this together" — and "I'll take you hunting"). Once the project executed, they got paid well. He converted the timing of his obligations, not his cash.
- **The seller-psychology read:** the landowner, Harold Pollman (~early 80s, WWII vet), said the parcel was *unbuildable* — he'd tried for 40 years. de León's line: **"What do you have to lose then? If it's not buildable, let me give it a shot."** A motivated/resigned seller and an asset everyone else had written off = the perfect zero-downside entry. He convinced the Army Corps of Engineers + city, designed around the problems, and it took a year.
- **Origin of the whole approach (age 14–15):** he was paid translation fees connecting US developers to Mexican manufacturing-plant sites — then realized *"what I was doing was more than translation... I was giving developers the ability to execute their business plan."* He used his grandmother's relationship with the local union leader to secure stuck job permits, then walked into the developer holding the scarce permits: *"You've been working on these for a year — what would you pay to have access?"* → traded the permits for **5% equity in the building.** He repeated it 4–5 more times. **First thing he ever owned, ~age 15, with zero capital.** This is the agency/information-asymmetry play in raw form (he held the scarce thing the principal needed) — see [[canon-jensen-meckling-agency-theory]].

## de León Mechanic #3 — The duplex starter (the entry-level version)

His literal advice to his 21-year-old nephew asking how to start:

- **Buy a duplex (or townhome), live in one half, lease the other half, and let the tenant's rent service the mortgage on both sides.** Get financing, own the asset.
- The amortization does the work: *"that asset he will pay off over a 15-year loan amortization... he's 21, so he'll be 36 and he'll own an asset."* The discipline is living within your means while the loan pays down.
- The principle generalizes: *"buy a stock, buy a bond, buy a house, buy a duplex, buy something, and then over time make it grow."* The point is to *find your own entry point* into ownership. Connects to [[entrepreneurship-operator-playbook]].

## de León Mechanic #4 — "Find the opposite" (the Crexi thesis)

How he chose what to build outside his comfort zone.

- Offered an office building to buy, he asked himself: **"What is the opposite of an office building?"** He disliked office (high CapEx, tenants roll over, *"cash flows unpredictable"*). The opposite of a single illiquid physical asset = **data**, the *"best commodity to try to own in commercial real estate."*
- He co-founded **Crexi** (~9 years ago, LA) — a Zillow-for-commercial-real-estate listings layer plus a data business: it **harvests, synthesizes, and sells lease comps and sales comps** to brokers, appraisal districts, and city governments. Now ~5M users, hundreds of thousands of subscribers, ~600 employees, second-largest purveyor of CRE data in the US.
- **Edge sourcing:** *"What is a business I want to get into that I know enough about that I have a competitive edge?"* He had a real-estate edge and used it to enter an adjacent data business — transition by leveraging existing domain knowledge into an adjacent, higher-margin, network-effect layer. The data/comps business has the makings of a **scale + network-effects power** ([[canon-helmer-7-powers]]); note he flagged *"semi-monopolistic competitors that were tough to engage and fight through."*

## de León Mechanic #5 — Fear-as-fuel and credentials-as-information-shortcut

- **Fear as fuel:** software/engineering scared him; the move is to *recognize* the fear, *compartmentalize* it ("put it in a box"), then *leverage* it — even with *"a certain resentment or anger against a certain weakness... I'm going to go fight it head-on."* *"If you're comfortable, you're not doing it right."* You train the brain like a weak muscle. Aligns with [[canon-taleb-antifragile]] (stress as input) and [[eyl-insights]].
- **Credentials = information shortcut, with a tax.** On Harvard: **"Brands are information shortcuts... Harvard is an information shortcut just like Apple."** Useful as a *signal* of discipline, but **a name "may often blind you to blind spots"** and can dull *"your scrappiness and your resolve."* The operator's test (Pollman): *"Did Mr. Pullman really care about my Harvard degree? Probably not. He was like, can you get it done or not?"* Don't let the credential become a crutch.
- **Capitalism-is-fair frame:** *"If you provide value, you get rewarded. If what you do doesn't provide value to others, you don't get rewarded."* The $30T US GDP has *"infinite pockets where wealth can be created."* (Tiger Sisters' refinement in the debrief: capitalism's *value-exchange principle* is fair even if *opportunity is not equal* — useful distinction to hold.) See [[tiger-sisters-insights]].

## Codie Mechanic #1 — Seller financing (the default, not the exception)

**Entrepreneurship Through Acquisition (ETA) = buying an existing business 1→2, vs. building a startup 0→1.** The unlock is that you rarely need your own cash.

- **The headline stat:** **"60% of all businesses that are sold are sold with some percentage of seller financing."** The seller *becomes the bank* — you pay them out of the business's **future profits** over time.
- **Why sellers agree:** most small-business owners are **65+**, **more than 60% have no transition/succession plan**, and *"they're ready to go to Florida."* Their problem is *exit + retirement income*, and seller financing solves it (a payment stream + a buyer who keeps the business alive). This is the same demand-side gap the search-fund model exists to fill.
- **Mental model:** it's a leveraged buyout (LBO) for normal people. PE does LBOs with bank/institutional money ("other people's money"); you do a mini-LBO with the seller's own equity as the loan. *"You don't think it's crazy to buy a house with a mortgage. In the future, buying a business will be just as normal."*

## Codie Mechanic #2 — Sweat equity and expertise-for-equity (the other two legs)

Codie's **"three legs of a stool to buy a business without using your own cash":**

1. **Seller financing** (above).
2. **Sweat equity** — same as a Silicon Valley startup grant, applied to a small business: *"Hey, I want to work and do these actions, and if I do these actions, could I get a part — or even the whole — business from you?"* You earn ownership through defined operational work.
3. **Expertise-for-equity** — sharper than generic labor: you bring a *specific* skill the business lacks. *"I am actually an expert in marketing and you have a business that needs more clients. Can I get a percentage of your business if I help market it to more people?"*

- **Start partial, learn on someone else's money.** Codie's Goldman mentor: *"You never want to invest your first $500,000 in a deal — you want to invest somebody else's first $500,000, because you're pretty sure to lose it."* So **buy a piece first** (a partial acquisition) while still employed; don't blow up your life. Men have long done this via "club deals" — owning slices of friends' businesses because they speak the language. Connects to [[entrepreneurship-operator-playbook]].

## Codie Mechanic #3 — Target profile and what to avoid

- **Size zone (search-fund / lower-middle-market):** acquisitions of roughly **$5–30M**, doing **$5–50M revenue** or **$1–5M annual profit** — *"the zone where you can come in as CEO and professionalize the operations to make more money."* (Codie herself also buys much smaller "boring" cash-flow businesses — laundromats, car washes.)
- **Buy a bond, not a turnaround.** *"Don't buy somebody else's pain or problems."* Avoid unprofitable businesses and turnarounds for your first deal. Want: *"a nice boring business, so easy grandma understands it, checks keep coming in month after month for at least the last 5 years."*
- **Don't buy the ugly house on the nice block.** Boring + consistent + simple beats sexy + growing + fragile.

## Codie Mechanic #4 — Diligence: the three hard rules

Diligence is *"a lot of work, but relatively simple... there's a formula you can follow."* The three rules:

1. **Make sure they're not lying to you too badly.** Every seller fudges a little — watch for fake **add-backs** (an "add-back" = an expense the seller claims doesn't really count, used to inflate adjusted earnings) and the *"if you just marketed it, it would grow — so buy it for the future potential"* pitch. **Don't pay for unproven upside.**
2. **The P&L must match the tax return** — to some degree. **"And if it doesn't, you buy it for the [number] that's cheaper."** (Sellers often show a rosy P&L to buyers and a lean return to the IRS; price off the lower, verifiable one.)
3. **Make sure you're buying a business, not a job.** Confirm there are *systems, processes, and enough employees* that you don't end up doing everything yourself — unless you actually want to buy a job (which is OK too, just go in knowing it).

- **Culture is part of diligence.** You're not just interviewing the owner — you interview the top 5–10 lieutenants who'll report to you, because their buy-in determines whether a young new CEO survives the handoff. Often you keep the seller on for a **transition/advisory period written into the contract.**
- **Deals die late, and that's fine.** Sellers have decades of emotional attachment; many pull out near signing, or skeletons surface in month 3 of reading financials. *"Never fall in love with something that can't love you back."* Buffett, via Codie: *"I always think about the deals I did that I shouldn't have"* — not the ones I missed.

## Codie Mechanic #5 — Roll-ups + multiple expansion

The advanced play once you can run one business.

- **Roll-up =** buy multiple small businesses in the *same category* (plumbing, dental, med spas, accounting firms, HVAC) and combine them under one umbrella. *"You buy a cookie shop, then five more cookie shops, and you're the Costco of cookie shops."*
- **Where the value comes from — two distinct levers:**
  - **Operating leverage / economies of scale:** centralize the boring stuff (HR, marketing, scheduling, pricing, software). Med-spa example: put all 5 locations on *one* scheduling platform; consolidate purchasing to negotiate suppliers down (Botox from Allergan: *"instead of $5 a portion, sell it to me for $3 — I'm buying for 100,000 people, not 10,000"*).
  - **Multiple expansion (the financial-engineering lever):** a business is valued at a **multiple** of revenue/profit. A single small shop trades at a *low* multiple; a professionalized multi-location umbrella trades at a *higher* multiple because it's perceived as more stable, more professionalized, higher-margin. So **the combined entity is worth more than the sum of the parts bought individually** — you buy at low multiples and the aggregate re-rates upward. This is the math that powers PE roll-ups. Maps to [[unit-09-capital-light-businesses]].
- **The cost:** every problem multiplies. *"Imagine the issues of buying one company — multiply by five"*: culture clashes, integration, compliance, mismatched software, "why do these two locations hate each other." Not a beginner move — *"you're basically a mini private-equity firm now."*
- **Live example (Tiger Sisters' professors):** two co-searchers bought a **traffic-cone / highway-cone logistics business** and rolled up several — a perfect "boring business" with real complexity underneath (interstate licensing, liability if an employee no-show delays a construction project, scheduling blue-collar crews).

## Codie — Negotiation & seller-psychology scripts

The deals close on **human psychology**, not spreadsheets. Codie's tactical scripts:

- **Mirror the seller.** *"You want to mimic and mirror the person you're buying from."* Buying a manufacturing company? Don't show up looking like a podcast host — go in *"looking like you've done this before, a little rough around the edges."* Mirroring movement/speech (not creepily) measurably raises how much people like you.
- **Find the "perfect-fit seller."** Lean on your own edge — Codie (Latina, Spanish-speaking) can close a landscaping company where others can't; a different cultural connection lets *you* do a better deal elsewhere. *"Business owners don't give a [damn] about PC — they're trying to survive."* If a seller will never sell to *you*, fine: next one. *"There's no last deal."*
- **Decode what an objection really means.** When a seller says "I can't put a young woman in front of my (male) investors," what he's *actually* saying is *"I don't trust your capability."* Reframe to the only thing he cares about: **"What matters? That you make more money, your employees feel taken care of, and we grow more this year than last. We'll handle the profits over here."** Shock + competence breaks the frame. ("Listen, Bob, I'm going to get [it] fixed and we're going to outperform.")
- **Three archetypes of the man across the table (the "three Fs"):** *father figure, friend, or a guy who wants to sleep with you.* Adjust per archetype.
- **Salary/ask scripts (transferable to any negotiation):**
  - **Father figure** → *go for advice, flip to the same side of the table:* "I know I have to negotiate my salary — would you help critique me on how I could do it better and get to the level I want?"
  - **Friend** → tongue-in-cheek + a Chris Voss line: *"Would it be unreasonable if I asked for this?"* + smile and shoulder-shrug. Or: **"Can't blame a girl for asking"** — disarms refusal.
  - The tough card (last archetype): *"What are we doing here? Are we going to lock this down? I'm doing amazing, you're doing amazing, we're all making more money — give me the higher salary."*
- **Prep is the whole game.** *"A pound of pre-production is worth 10 of post."* / *"Prepare to win."* Fear usually = under-preparation; prep dissolves it. And **say fewer things** — high signal-to-noise. Not *"well I was kind of thinking maybe we should…"* but **"Here's what I think we should do, here's why, backed by this data — is A, B, or C right?"**

## Codie — Who this is (and isn't) for, and the meta-move

- **For you if:** you like operating and leading teams, can handle messy real-life situations, don't need it to feel glamorous (*"you're going to be changing the printer cartridge"*), and can decide without a 47-page deck.
- **Not for you if:** you want passive income (*"there's nothing passive about this"*), hate managing humans, want "work-from-Bali energy," or think buying a business means never working again.
- **Why startups are the worse bet for most:** most businesses never make money; those that do lose money for 3–4 years on average; the average entrepreneur makes **$46K–$67K/yr salary** for all that risk — *"you could make more as an employee,"* and you have to *fund* the startup for the *right* to maybe one day make money. VCs are fine with 1-in-10 odds; *"that's okay for the VC, it's not okay for the founder."* See [[canon-thiel-zero-to-one]] for the opposing (build-a-monopoly) case — Codie's argument is that ETA gives better *expected* value for the operator who isn't chasing a unicorn.
- **Best ROI is spelled YOU.** 90-day move: go learn dealmaking, or learn *something* — *"the more you invest in getting better yourself... the more money you'll make,"* unlike every other asset class which has a ceiling.
- **Information + implementation.** *"If information was enough, librarians would be rich."* And the durable second-order benefit: learn to do one deal and *"it'll be like the Matrix — you won't see the world the same. You'll see opportunities you hadn't seen before."* This is the real product of ETA literacy. See [[eyl-insights]] and [[tiger-sisters-insights]].
- **Critique of the classic search fund:** great for the *fund*, often *terrible* for the *searcher* — investors put up a small amount but take **50–70% of the company** that the searcher finds, runs, and raises money for. Codie's counter-model: learn to buy for **<$1,000/month and keep all your economics.** Weigh search-fund infrastructure (Stanford/HBS ecosystem, ~2-year search, RoFR-funded acquisition) against keeping your own equity.
