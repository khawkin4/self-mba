## 1 · Fund economics — the bedrock mechanics
- **2-and-20:** ~2% annual management fee (a $500M fund → $10M/yr for salaries/offices/diligence) +
  **20% carried interest** = the GP's cut of *profits* (elite funds 30%; challengers waive/taper the fee).
- **The 4-step distribution waterfall:** (1) return of capital to LPs, (2) **8% preferred return / hurdle**,
  (3) GP **catch-up**, (4) **80/20** split above. Example: $100M fund returns $300M → return $100M → $200M
  profit → GP carry = $40M.
- **European vs American waterfall:** VC uses **European** (LPs get *all* capital back first, then 80/20) —
  because VC loses half+ the portfolio, LPs won't let the GP take carry on the first winner. PE uses
  **American** (deal-by-deal carry, with **clawbacks** if later losses shortchange LPs).
- **Carry is split into units, set up front,** and is **$0 if the fund never reaches full-carry mode** — and
  then you likely can't raise the next fund. The J-curve + power law (~75% of startups fail) mean winners
  must cover all losses *and* return profit; **markups are only leading indicators** (can evaporate 70%).
- **The carry tax fight:** capital-gains vs ordinary-income treatment ("carry loophole") — perennial.

## 2 · Fund size is the master constraint (everything cascades from it)
- **Camp → ownership → reserves → check size all flow from fund size.** Three portfolio-construction camps:
  concentrated (<20 deals), diversified (20–45, the seed average ~28 per Sapphire), spray-and-pray (45+).
- **Ownership is math indexed to fund size:** sub-$50M funds can run low ownership (5–8%) because a
  $500M–$1B outcome returns the fund; at **$100M+ you must own 10–20% or the math fails.** HC: *"$1–2.5M for
  10–15%"* — can't go below that threshold. First US funds average $40–50M (the 5–8%-ownership sweet spot).
- **Price only matters relative to fund size** (echoes Keith Rabois "seed price doesn't matter" — true only
  when small). "Markets always correct" — outsized-outcome arguments collapse when valuations drop 70%.
- **Reserves/SPVs are a capacity question,** not a right/wrong — how many boards a human can sit on,
  back-office load. Back into max check: camp → ownership target → reserve model (~1:1) → solve for check.

## 3 · The two jobs of VC (different skill sets)
- **(1) Pick/win deals vs. (2) run the fund/firm** (from TPG's Jim Coulter). Great sourcing makes a great
  *angel*; great fund-management is a separate money-management skill (a16z's $4.5B fund ≠ deal-picking).
  *"Minimize headcount, maximize AUM."* Emerging managers wrongly think picking = managing.
- **LPs buy institutionalized *process*, not a lucky home run** — they'd take a steady 2x ×10 funds over a
  5x-1x-3x-2x scatter. They invest in repeatable sourcing + memos + back-office. "Can you operate at scale
  for long periods?"
- **Deal memos = 30–60 pages, built in PowerPoint** (deals close in 48–72h): consolidate diligence for fast
  partner review, prove process to LPs, and **gift the memo to the founder post-close** (+ share downstream
  to short-circuit the next investor's diligence). Founders are stunned — it shows their flaws *and* that
  "we invested" ≠ "we think you're flawless; the reward simply exceeded the risk."
- **Junior-speaks-first IC** (associate → GP) + comments in Word not Google Docs, so seniors don't anchor.

## 4 · How VCs decide — the investment bar
- **HC's box (need 2 of 4 *great*):** Founder, Market, Business, Ownership. Two great → invest; you never
  get all four. *"You don't know a 1-in-1,000 company until you've seen 1,000"* — reps compound judgment.
- **The lens shifts with reps + the cycle:** deal #1 was an unknown accelerator founder; #50 was someone
  known 12 years → **network-driven**, and **market now matters far more** ("great founder + great company"
  isn't enough without market tailwind + large TAM). Downturn → away from consumer, toward B2B/vertical
  software + AI.
- **"Is it venture?" three tests** (John Henry): market size; founder vision/storytelling (the swing factor —
  Casper/SoulCycle/WeWork "weren't obviously venture" but executed that way); *will investors actually fund
  it* (Amazon unprofitable for ~decades but fundable). Venture = trade equity for 50%+–3x annual growth and
  a mandatory IPO/acquisition exit; a 10–15%/yr business is a great company but **not venture.** *(↔
  entrepreneurship-playbook §5 "don't put jet fuel in a Camry.")*
- **Founder signals others use:** Founders Fund — must be the founder's *life's work* (not a "whiteboard
  founder"). a16z — the **"idea maze"** (do they know which turns lead to death vs treasure, who the past
  casualties were, which tech moves the maze walls?); if stumped in 20 min, bad sign. GV — back **velocity**
  (direction + speed), build-for-yourself intuition; joy/delight drives adoption more than productivity.
- **HC's early risk-reduction model:** diverse founders often build $100M–$500M companies, so early on HC
  **didn't invest pre-revenue** (preferred ~2 yrs of revenue) — the model doesn't *require* every bet to be
  a unicorn. Macro: 60% of *exits* are enterprise but 60% of exit *value* is consumer (the giant outcomes
  skew consumer). West Coast = spray-and-pray for billion-dollar swings; East Coast = "intelligent investor"
  risk-reduction temperament.

## 5 · Building a fund from nothing (the founder-of-a-fund playbook)
- **The 4 things LPs buy, ranked** (Brandon Bryant): **(1) Team** (who, tenure together, track record — HC
  met 16 yrs ago, interned/roomed/banked, group-text → LLC; don't raise solo if avoidable). **(2) Strategy**
  (stage/asset class + why *you* are uniquely qualified; research what the LP needs to add to their book).
  **(3) Deals & Sourcing** (could be #1 — you need an angel portfolio; LPs reference-call every founder;
  *sourcing is the future bet*). **(4) Returns** (take 10–12 yrs → least important for Fund I, critical by
  II–III; markups as leading indicators).
- **Start as an angel syndicate:** HC began 2015 as an LLC, 4 partners × $5k/deal → one **$25k "Harlem
  Capital Angel" check** ("buying businesses instead of bottles" on IB salaries) — one entity converts
  better than four small checks. Built a track record → institutional Fund I.
- **Fund I was brutal:** 18 months, ~400–500 meetings, 12 of 18 months *while at HBS* (Amtrak to NYC
  Wed→Sun, fundraised through spring break); 55 LPs, **6 institutions** (rare for a first-timer). **Fund II:
  $134M in 6 months** (post-George-Floyd tailwind). Took **zero salary** while raising to avoid "funding
  their lifestyle with fees." Check size scaled $25k angel → $250k Fund-I-first (a terrifying 10x) → $2M
  Fund-II-average in ~3 years — *it gets easier.*
- **GP commit mechanics:** standard 1% (HC set it on the *target* not the cap); Fund II used an **SVB GP
  credit line** (bank funds 60% of the stake, GP puts 40% cash) because stacked commitments with no realized
  carry strain a young GP. "Use other people's money as much as possible" (Henry Kravis was an LP).
- **Most common LP "no":** don't back first-timers; don't believe diversity is an asset class; "you can't be
  a specialist for 30–40% of the market." HC reframe: *"we know the underserved 2% better than anyone."*
- **Fundraising discipline:** **ask for the money, not advice** ("advice is cheap" — a billionaire's note);
  **do the LP-intro homework for them** (scrape the boards your LPs sit on, hand them 5–10 names, ask for
  2–3 intros — "it's not my job to find people in *your* network"); rising minimums ($100k→$250k→$500k)
  force first-movers; an **anchor who advocates** (TPG rode along to vouch; sourced 3 of 6 institutions) is
  worth multiples of its check. "Don't take the no personally — the people you expect to back you won't."
- **The intern engine as moat + mission multiplier:** 6 interns × 3 classes/yr; applications 125 → 1,250;
  **24 alumni now work at other VC funds** — seeding diverse investors industry-wide to hit the 1,000-founder
  goal HC alone (8–15 deals/yr) can't.

## 6 · Brand / media / trust as the scalable lever
- **Naval's leverage, applied to a small seed fund:** capital and headcount don't scale — **code and media
  do** — so HC goes all-in on media as its sourcing *and* capital engine. *"You don't have to create, you
  just document"* the journey (wins and losses). "Purpose opens doors profit can't."
- **Brand → inbound, at every scale:** "people banged down our doors at $25k checks"; **~$50M of LP capital
  traced to LinkedIn DMs + press.** One Stanford-MBA DM → KKR's Roberts family → first $1M check → TPG anchor
  → ~$21M direct + halo. A Sunday WSJ print feature → Jamie Dimon's chief of staff emailed within days; two
  billionaires invested after a CNBC hit. Founders now DM "I've been watching your YouTube" — *that is
  sourcing.*
- **Platform discipline:** curate LinkedIn for the premium audience (bosses/LPs/founders follow there);
  diversify platforms (you can lose a channel — post-Elon, X reach collapsed vs LinkedIn). Newsletter/YouTube
  as owned platforms. Time portfolio press to when product/waitlist can convert, or right before a raise to
  harvest investor inbound.
- **Trust is the real moat (pre-fundraise):** "the future of investing is trust" — when everyone has money
  and AI to reach founders, the winner is whoever the founder *trusts* enough to let on the board. So
  **track founders for years before they have a company** (ex-OpenAI/Anthropic founding engineers — many
  2024–25 **$100M–$1B seed rounds** went to them); be the first call at company formation. The SoHo HQ
  (incubate a billion-dollar company in-house, "turn overhead into an asset"), dinners, paddle, basketball
  are all relationship/sourcing surface. "Maximize your surface area for luck."

## 7 · The diverse-founder thesis (pitched as inefficiency, not charity)
- **~3% of VC goes to women/underrepresented founders who are ~70%+ of the population → a market
  inefficiency → invest in an overlooked group for outsized returns + impact.** Funding ebbs/flows with news
  cycles (spikes post-George-Floyd, #MeToo) → **invest counter-cyclically.**
- **Two-sided market:** capital scales in weeks (OpenAI $40B→$100B) but you must *create* diverse founders
  (give them confidence to leave Facebook) — the slow side. "You can't be what you can't see" forces
  ecosystem/media/events/reports a white-male-Stanford fund never needs ("they can put a sign on the corner
  and find them"). A diverse GP *investing in diverse founders* has deeper network effects than a diverse GP
  with a white portfolio. "Talent is evenly distributed; opportunity is not."
- **Downstream-capital risk is the killer in a downturn** (Henri, post-SVB): early-stage isn't immediately
  hurt, but harder GP fundraising → harder *downstream* rounds for portfolio cos. HC did **zero deals in
  one Q4** (a first). Post-crash vintages historically outperform; 2021–22 vintages won't.

## Founder-side takeaways (the mirror — what to do differently knowing how VCs think)
1. **Know if you're even a venture business** — a path to $100M–$1B+ via IPO/acquisition + 50%+ annual
   growth. If you're a great cash-flow business, a "no" isn't an insult. *(↔ playbook §5.)*
2. **Match your raise to the investor's fund size** — a tiny check from a big fund (or asking a small fund to
   lead a hot priced round) breaks *their* ownership math. Understand their portfolio construction first.
3. **Engineer warm intros and do the work for them** — pre-build the target list, ask for 2–3 specific
   intros; the DM that became $21M proves warm + inbound beats spray (~2% cold-reply). *(↔ playbook §4.)*
4. **Ask for the check, not advice;** project calculated conviction.
5. **Storytelling + materials are the gate past ~$3M revenue** — investors see 4–5k cos/yr, pick 2–3; have
   a tight framework, painkiller-not-vitamin clarity, idea-maze depth. *(↔ playbook §1 "interesting = no.")*
6. **Treat the raise as a distinct, time-boxed job** (3–6 months + ~8 weeks of data-room prep); switch from
   "sell customers" mode to "sell the vision" mode.
7. **Build cash-efficiency / a clear milestone in** — don't assume a quick markup round (the 2021 fallacy);
   downstream-capital risk can strand you. *(↔ playbook §5 "between-rounds death.")*
8. **Win on more than money** — at 10 term sheets, pick the investor with unique value-add/network/trust;
   and remember your investor still prices real risk in you (the memo proves it).
9. **Use brand strategically but authentically** ("document, don't create"); time announcements to convert
   the funnel. **"You're always launching"** — give each pivot a real 90-day–6-month test, then kill fast.

## 8 · Brandon's 4-characteristic framework (deepening §4)
- **5,000 deals/year, invests in <10 → 99%+ rejection rate.** The four things that convince an investor to
  write the check:
  1. **Management Team** — broken into four sub-tests:
     **(a) Tenure** — prefer teams that have worked together at multiple companies or known each other for
     years (college roommates, childhood teammates). High-stakes + money strains relationships; prior
     trust provides resilience.
     **(b) Experience / Expertise** — domain fit. AI agents for dental practices? Want someone who went to
     dental school, family managed 30 practices, or manufactured dental products. Not transferable skills —
     actual domain lineage.
     **(c) Pedigree / Reputation** — not just school logos but "best professor at best dental school would
     say you were a top student." Three-question reference protocol: (1) Are they exceptional to work
     with? (2) Would you invest your own personal capital? (3) Would you start a business with them?
     All three yeses → go deeper on superpowers.
     **(d) Culture / Mission** — what type of people does this company attract? "OpenAI people vs Anthropic
     people vs Brex people vs Ramp people are all *different types*." The mission shapes the talent pool
     and therefore the execution ceiling.
  2. **Velocity** — not raw speed but *how fast you get to the right solution.* "Good businesses don't avoid
     problems — they solve problems faster than other businesses." Lean into adversity, not away from it.
     "Anyone can start an app now with AI — the question is how fast you get to the product that runs someone's
     entire business." *(Echoes [[entrepreneurship-operator-playbook]] §5 PMF velocity.)*
  3. **Ability to hire exceptional talent** — can you recruit from centers of excellence? (nuclear company
     pulling engineers from SpaceX, NASA, Anduril, Uber Freight, Tesla). Can you *retain* them? What is
     your *reputation* around talent? "A+ players want to play with other A+ players — LeBron's team, not a
     no-name's team." *(↔ [[canon-bezos-shareholder-letters]] "hire missionaries not mercenaries.")*
  4. **Intangibles / Superpowers** — irreplicable founder qualities. Sierra's Bret Taylor: started company
     acquired by Facebook, CTO of Facebook mid-20s, chairman of Twitter, co-CEO Salesforce, now chairman of
     OpenAI. That access/insight creates 10x–1000x leverage. Pattern: founders who sold sneakers on the
     internet as teenagers (Cursor founders, MerchCore founders, Brandon himself — eBay power seller at 16).
     "Can you productize that unfair advantage?"
- **Ownership math reiterated:** 5% of a $1B exit vs 8% = difference between 5x-ing and 8x-ing the fund.
  "Every single piece of ownership from your first investment is very important." *(Reinforces §2.)*

## 9 · Henri's IC mechanics and voting system (deepening §3 & §4)
- **1-to-10 scoring system:** every partner scores each deal. Average deal score across the portfolio: **7.6**;
  top-performing investments averaged **8.1–8.2**. The delta looks tiny but is statistically significant across
  hundreds of deals.
- **Single-partner conviction cap: 60% of deals.** Maximum fraction of investments where only one partner
  has high conviction — the rest must have broader team buy-in. Team vote correlates more strongly with
  outcome than any individual partner's enthusiasm.
- **Speed metrics:** fastest deal ever closed in **36 hours**; average from first call to term sheet: **13 days.**
- **Four founder archetypes** that HC sorts into: **(1) Stars** (obvious talent, pedigree, momentum),
  **(2) Veterans** (deep industry operators who know the space cold), **(3) Academics** (researchers with
  technical depth turning insights into products), **(4) Operators** (execution machines from scaled companies).
  Each archetype has different risk profiles and evaluation heuristics.
- **Diversification across 10 dimensions** — not just sector/stage but geography, founder background, business
  model, customer segment, etc. Deliberate portfolio balance; no single dimension dominates. Josh Kaufman
  ownership target: think backwards from "what ownership % do I need at exit to return the fund?"
- **References beat interviews** (Harvard Business School study Henri cites) — what former colleagues say
  predicts founder success better than any interview performance. HC operationalizes this: reference calls
  on every founder, not just competitive deals.
- **Fund I median ownership: 8%; Fund II: 10%.** The step-up tracks fund-size scaling (§2) — larger fund
  demands more ownership, which demands larger checks, which demands winning competitive rounds.
- **100 interns through the program, 34 now work in venture** — evolving the metric from §5's earlier count.
  HC's intern engine is now producing a measurable fraction of diverse VCs industry-wide.

## 10 · The LP perspective — what allocators actually want (Beezer Clarkson / Sapphire Partners)
- **Sapphire Partners + CalSTRS partnership:** Sapphire acquired CalSTRS's legacy emerging-manager VC
  portfolio and the go-forward program. CalSTRS = world's largest educator pension. "We feel immense
  gratitude and responsibility investing teachers' pensions" — changes the LP's risk tolerance calculus.
  CalSTRS has been investing in emerging managers (both PE and VC) since the early 2000s — *decades* of
  institutional commitment. *(↔ [[canon-jensen-meckling-agency-theory]] — principal monitors agent.)*
- **The universal filter — same for emerging and established managers:**
  - Seed/pre-seed funds → **can this be a 5x net?**
  - Series A funds → **can this be a 3x net?**
  - Later stage → same math, just harder to see a $3B vehicle at 3x.
  This is why Sapphire focuses where they focus: return potential ceiling drives allocation.
- **Fund 1 data gap:** LP must infer from prior career. What has the GP done that gives reason to believe
  they can be this kind of investor? Fund 2–3 → more data: portfolio selection, go-to-market, how they
  compete, firm structure. "Same filter, different inputs."
- **Deck requirements (what to include when pitching an LP):**
  - **Team as people, not logos** — who you are, not just where you went to school.
  - **Have you managed someone else's money?** Even at scout/AngelList level. Sapphire has not yet invested
    in someone who has never managed external capital. *(Tension with §5's "start from zero" playbook —
    there's a gap between angel-with-own-money and LP-ready.)*
  - **Check sizes + portfolio construction** alignment — $5–10K checks before ≠ $1M checks now requires
    translation. $200K→$200K is an easier narrative than $5K→$1M.
  - **The "why you" factor** — not just the thesis but why *this GP* is the best person on the planet to
    execute it. What network, access, or insight creates a right to win?
  - **Loom video over a deck = "amazing"** — 3 minutes of talking points over slides. One-to-many format
    that lets the LP process asynchronously. Beezer: "if I could wave a magic wand and have that happen
    always, it was just the best."
- **Audited funds required** at institutional LP level (vs. sub-$10M friends-and-family funds that don't
  need audits). This is a structural gate between angel-fund and institutional-fund worlds.
- **Portfolio construction: open-minded but guarded.** Generalist, specialist, concentrated, diversified — all
  can work. But: "if you want a billion-dollar fund with 1% stake in 2,000 companies, the math is going to
  work against you." Many emerging managers lack experience to model their own portfolio outcomes. An LP like
  Sapphire thinks in portfolio-outcome distributions; the GP should too.
- **First-principles thinking separates winners:** "The people I see being successful really sit down and
  think through what they believe is true about themselves as investors, what they believe about the world,
  how they do this." Authentic + unique perspective is "really powerful."
- **Venture's time horizon is extreme:** each fund vehicle = **15–18–20 years.** "Longer than marriages.
  Your kid's not going to be at home that long." If you're not committed for 40+ years of fund-raising
  cycles, consider alternatives: AngelList, rolling funds, scouts. "Raising for institutional LPs might
  not be the best way to dabble." *(Counterpoint to the "anyone can start a fund" narrative.)*
- **Current market advice (2025–26):**
  - Harder market — don't sugarcoat it. Valuations "all over the place."
  - Communicate portfolio development at a granular level: not just TVPI but product-market fit, growth
    rates, cash burn, operating margin. LPs are asking for operational insight, not just marks.
  - "The last few years were an aberration, not the norm." Fundraising taking longer is historically normal.
  - Some EMs merging with others or hanging up skates — "fine too."
  - "When the good times come back, we'll know what to do."
  - Beezer's own first fund shut down: "going through down markets teaches you a ton."
- **Social media / brand for EMs (LP view):** Sapphire is not opposed — wants to understand *how it supports
  strategy.* If it increases sourcing, builds community, opens doors → great. If it's a disproportionate
  time sink that's "fun but not productive" → that's a flag. Old analogy: "how many hours a day are you
  spending golfing, Mr. VC?" Build-in-public and rolling-fund fundraising via social media: "phenomenal" —
  funds that couldn't have existed through traditional channels have been raised this way.
- **Open LP democratization thesis:** "The arbitrage shouldn't be that Nicole knows how to make a data room
  better than Beezer — that's a crappy competitive advantage." The same way founders shouldn't fail because
  they didn't know interview structure, GPs shouldn't fail because they didn't know how LPs think. "It's hard
  enough to pick great companies — the other stuff should be table stakes."

## 11 · Emerging-manager scaling playbook (Eniac $1.6M→$220M, Primary $60M→$1.65B)

### Eniac (Nihal Mehta)
- **Fund I: $1.6M (2009).** No fees taken — fees would have been ~$30K/year ("can't pay anyone off that").
  Three founding partners are all engineers, still running startups — investing was "nights and weekends, an
  experiment." Treated it like a real fund: **32–40 slugs per fund, half reserved for follow-on** — same
  portfolio construction since inception.
- **Transition: "when you can pay yourself from your hobbies, you're living the dream."** Nights-and-weekends
  became the day job.
- **Current state (Fund VII, $220M):** 4 partners (Tim, Hadley, Kristen, Nihal), steady state of ~10 people +
  1 junior investor + platform + CFO + ops. Lean and partner-centric even at $220M. *(↔ §2 "minimize
  headcount, maximize AUM.")*
- **The seed investor landscape has leveled:** "if you look at the seed investors of all the AI unicorns and
  decacorns, there's not actually one consistent seed investor. They're young, older, second-time, all over
  the place. It gets consolidated downstream [Series A/B/C] at Andreessen, Thrive, Lightspeed, Sequoia." The
  implication: seed is democratized, Series A+ is oligopolistic.
- **The Human Rolodex method:** old-school Google Sheet with tabs by city, always open on every machine, 20+
  years running. People at top of each list = need to catch up with. When visiting a city, "bomb that list."
  "Karma is real in our industry — when you help a bunch of folks, it comes back 100x." Ahimsa philosophy
  (Jain principle of non-violence/compassion): give without asking for anything in return. "Reputation
  compounds over years — it can be broken in a second." *(↔ §6 trust-as-moat.)*
- **Pitch and Run:** running community for founders, now in 18 cities. Monday circle: introduce yourself, put
  something you want to manifest for the week. "Building a startup is very lonely — other founders know."
  Organic exponential growth over 5 years. *(Relationship/sourcing surface — extends §6.)*

### Primary VC (Ben Sun)
- **Origin story:** Ben Sun founded Community Connect (AsianAvenue/BlackPlanet/MiGente) — 12-year run. First
  VC investor was Bob Goldhammer, early 70s, former Kidder Peabody vice chairman, didn't use email ("I had to
  fax him everything"). Bob invested $5M and said: "more importantly, I'm betting on you." Bob's prior bet:
  a biotech penny stock (the Waxel brothers) → **sold to Eli Lilly for $7B.** Lesson: "the amazing thing
  about people is they could have potentially unlimited upside."
- **Post-exit reflection:** "Were my VCs really that helpful beyond the capital? No." Plus some "shady
  maneuvering" — VCs agreeing to terms then trying to change at the last minute. Bob (not a VC) was the real
  mentor. This shaped Primary's north star: "the more you operationally get involved and know the ground
  truth, the better the investor you'll be. Over time, if you build a reputation of being really helpful, you
  get the better deals with the best founders."
- **Angel period / Coupang:** invested own capital for 4–5 years. Met Bomb Kim playing pickup basketball at
  Stuyvesant High School. Brainstormed ideas for a year (none stuck). Bomb went to HBS; over lunch Ben
  explained Groupon → Bomb said "will this work for Korea?" → dropped out of HBS → Ben invested + joined
  board. Coupang is now a **$40–50B market cap, 100K+ employees,** the Amazon + DoorDash of Korea. Ben put
  together SPVs totaling ~$50M invested → **over $1B in returns at IPO.** "Life-changing event."
- **Meeting co-founder Brad:** Brad had started High Peaks (a fund where his two partners lost 95 cents on the
  dollar, but Brad carried it to a 1.5x fund). LPs bet on Brad solo → he raised a second fund but hated
  being a solo GP ("a really lonely job"). 2.5-hour lunch → co-founded Primary in 2013–14.
- **NYC thesis (2013–14):** "Tumblr ($1B to Yahoo) was the biggest exit in NYC tech history. We had one
  unicorn exit." Thesis: New York was always a great magnet for talent — they just weren't working on
  startups. **Financial crisis displaced 200K Wall Street jobs** → Mark Lori (Diapers.com/Jet/Wonder), Dave
  Gilboa (Warby Parker), Alexa von Tobel (LearnVest), etc. "Talent was there; opportunity wasn't."
- **Fund I–II ($60M + $100M, NYC-only): 8 unicorns from ~35 deals** — an extraordinary hit rate. Then the
  secret got out: 50+ NYC tech IPOs (Datadog, MongoDB, Oscar, Peloton, Warby Parker, DoubleVerify, Yext...),
  150 private unicorns (#2 after Bay Area, more than next 10 markets combined). Every VC opened a NYC office.
  "Who's your competition? Everybody."
- **Scaling into industrialized seed:** US seed market grew from ~$5B to **$20B+ in 10 years** (4x). Millions
  of people now work at venture-backed companies → primordial pool of founding talent → AI as the spark.
  Capital follows great people on great ideas. A $1B seed fund deploying $200M/year in checks = **1% of the
  seed market.** The framing resets the "too big for seed" objection.
- **Management fees as the operating budget:** 2% of a bigger fund → bigger team → more surface area.
  Primary now: **65+ people (growing to 80), 10 recruiters** who **directly placed 170 people** in portfolio
  companies last year. Go-to-market team. Strategic finance support. "Most seed funds were small boutiquey
  low-resource firms — we said, how do we supercharge it?"
- **Primary's 4 controllable inputs** (borrowed from Amazon's outputs-vs-inputs framework):
  1. **Seeing** deals (events, content marketing, AI sourcing agents).
  2. **Picking** deals (research, intel, team analysis).
  3. **Winning** deals ("forward-deployed motion" — learn the business during diligence, pitch with actual
     customers found and recruits identified, not just slides).
  4. **Supporting** deals (recruiters, go-to-market, strategic finance embedded in portfolio cos).
- **"Show don't tell" in the win pitch:** go-to-market team literally builds a custom app/dashboard for
  each finalist company showing customers they've already sourced, enriched with AI signals (e.g., combing
  Google reviews for a home-services AI company to find which merchants are the best prospects). Not slides —
  a working tool. "When we do it, they're like, wow, this is different."
- **Fund size ≠ worse returns (contrarian):** "it's not really the size, it's the stage." Early stage =
  high return dispersion (one fund 50x, another 0x). Late stage = compressed (3x vs 1.5x). Reporting the
  "best funds" picks the top of the high-dispersion distribution, which are small *because they're early
  stage*, not because small is inherently better. Primary's largest fund is on track to be their best
  performer. *(Important counter-narrative to the conventional "small fund = better returns" heuristic.)*
- **Altus (incubation — VC research-as-a-product):** Tiger Global paid Bain $100M+/yr to do pre-meeting
  research on companies (customers, competitors, former employees, other investors). Tiger showed up knowing
  the company better than the founder knew it. Primary co-founded Altus to democratize this: institutional
  research on private companies (Harvey, Lora, 11 Labs, plus smaller startups) available to any investor.
  Pitchbook: acquired at $30M ARR, now does $700M ARR + $400M FCF. The VC research market is large and
  growing. *(Emerging [[entrepreneurship-operator-playbook]] case: research-as-infrastructure for an
  opaque market.)*
- **Steve Jobs priority framework** (via Kevin Ryan / DoubleClick): new hire lists 30 priorities → boss
  tears off all but the top 2. "Focus on these two things, forget everything else." Operationalized at
  Primary: every investor writes an annual strategic plan — portfolio review, market mapping, competitive
  landscape, target founders, target sectors. Measurable KPIs (deals seen, win rate). "You can't do strategic
  thinking well unless you can measure the results."

## 13 · Anti-consensus investing and the "messy middle" (Lerer Hippeau)
- **Ben Lerer's frame:** "Venture capital should exist to take risks that most people don't want to take. A
  lot of VC right now is taking risks that everyone wants to take. That's not risk — that's consensus
  investing. Consensus investing means you're investing in things that are highly competitive, which means
  prices are super high. And investing in venture at super high prices is a really shitty business model."
- **"The fun of venture"** = be in the generational company that people don't expect, at a price where "when
  you're right, you're *really really really* right." Non-consensus + right = outsized returns. Consensus +
  right = mediocre returns (price paid too high). *(↔ Howard Marks: "you can't do the same thing as everyone
  else and expect to outperform." See [[canon-bezos-shareholder-letters]] contrarian bets.)*
- **The messy middle:** "a lot of money in the ground that's going to zero." Not Open AI / Anthropic money,
  but the middle tier — companies that raise at higher prices early → "appear more successful" → get marked
  up faster. But DPI is "really really really really hard." Tiger/SoftBank in '21: some great companies, but
  that money also "destroyed a lot of companies." In ZIRP bubble, Series C failure rates looked more like
  seed failure rates — "that screws with the market."
- **AI market specifically:** "we have top-tipped the market in this particular moment of AI." Not bearish
  on Anthropic/AI long-term, but the fervor of daily/weekly mega-rounds into productless, revenue-less neo
  labs is "on the backside." Next phase: prove it. Application layer is where value accrues, and "that's
  New York time."
- **The 3-fund hump:** every 3 funds, you must demonstrate DPI to raise the next 3. Funds that appear to be
  the "best thing since sliced bread" often haven't driven much DPI because converting paper returns to
  real exits is extraordinarily hard. *(Critical check on TVPI-as-vanity-metric.)*
- **LH's portfolio examples of non-consensus wins:**
  - *Augmoto:* camera name badges tracking retail inventory in real time — "graveyard of companies" tried
    to sell hardware+software to supermarkets; Sand Hill Road sees the deck and says "next." But
    exponentially more demand than supply now.
  - *Caladan:* desktop bioreactors from ground zero — multi-year pre-revenue, large incumbents with
    long-term relationships. "Years later — yep, they did it."
  The pattern: hard markets + non-obvious bets + time = outsized returns when it works.

## 14 · The New York ecosystem thesis
- **Nihal (Eniac):** "New York is by far the most ambitious place on the planet." Not concentrated in one
  industry like SF — density of ambition across law, medicine, modeling, real estate, politics, activism,
  entrepreneurship. "Every day you can fill your calendar with 12 meetings of people better than you."
  NYC = horizontal ambition + diversity of experience. SF = concentrated AI depth. "For people looking to
  build in horizontal environments and have diversity in their lives, New York's the place."
- **Ben Lerer (LH):** "If New York sets its sights on anything, it figures it out. Maybe the greatest city
  in the world, maybe in the history of the world." When LH started, "everybody who was doing VC could fit
  in this room." Any fund deploying money in NYC saw everything — no competition. Now NYC is huge, but still
  produces bigger and bigger outcomes.
- **Ben Sun (Primary):** Tumblr was the biggest NYC exit in 2015. By 2020–22: 50+ NYC tech IPOs (Datadog,
  MongoDB, Oscar, Peloton, Warby Parker), 150 private unicorns — **more unicorns than the next 10 markets
  combined** (LA + Chicago + Miami's 6 unicorns + etc.). NYC became the #2 global tech market in a few years.
- **AI application layer = NYC's next advantage:** foundation models and infrastructure → SF. Application
  layer (B2C + B2B) → NYC. 9M+ consumers in the tri-state area for consumer apps. Highest concentration of
  Fortune 500 HQs for enterprise sales ("Jamie Diamond comes to work every day and takes meetings"). AI-
  native companies "don't need hundreds of folks — you need maybe dozens" → NYC's lifestyle + talent
  magnet advantage strengthens.
- **Recruiting advantage:** Traversal (Columbia professor, backed by Sequoia + Kleiner) says NYC is the best
  place to recruit — less competition for engineers, and "every young person wants to live in New York."
- **Charles (Rain):** SF still #1 for tech broadly, #1 for AI. But SF has "monoculture / group think" — if
  you're building for the rest of the world, that's a disadvantage. NYC = center of finance + global
  diversity. "Third wave of NYC startups — there's now enough ecosystem to build successfully here."

## 15 · Second-time founder mechanics and the idea maze (Rain + DRF)

### Rain (Charles)
- **First company → Intuit acquisition:** Stanford CS 2012, saved $50K at Microsoft ("if I save 50K, I could
  squeeze that for a year"), quit. Built contractor management tools, sold to Intuit earlier than expected.
  Stayed 4.5 years for the learning (beyond the 3-year vest). "Training ground for shipping products and
  leading teams."
- **The idea maze in practice:** "Pick a direction and just make progress. Don't get stuck figuring out where
  the end of the maze is." Worked on 5 different product ideas. World changed (COVID) → ideas changed.
  Eventually found co-founder Farooq through Deck Founder Fellowship. Built SignInWire (fundraising doc +
  payment tool) as a microsaas — "not meant to be venture scale, we just wanted to work together and build
  something." Through building it, discovered the real problem: broken financial rails.
- **SignInWire → Rain pivot:** added USDC to SignInWire → investor in Singapore sent funds on Christmas Day
  when banks were closed → "aha moment: this is 100x better." But couldn't spend USDC anywhere → the
  acceptance/interoperability layer became the real business. Led to Visa principal membership (multi-year,
  "a lot of chewing glass") → world's first 24/7 stable coin settlement → cut out banking dependencies
  entirely → lower cost of capital + reduced collateral.
- **Stable coin thesis:** 1% of M2 money supply is in stable coins today. "If you think money is going to go
  on-chain and become tokenized and move at the speed of the internet, then 1% is eventually going to be
  100%." Timeline uncertain (5/10/20 years) but monotonically increasing.
- **Three rounds in 12 calendar months:** "You kind of have to will it into existence." Went to market in
  2024 "a little bit prematurely" — had product velocity but too-early revenue for investors to appreciate.
  Ground out 6 months with a smaller team, proved the revenue, and then "without even trying to fundraise,
  people were knocking on our door." "No amount of PowerPoint wizardry is going to help — the business has
  to speak for itself." *(↔ §4 — revenue velocity as the ultimate conviction driver.)*
- **Second-time founder psychology:** advantages = know the emotional roller coaster, willing to play the game
  again with that assumption. Traps = "you know all the bad things that are going to happen and you're
  scared to repeat the mistakes" → over-index away from hard things. Consumer was brutal → flee to B2B
  without knowing how to sell to enterprises. "Starting a startup is about running through walls — you
  shouldn't be avoiding those moments."
- **HC pattern: 6 investments into second-time founders in 2025.** Most of them focused on **distribution**
  the second time around — not just brute-force selling but strategic channel leverage (Rain + Visa is the
  paradigm case).
- **Culture at scale (20→120+ in 12 months):** 25 direct reports, inspired by Jensen. Screen for autonomy
  ("go figure it out"), low ego, no empire-building, high bias toward action. "Don't get stuck in figuring
  out task-level direction — we set aggressive goals that may not be achievable without AI."

### Dorm Room Fund (pre-seed student investing)
- **Structure:** Founded 2012 by Josh Koppelman / First Round Capital. 60 student partners, 4 teams of 15,
  **3% acceptance rate** (harder to get into than some VC firms). Went national in 2020. 500+ alumni, **8
  unicorns** (including second check into Cursor).
- **Information asymmetry edge:** students can evaluate student founders better than any institutional VC.
  They see founders in class, in dorms, on campus — ground truth that no Zoom call or reference check
  replicates. Pre-seed = maximum information asymmetry advantage. *(↔ [[canon-jensen-meckling-agency-theory]]
  — information asymmetry creates alpha in private markets.)*
- **"No chumps" rule:** don't invest in people who cut corners or lack integrity, regardless of traction.
  Character is the one un-coachable founder attribute.
- **Sneaker reselling as founder signal** (also noted in Brandon's intangibles framework): founders who ran
  reselling businesses as teenagers demonstrate marketplace intuition, hustle, and commercial instinct before
  they've built anything "official." Pattern appears across Cursor, MerchCore, and multiple DRF-backed
  founders.
- **DPI focus:** student fund emphasizes realized returns, not paper markups. Aligns with Beezer's LP
  perspective (§10) — "the last few years were an aberration."

## Updated founder-side takeaways (Batch 2 additions)
10. **Will the business into existence through revenue velocity** — Rain's three rounds in 12 months happened
    *after* proving revenue, not before. "No amount of storytelling or PowerPoint wizardry is going to help"
    once you pass the seed stage. The business must speak for itself. *(Extends takeaway #5.)*
11. **Choose distribution over brute force the second time** — HC's pattern across 6 second-time-founder bets
    in 2025: all focused on leveraging existing distribution (Rain + Visa, not building merchant acceptance
    from scratch). Think about who has already solved the distribution problem and how to partner with them.
12. **Prepare for the LP lens if you're raising a fund** — LPs filter on "can this be 3x–5x net?" Seed
    aspirants must model portfolio outcomes (not just pick companies). Manage others' money first (scout,
    AngelList, rolling fund). Get audited. Have a 15–20-year commitment horizon. Send a Loom video over your
    deck.
13. **Your intangibles are leverage — identify and productize them** — Brandon's 4th characteristic. What can
    your team do that nobody else can? Sneaker reselling, chess championships, domain-specific pedigree. Find
    the 10x–1000x unfair advantage and make it visible in the pitch.
14. **Reference checks > interviews** — VCs increasingly weight what former colleagues say over how a founder
    performs in a pitch meeting. Build a reputation that survives scrutiny when the investor calls your old
    boss, co-founder, or direct report.
15. **Be non-consensus and right** — Lerer Hippeau's frame: consensus investing at high prices is "a really
    shitty business model." The outsized returns come from companies people don't expect, at prices where
    being right makes you *really* right. If every VC wants in, the price already reflects consensus — and
    the upside is compressed.

## Cross-cutting additions (Batch 2)
- **The LP-GP-founder triple alignment:** Beezer's LP perspective (§10) adds the third seat at the table.
  Founders see the VC; VCs see the LP; but most founders never consider *LP incentives* — preferred returns,
  DPI obsession, 15–20-year vehicle horizons, the 3-fund hump. Understanding all three seats is the full
  capital-allocation picture. *(↔ [[canon-jensen-meckling-agency-theory]] — nested principal-agent chains.)*
- **AI as the new leverage layer:** Naval's leverage taxonomy (§6) was capital, labor, code, media. AI is now
  a fifth: Eniac's "army of agents" for deal processing, Primary's AI sourcing/customer-finding tools,
  Rain's AI-native flat org. The firms that build proprietary AI workflows earliest will compound an
  operational advantage that compounds like brand — hard to replicate, easy to extend. *(↔
  [[canon-bezos-shareholder-letters]] "build on things that won't change" — the underlying principle is
  information compression, which AI accelerates.)*
- **NYC as the application-layer thesis:** a cross-source consensus (Eniac, LH, Primary, Rain) that SF owns
  foundation models but NYC owns the application layer — consumer (9M+ density) and enterprise (Fortune 500
  HQ concentration). AI-native companies need dozens, not hundreds → NYC lifestyle + talent magnet advantage
  strengthens. This is a *geographic portfolio construction* insight for both LPs and founders choosing where
  to build.
- **The "industrialized seed" paradigm shift:** Primary's $1.65B seed fund, Lerer Hippeau's 9th fund at
  $1.4B+ AUM, the US seed market at $20B+ — seed is no longer a cottage industry. The implication: seed-
  stage founders now face institutional-grade investors with AI-powered sourcing, professional win processes
  ("show don't tell" with custom apps), and portfolio support at a level previously reserved for Series A+.
  The bar has risen; founders should calibrate expectations accordingly.
- **DPI as the ultimate truth:** repeated across Beezer (LP view), Ben Lerer (3-fund hump), Henri (markups
  ≠ returns), and the Dorm Room Fund (DPI focus). Paper returns are "leading indicators that can evaporate
  70%." The entire LP community is recalibrating from TVPI to DPI post-ZIRP. Founders should understand
  that their investors' fundraising ability — and therefore their own downstream-capital access — depends on
  *realized exits*, not markups.
