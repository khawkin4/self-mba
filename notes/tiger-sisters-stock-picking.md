# How a Hedge Fund Manager Picks Stocks — Imran Khan (Tiger Sisters)

> Source: Tiger Sisters podcast episode "How a Hedge Fund Manager Picks Stocks (The Rules He Uses)" (YouTube `AHOKAM47sS0`), with Imran Khan — founder of Prometheus Asset Management (hedge fund deploying hundreds of millions), former Chief Strategy Officer at Snap, ex-Wall Street analyst who worked the Alibaba and Snap IPOs. Pulled 2026-06-29. Summarized in own words + brief attributed quotes — not reproductions. What it adds over the canon: a working practitioner's *ordered* decision sequence (understand → ask why → fact-check → valuation last), plus concrete AI-era tooling tactics (year-over-year filing diffs, sentiment scraping) that the value-investing classics predate. Maps to Course 02.

## The core mental model: investing is pricing risk under uncertainty

- **"Do you understand what you're investing in? Because if you don't understand, you don't understand the risk."** This is Khan's first filter and the spine of everything else. He explicitly maintains a **circle of competence**: there are "many sectors, many things I don't even touch." Sitting out is a legitimate move, not a failure.
- **Investing IS risk-pricing.** Buying anything = making a prediction about the future, and "by default, predicting future is speculative." So the relevant question is never "is this safe?" but "is the risk correctly *priced* at this price?" This reframes valuation as a measurement of how much risk/optimism the market has already baked in — see [[unit-06-whats-a-business-worth]].
- **All markets are speculative — always have been.** Khan rejects the "retail made markets reckless" narrative: 2008 was "sophisticated" professionals YOLO-ing credit default swaps and nearly taking down the US economy; nobody called *that* gambling because the players wore the elite badge. Speculation is intrinsic to forecasting, not a feature of the new crowd. Useful inoculation against status-based reasoning — the credentialed consensus is still a consensus ([[canon-kahneman-thinking-fast-and-slow]] on authority/halo bias).

## Rule 1 — Understand the business before anything else

- **"Try to learn about what you're investing in. Don't listen to somebody else, be educated about it."** First formula, stated first for a reason.
- **Democratization of knowledge is the enabling shift.** 30 years ago edge came from access — private management meetings, invite-only conferences, relationships you could phone for color. **Reg FD** (companies must disclose material info to everyone simultaneously) plus the internet plus social media collapsed that access moat. Now retail and pros read the *same* information; AI commoditizes it further.
- **The implication is a duty, not a gift:** because everyone has the same inputs, "everybody should have their own ideas and have to make the decision." Edge migrates from *access* to *interpretation* — what you do with commodity information ([[canon-mauboussin-success-equation]]: when the inputs are public, alpha comes from a better process, not better data).
- **Social media as a primary-research surface.** Old way: go to a user conference, talk to maybe five hand-picked customers. New way: on X / Reddit you hear from actual customers *and* the people who hated the product — "a much more holistic view." But raw and adversarial, so it needs filtering (Rule 2).

## Rule 2 — Read the source documents (and read the footnotes)

- **Primary sources, in order of trust:** the company's own filings and investor relations. Khan singles out the **S-1** (IPO registration), **10-K** (annual report), and **10-Q** (quarterly report). "Boring legal documents... companies spend hundreds of hours putting these together," and "legally, they have to be accurate, otherwise we have a bigger problem."
- **The edge is in the tedium.** Most people won't read filings; the ones who do gain a real differential. The footnotes and final line items are where the signal hides — Khan warns that "things are hidden on the final line item that ChatGPT or AI is not good enough to figure out." Read the prose for **mission, vision, where they want to go, and — if the stock is troubled — how they plan to get out of the hole.**
- **Triangulate social media.** When something looks questionable, "I read the comments," then verify against other sources. Treat no single source as authoritative.
- **13F caveat — the pros herd too.** Quarterly **13F** filings disclose what every major hedge fund owns, and "there's a huge overlap of similar stock they own." Group-think is not a retail disease; copying the smart-money consensus is copying a crowd. Reinforces that following beats following ([[canon-munger-poor-charlies-almanack]]: incentive-caused and social-proof bias inside the institutions themselves).

## Rule 3 — Be curious; ask "why" five times

- **"If you're not curious, you're never going to be a good investor."** The operational form: don't ask one question, ask ten — "why, why, why, why." Keep digging until the picture resolves.
- **Default skepticism:** "Don't believe anything you read in social media — ask why." Curiosity is the engine that turns commodity information into a differentiated thesis. This is the iterative-questioning discipline of good forecasters ([[canon-tetlock-superforecasting]]: actively seeking disconfirmation rather than confirming a prior).

## Rule 4 — Apply critical thinking; respect the consensus-trap

- **The signature line:** *"The market likes to make a fool of the greatest number of people."* Therefore anything that is **consensus** deserves the most suspicion, because if everyone already believes it, **the risk is probably not priced in.**
- **"When everybody tells you something, that is the time to be the most fearful that something could go wrong."** Maximum-agreement = maximum-fragility, the exact zone where a surprise produces "the sharpest move" in any asset (stocks, bonds, art, real estate). This is precisely the hidden-tail-risk-in-the-consensus intuition of [[canon-taleb-black-swan]], and the disaster-myopia behavior 2008 exemplified.
- **But contrarianism is NOT the prescription.** Crucial nuance: "Contrarian for the sake of being contrarian is not the right approach." Consensus "can be right for 1, 2, 3, 4 years" before it breaks — *The Big Short*'s Michael Burry was right but "wrong for a very long period of time," same as anyone short Japanese bonds. **Being early is indistinguishable from being wrong, and you can be carried out before the call pays.**
- **The resolution: be fearful at consensus, not automatically opposed to it.** Distinguish (a) "the consensus is wrong" — a great, high-return buy — from (b) "the consensus is right, possibly for years." That distinction is the work. *"Contrarian with the right timing."*

## Rule 5 — Never buy a stock just because it's cheap (valuation comes LAST)

- **"Never buy a stock because it's cheap" — his stated #1 career mistake.** A low P/E is not an edge: "anybody can do a P/E calculation," so buying on cheapness alone is "not doing anything incremental." Price already reflects everyone else's arithmetic.
- **A cheap stock is a question, not an answer:** "The stock is cheap because clearly the market thinks something is going wrong." So ask **why is it cheap? what is the fear?** — then do the work to judge whether that fear is true, whether it will actually happen, and whether it's consensus (loop back to Rule 4).
- **Symmetry on the short side:** never short a stock merely because it's expensive — "anybody can do a P/E calculation. If a stock is very expensive, the market is telling you something." Expensiveness encodes optimism the same way cheapness encodes fear.
- **Valuation is the LAST stop, and it's a *diagnostic*, not a trigger.** "Valuation should be the last stop, because valuation tells you how much risk is priced into the stock." A great business at "an incredible multiple" means "all the good news is most likely priced in," so "any kind of mistake will get hit hard." Valuation measures the gap between expectations and reality — the heart of expectations investing ([[unit-06-whats-a-business-worth]], [[canon-mauboussin-success-equation]]).
- **This is the margin-of-safety logic inverted into a process:** cheapness without an understood, mispriced fear is a value trap; the safety comes from understanding *why* the discount exists and judging it wrong ([[canon-klarman-margin-of-safety]]).

## Rule 6 — Never act on someone else's conviction

- **"Never buy a stock listening to someone else. Do your own work."** Stated as his second big mistake — the failure mode of triggering "too early without understanding everything" because a convincing banker pitched it. "Those never work out."
- **Even a correct tip is unusable, because the tipper's parameters aren't yours:** "Even if that person has a perfect crystal ball, they might have a different volatility pain threshold, a different time horizon." A call is only actionable inside the caller's risk profile and holding period — which you don't share. The position has to fit *your* pain tolerance and horizon (see pain-tolerance section).

## The trust factor — the variable that drives the multiple

- **"When people give you their money, they give you their trust. The entire US capital market is built on trust."** Disclosures aren't line-by-line policed by the SEC; the system runs on the assumption that filings are accurate, nothing material is omitted, and auditors did their job. You are trusting many parties at once when you buy equity.
- **Trust is the multiple.** "If people can trust the business, trust the predictability of the business, they're willing to pay more — the multiple will expand." Conversely, **a broken trust is a structural reason a stock is cheap** — so when something looks abnormally cheap, ask not just "what's the fear?" but specifically "did management or the business break trust?" Trust/predictability is a first-class valuation input, not soft stuff.
- **Macro aside (why this matters):** Khan credits trustworthy capital markets as a core US advantage — they funded ventures others wouldn't, including 1990s–2010s Chinese tech (Alibaba, Tencent) that domestic Chinese markets wouldn't back, and they tolerate honest failure ("America is okay with failure, as long as you put in the right effort, *as long as you are trusted*"). Break trust and "things fall apart."

## AI-era tooling tactics (concrete, do-this-today)

- **Summarize, but don't *only* summarize.** You can drop a 10-K into ChatGPT for a fast summary, "but I also encourage you to still read the documents" — the model misses the final-line-item / footnote signal.
- **Diff filings year-over-year and quarter-over-quarter — Khan's "favorite thing."** Companies spend hundreds of hours wording these documents, so any change in language is deliberate and worth noticing. Workflow: read the *first* document fully to build a baseline, then **every subsequent year/quarter just compare to the prior version to pick up the incremental changes** — far cheaper than re-reading from scratch.
  - **Watch modal/word shifts:** a change from **"may" to "will"** (e.g. "this may happen" → "this will happen") is a material tone shift. Also watch for **newly dropped or removed data points / disclosures.**
  - **Mechanics:** upload both documents (or paste Word docs) and ask the model to compare; AI just does it faster than eyeballing.
- **Sentiment tracking — quantify the consensus directly.** Pull commentary from Reddit, X, Substack, and research; feed it to the model and ask **how sentiment is trending (positive vs. negative) and how it has changed versus the prior period.** Practical recipe Khan endorses: scrape a company's Reddit page / comments → paste into the model → "what is the sentiment and how has it changed from last year?"
  - **Why it works:** *"The stock market is a voting machine"* (Buffett) in the short run — measuring which way sentiment is moving is a direct read on consensus, which feeds Rule 4. (Forecasting consensus drift ≈ [[canon-tetlock-superforecasting]].)
- **AI as a fast on-ramp to a new domain, then go primary.** Khan learned wine "from zero" via ChatGPT for the basics, *then* "did the primary research." Same pattern for a new sector: use AI for baseline literacy, never as the final word.
- **Direction of travel:** Khan wants to *build tools* to automate the diff + sentiment pipeline — "we're not there yet, but yes." A signal that the durable edge is in the *workflow*, not the one-off prompt.

## How to start (for the intimidated beginner)

- **Start small — absurdly small if needed.** "If you have $100 and you're intimidated, invest $5." Fractional shares (Robinhood et al.) make this real; barriers to entry have "come down dramatically" over 30 years.
- **Marathon framing:** runners don't run 26 miles on day one; they build up. Take baby steps; "don't try to put all your money in investing in one day."
- **Two things small bets teach you that nothing else can:**
  1. **Pattern recognition** (see below) — you have to log reps to acquire it.
  2. **Your own pain tolerance** — you only learn it by having real money at risk.
- **Only do it if you're genuinely curious.** Repeated caveat: if investing doesn't excite you, *don't* — hire a professional instead. "Intimidation you can overcome; lack of curiosity you can't." Don't quit something you love out of fear, but don't force something that bores you.
- **Start the next generation early** — even with fake money. "The longer they do it, the better they get." Financial discipline matters as much as picking: "it's much easier to ruin your assets than to earn them. You can lose a lot of money very fast."

## Pattern recognition — why investing compounds with time

- **"Investing is one of the many things in life where the longer you do it, the better you get."** Edge accrues through cycles: "Oh, I've seen this story 5 years ago... a similar situation 10 years ago." You start **connecting the dots** across episodes.
- **Cycle exposure is the moat that can't be shortcut.** Khan contrasts himself (never heard of Wall Street until junior year of college) with a peer from an East-Coast investing family who started at a hedge fund right out of school and so had "15–20 years of experience, been through the cycles" much earlier. The asset is *time through regimes*, not raw IQ.
- **It's transferable.** Pattern recognition built in one domain ports to others — the Tiger Sisters credit cross-industry pattern transfer for building their business fast. A meta-skill, which is exactly why deliberate rep-logging early pays off.

## Pain tolerance — the trait that gates long-term success

- **"Nobody I know ever became very successful for a long period of time who doesn't have very strong pain tolerance."** True across investing, athletics, and founding companies.
- **Because losing is structural:** "If you invest money, you will lose money. And that's painful." The question isn't whether you'll feel pain but whether you can *function* through it.
- **The failure mode is concrete and human:** Khan has watched hedge fund managers undone by losses — "can't talk... impacts their life, impacts the relationship with their wife... a complete mess." His verdict: **"These people should never come to investing"** — for them it's a miserable life.
- **Pain compounds mistakes:** "When you're in pain and you can't take the pain, you make mistakes." This is loss-aversion under stress wrecking judgment ([[canon-kahneman-thinking-fast-and-slow]]) — the System-2 process collapses exactly when you need it.
- **The actionable bridge:** discover your pain threshold *with small experiments* before you scale up. Position sizing should respect *your* tolerance and horizon, which is also why other people's tips don't fit you (Rule 6).

## Quick-reference: the decision sequence in order

1. **Understand the business** — circle of competence; sit out what you can't grasp.
2. **Read the primary documents** — S-1 / 10-K / 10-Q, including footnotes and final line items.
3. **Ask "why" relentlessly** — curiosity, default skepticism, triangulate sources.
4. **Stress-test the consensus** — "the market likes to make a fool of the greatest number"; be *fearful* at consensus, not reflexively contrarian; mind the timing.
5. **Check the trust factor** — is predictability / management integrity intact? It drives the multiple.
6. **Value it LAST** — valuation measures how much risk/optimism is already priced; cheap ≠ buy, expensive ≠ short.
7. **Decide for yourself, sized to your own pain tolerance and horizon.**
