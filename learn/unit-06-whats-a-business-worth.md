# Unit 06 — What's a Business Worth?

> **For a beginner.** New idea: a business is worth the **cash it will generate in the future** —
> brought back to what that future cash is worth **today.** This is the intuition behind every valuation.

## Plain words first (read once)
- **Revenue / profit** — total sales (money in from customers) / what's left after all costs (Unit 01).
- **Value / worth** — what someone would rationally pay to own the business.
- **Future cash flows** — the cash the business is expected to throw off in the years ahead.
- **Present value** — what a future dollar is worth **today** (always less than the dollar itself).
- **Discount** — to shrink a future amount down to its present value (because waiting and risk cost you).
- **Market value (market cap)** — what investors are collectively pricing a public company at right now.

## ① Material — what we're learning
Units 01–05 asked "is it healthy / is its growth good?" Now: **what is the whole business worth?** The
core idea is simple and powerful: a company is a **machine that produces cash for years**, and its worth
is **all that future cash, with later years counted for less.**

## ② Understanding — the idea in plain language
Quick gut check: would you rather have **$100 today** or **$100 in five years?** Everyone picks today —
you could use it or invest it, and five years is a long time for things to go wrong. So **a dollar in the
future is worth less than a dollar now.** Shrinking future money to its today-value is called
**discounting.**

Now picture a business as a machine that prints, say, **$10 a year forever**. Its worth is *all* those
future $10s added up — but each year's $10 is discounted more the farther out it sits. Two things drive
the answer:
1. **How much** cash it will produce (more cash → worth more).
2. **How certain and how soon** that cash is (safer, sooner cash → worth more; risky, distant cash →
   worth less).

That's it. Everything fancy in valuation is just careful bookkeeping on those two ideas.

## ③ Example — why two similar-size companies can be worth wildly different amounts
Recall two real companies from earlier units, with *roughly comparable revenue* in recent years:
- **Mastercard** — keeps ~35–45¢ of profit on every sales dollar, year after year (Unit 07), and grows.
  It's a dependable **future-cash machine**, so investors value it very highly.
- **Peloton** — similar-ballpark revenue, but it **burns cash and has never made a profit** (Unit 05).
  Its future cash is doubtful, so it's worth far less.

The proof that value = *expected future cash*, not today's sales: at its 2020–21 peak, the market valued
Peloton in the tens of billions on the *story* of huge future profits. When that story died — gyms
reopened, losses exploded — its market value collapsed **roughly 90%+**, even though it still had
billions in revenue. **The revenue barely moved; the *belief about future cash* did.** Price follows
expected future cash, not the headline sales number.

## ④ Application — your turn
**A. Present value (no computer needed).** Which is worth more *today*: $1,000 paid to you now, or
$1,000 paid in 4 years — and why in one phrase? *(Now — future money is discounted; you could use or
invest it, and waiting carries risk.)*

**B. Reason it out.** Two companies have identical revenue this year. One reliably profits and grows;
the other loses money. Which should be worth more, and why? *(The profitable, growing one — it will
produce more, safer future cash; value follows expected future cash, not current revenue.)*
