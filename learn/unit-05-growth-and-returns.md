# Unit 05 — Growth & Returns: why growth isn't always good

> **For a beginner.** Starts Arc B (value & growth). New idea: growth only helps if it's **profitable
> and durable** — fast growth that loses money or rides a fad destroys value instead of building it.

## Plain words first (read once)
- **Growth** — sales (revenue) getting bigger over time.
- **Durable** — lasting; *not* a one-time spike that fades.
- **Cash burn** — spending more cash than you bring in (the opposite of generating cash).
- **Compound** — stack gains on gains: profits reinvested that go on to earn still more profits.
- **Fiscal year** — a company's official accounting year; some don't end in December (Peloton's ends
  June 30). · **$X B** = X billion. · A **minus sign** on a money figure (−$2.8B) means a **loss**.
- **Revenue / profit** — money in from customers / what's left after all costs (Units 01–02).

## ① Material — what we're learning
Headlines love "revenue grew 120%!" But growth is a *means*, not the goal. The real questions: **does the
growth make a profit, and will it last?** Growth that loses money just loses money *faster*; growth built
on a temporary craze reverses when the craze ends.

## ② Understanding — the idea in plain language
Your lemonade stand does well, so you open a **second stand**. That's growth. Good or bad?
- If each new stand **makes money** after its costs → growing is great; you compound.
- If each new stand **loses money** → growing faster just **loses money faster.** Growth amplifies
  whatever the business already is.
- And if sales spiked only because of a **one-week heat wave**, that growth isn't **durable** — when the
  weather breaks, sales fall back. A spike is not a trend.

The trap: revenue going up *looks* like success, so people fund "growth" that never earns a profit.

## ③ Example — Peloton (real figures; the COVID home-fitness boom and bust)
Peloton sells connected exercise bikes. When COVID shut gyms (2020–21), demand exploded — then reversed.

| Fiscal year (ends June) | Revenue | Profit (net income) | What was happening |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2020 | $1.83 B | −$0.07 B | COVID hits; home-fitness demand surges. |
| **2021** | **$4.02 B** | −$0.19 B | **Revenue +120%** in one year — the boom's peak. *Still losing money.* |
| **2022** | $3.58 B | **−$2.83 B** | Gyms reopen; growth **reverses**; a **$2.8B loss**. |
| 2023–25 | falling to $2.49 B | still losing money every year | The spike faded; never turned a profit. |

Read it:
- **The growth was real but not good.** Revenue *more than doubled* in 2021 (4.02 ÷ 1.83 ≈ 2.2× = **+120%**)
  — a number that thrilled headlines. But Peloton **never made a profit**, in the boom *or* after.
- **It wasn't durable.** The surge rode a one-time event (locked-down customers). When gyms reopened,
  revenue **fell** and the losses ballooned to **−$2.83B** in 2022.
- **The lesson:** explosive revenue growth that (a) never earns a profit and (b) depends on a temporary
  craze isn't value being created — it's a **cash bonfire** dressed up as success. Always ask of growth:
  *is it profitable, and will it last?*
