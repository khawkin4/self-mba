# Unit 07 — The Landscape: Why Some Businesses Are Just Better (Moats)

> **For a beginner.** Arc C (the competitive landscape). New idea: some businesses stay highly
> profitable for *years* because they have a **moat** — a durable advantage rivals can't easily copy.
> Others have their profits competed away. This is your bridge to strategy.

## Plain words first (read once)
- **Margin** — profit kept per dollar of sales (Unit 01); a high, *steady* margin is a clue to a moat.
- **Competitive advantage** — a reason a company beats rivals.
- **Moat** — a *durable* competitive advantage: something hard for competitors to copy, that protects
  profits over time (named after the water-filled ditch protecting a castle).
- **Commodity business** — one selling something interchangeable, where rivals compete mainly on price,
  so margins stay thin.
- **Network effect** — the product gets more valuable as more people use it (one kind of moat).

## ① Material — what we're learning
Why do some companies earn fat profits for decades while others scrape by? The answer is the **moat**.
In a normal market, high profits attract competitors who copy you until the profit is competed away —
*unless* you have something they can't replicate. Spotting a moat (or its absence) is the first move of
strategy, and you can often see it **right in the margins.**

## ② Understanding — the idea in plain language
Your beach lemonade stand makes a great margin. What happens? Others see it and open stands next to you,
cutting prices, until nobody makes much — *unless* you have a **moat**:
- You hold the **only permit** to sell on that beach (rivals are locked out),
- or a **secret recipe** everyone prefers (they can't copy the product),
- or a **brand** people trust enough to pay more for,
- or you're the spot **everyone already meets at** (the more people come, the more people come — a
  network effect).

A moat shows up as a margin that **stays high year after year** instead of getting competed down. No
moat → a *commodity* → thin, fragile margins.

## ③ Example — Mastercard vs. the cruise lines (real figures)
**Mastercard** runs a global payment network. Look at its profit margin over time (net income ÷ revenue,
real filings):

| Year | Revenue | Profit | Net margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2009 | $5.10 B | $1.46 B | **~29%** |
| 2013 | $8.35 B | $3.12 B | **~37%** |
| …and it stayed in a **~30–45%** band for over a decade while revenue kept climbing. | | | |

Keeping **~35¢ of profit on every dollar, for 15+ years**, is extraordinary — and durable. The moat: a
**network** almost impossible to rebuild (more cardholders attract more merchants, which attract more
cardholders), plus trusted rails. Rivals can't simply copy it, so the profits survive.

Now contrast the **cruise lines / airlines** from Units 01–04: thin margins in good years, and
**catastrophic losses** the moment a shock hits (Royal Caribbean swung from a 17% margin to a −262% one
in 2020). They sell a fairly interchangeable service, carry huge fixed costs, and have **no moat** — so
their profits are thin and fragile. *Same economy, opposite durability — the difference is the moat.*

This is the doorway to the strategy canon: **`notes/canon/canon-helmer-7-powers`** (the seven kinds of
durable moat) and **`canon-porter-competitive-strategy`** (why industry structure sets profitability).
