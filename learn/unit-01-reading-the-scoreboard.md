# Unit 01 — Reading the Scoreboard: Revenue, Profit & Margin

> **For a complete beginner.** No prior business knowledge assumed. By the end you'll be able to look
> at a company and answer the first question that matters: *did it actually make money — and how much
> of each dollar did it keep?* The four pillars are labeled so you can see how this is taught.

---

## Plain words first (terms we use in passing — read once)
- **$X B** — X **b**illion dollars.
- **Net income** — just another name for **profit**: what's left after *all* costs ("net" = after everything).
- **Debt** — money the company **borrowed and must pay back**, usually with interest.
- **Fixed costs** — bills that **don't shrink when sales fall** (rent, loan payments, salaried crew).
- **Public company** — a company whose shares anyone can buy on a stock market; in exchange it must
  publish its finances. (A *private* company doesn't and can keep them secret.)
- **10-K** — the detailed **annual report** every U.S. public company must file with the government.
- **SEC / EDGAR** — the **SEC** is the U.S. agency that regulates public companies; **EDGAR** is its free public website where every filing lives.
- **Fiscal year** — a company's official 12-month accounting year (often, not always, Jan–Dec).
- **Second-order effect** — the **knock-on** consequence: what happens *next*, after the obvious first effect.

*(Revenue, profit, and margin are the lesson itself — taught below.)*

## ① Material — what we're learning, and where it lives
Every company keeps a scoreboard called the **income statement** (you'll also hear "P&L" — profit and
loss). It answers one question: over some period, *did the business make money?* It has three core
ideas, and that's all this unit covers:

- **Revenue** — all the money customers paid the company.
- **Costs** — everything it spent to earn that revenue.
- **Profit** (a.k.a. *net income*) — what's left: Revenue − Costs.

**Where it comes from:** every U.S. public company files this with the government every year, in a
document called a **10-K**, free at SEC EDGAR. We'll read a real one below.
*(Go deeper later: `lessons/core-01-accounting.html`, and the worked Apple example in `notes/01-accounting`.)*

## ② Understanding — the idea in plain language
Picture a **lemonade stand**.
- Customers pay you **$100** over the summer → that's **revenue**.
- Lemons, sugar, cups cost **$70** → those are **costs**.
- You keep **$30** → that's **profit**.

Three things a beginner must internalize:

**1. Revenue is not profit.** Revenue is the *top* line (money in the door). Profit is the *bottom*
line (what survives after costs). A company can have *huge* revenue and still lose money if its costs
are higher. People who only watch revenue get fooled constantly.

**2. Margin is the number that actually tells you something.** **Net margin = profit ÷ revenue** — how
many cents of each $1 of sales the company keeps. *(It comes out as a decimal; multiply by 100 to make
it a percent — e.g. 0.17 = 17%. "17%" and "17 cents of profit per dollar of sales" are the same thing.)*
> Stand A: $1,000,000 revenue, keeps $50,000 → 5% margin.
> Stand B: $200,000 revenue, keeps $80,000 → 40% margin.
> Stand B is the *better business*, even though Stand A's headline is 5× bigger. Margin > headline.

**3. Some costs don't shrink when sales fall.** The lemonade stand's lemons scale with sales — sell
nothing, buy no lemons. But if you signed a **year's rent** on the stand, that bill comes whether you
sell one cup or a thousand. Those are **fixed costs**, and they're why a bad year can turn *brutal*:
revenue falls, but the big bills stay. Hold that thought — the real example shows it vividly.

## ③ Example — real company, real event (the part a textbook can't do)
Meet **Royal Caribbean Cruises** — the cruise-ship company. Here are its *actual* numbers, pulled
from its SEC filings (all figures real, in USD):

| Fiscal year | Revenue | Profit (net income) | Net margin | What was happening |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **2019** | **$10.95 B** | **+$1.88 B** | **~17%** | A normal year — ships full, sailing. |
| **2020** | **$2.21 B** | **−$5.80 B** | **≈ −262%** | **COVID-19.** Cruising was ordered to *stop* (no-sail orders, ~March 2020). |
| 2022 | $8.84 B | −$2.16 B | **≈ −24%** | Sailing resumed; recovering — but not all the way. |

Read what this teaches, in order:

- **2019 — the baseline** (the "normal" year to compare against). For every $1 of tickets, Royal
  Caribbean kept about **17¢** of profit (a 17% net margin). Notice revenue ($10.95B) and profit ($1.88B)
  are *very different numbers* — revenue ≠ profit, exactly as we said above.
- **2020 — the event hits.** The pandemic (cruising was literally ordered to halt) erased the customers.
  Revenue **collapsed ~80%**, from $10.95B to $2.21B. Here the real filing shows the *number* and the
  real event explains the *why* — that pairing is the whole point of using a real company.
- **A margin can go below zero — far below.** 2020's net margin was **≈ −262%**: −$5.80B loss ÷ $2.21B
  revenue ≈ −2.62. How do you "lose 262% of sales"? Because **costs ran far past revenue** — costs must
  have been about **$8B** (the $2.2B taken in *plus* the $5.8B lost), so the loss is bigger than
  everything you sold. Margin is just
  profit ÷ revenue; when profit is a big negative, the percentage can exceed 100%.
- **The brutal part — fixed costs.** That loss was a **~$7.7B swing** from 2019's profit (the gap from a
  +$1.88B profit to a −$5.80B loss is the two added together: 1.88 + 5.80 ≈ 7.7). Why so much worse than
  the 80% revenue drop? Because ships, crews, and debt payments (money borrowed for the ships) **don't
  disappear when the boats stop** — those fixed costs kept billing, turning a revenue drop into a
  catastrophic loss. *This* is why the cruise lines nearly didn't survive.
- **2022 — the second-order lesson.** Revenue recovered to $8.84B (~81% of 2019) — but they **still lost
  money** (−$2.16B). Take-away a beginner should carry forever: **"sales came back" is not the same as
  "the business is healthy again."** Profit lags revenue. Watch the bottom line, not just the top.

> That's the whole differentiator: you didn't learn "margin" from a definition — you watched it happen
> to a real $10-billion company during an event you already understand.

## ④ Application — your turn (small and doable)
**A. Compute (2 min).** Royal Caribbean 2019: profit $1.88B on revenue $10.95B. What's the net margin?
*(Answer: 1.88 ÷ 10.95 ≈ 0.17, and 0.17 × 100 = **17%**.)*

**B. Explain in one sentence.** In 2020 Royal Caribbean still took in $2.2B of revenue but lost $5.8B.
*Why was the loss so much bigger than the revenue drop?*
*(You're right if you said: its big fixed costs — ships, crew, debt — kept billing even though sailings
stopped.)*

**C. One more margin (no computer needed).** Apple in a recent year earned about $97B of profit on
$383B of revenue. Estimate its net margin, and say whether Apple keeps more or less of each sales
dollar than Royal Caribbean did in 2019 (17%). *(≈ 97 ÷ 383 ≈ 0.25 = **25%** — Apple keeps more.)*

**D. Optional — pull it yourself (only if you have this project set up).** If you've got the repo
running, you can fetch any company's real numbers: `python3 _ingest/edgar.py facts AAPL`. No setup? Skip
it — you've already done the core skill in A–C.

### Check yourself
1. A company reports $5B in revenue and a $400M loss. Did it make money? *(No — profit is negative;
   revenue ≠ profit.)*
2. Company X: $50M profit on $1B revenue. Company Y: $50M profit on $200M revenue. Which is the
   stronger business? *(Y — 25% margin vs X's 5%.)*
3. Why can a company's revenue recover after a shock while it's *still* unprofitable? *(Costs —
   especially fixed ones, and debt taken on to survive — can stay high even as sales return.)*
