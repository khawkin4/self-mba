## 3. People & views

### David Solomon, Chairman & CEO (2019–2024 transcripts)

**Signature views**
- **One GS / client-centricity**: see the firm "through their eyes" (the client's), not through the org chart.
- **Plan-driven management** in a firm historically run as an adaptive partnership.
- **Durable, fee-based revenue** (AWM, alternatives).
- **Tech as core, capital as commodity.**
- **Performance earns latitude.**
- **Corporates should not set policy.**
- **"Balance" on the energy transition.**
- **Long-term over quarter-to-quarter.**

**How his views shift over time**
- **2019**: Build mode. Consumer is an "open field", Apple Card, transaction banking, wealth for "everyday Americans" via Marcus.
- **2022**: Still building. GreenSky's merchant network is bought to "generate assets in our digital consumer bank".
- **2023**: Partial retreat. Consumer provisions are named as a drag.
- **2024**: Explicit pivot. The core is "two principal businesses" and consumer is narrowed. Spin: innovation plus being "nimble".
- **On AI**: 2023, "early stage of testing". 2024, euphoria versus efficacy, a "narrow set of use cases", and data/regulatory control concerns.

### John Waldron, President & COO (2019, 2025)

**Signature views**
- **The COO role is internal, the President role external.**
- **De-siloing**: the org structure was "getting in the way of the ethos".
- **Disagree behind closed doors, then show unanimity.**
- **Long-horizon KPIs balanced with room to fail.**
- **Risk management as core competency**: risk functions have parity with revenue producers, mark-to-market culture, "third-party bid" discipline.
- **Durability as measurable.**
- **Technology as the "definitional success" of the firm**, which he admits is not his expertise.

**How his views shift over time**
- **2019**: The firm is learning and de-siloing, with a J-curve view of new businesses (Apple card, transaction banking).
- **2025**: The strategy is presented as settled and consistent, using three pillars: One GS for wallet share, durable differentiated businesses, and operating at scale. His leadership lesson is to communicate far more, through more channels.

## 4. Mission / identity statements (as stated)

- **Solomon 2019 — "cultural pillars"**: "our focus on clients, our focus on our people, the quality of our people, a real focus on excellence in everything we do, collaboration, work as a team". He adds that "no company can stay the same".
- **Solomon 2019 — the firm's building blocks**: "we have people, we have technology, we have capital". Capital is a commodity; the differentiator is marrying people and technology.
- **Solomon 2019 — client segmentation**: three principal client groups: governments and corporations, institutions, and individuals.
- **Solomon 2019 — identity shift**: from a "small private partnership" at the 1999 IPO to a "Fortune 50 company". This is why the firm now needs a plan, where it was historically "adaptive"/"nimble".
- **Solomon 2022 (The Hague) — business lines**: "a handful of principle [sic] businesses": investment banking, markets, asset management and wealth management, plus "a small digital consumer business that we're building".
- **Solomon 2024 — narrowed identity**: "two principal businesses where we have a significant right to win". These are Global Banking & Markets ("our preeminent investment banking business", "leading FICC and equity businesses") and Asset & Wealth Management.
- **Waldron 2019 — culture**: "fundamentally grounded in respect, lots of communication and a collaborative perspective".
- **Waldron 2025 — One GS**: an "operating philosophy and infrastructure" to "serve our clients holistically… not worry so much about how much money we're making along the way". Also: "Goldman Sachs is, fortunately, 156 years old"; risk management is the reason it has lasted.
- **Waldron 2025 — people**: "We don't have manufacturing facilities. Our manufacturing is our people."
- **Solomon 2022 — stakeholder hierarchy**: his responsibility when speaking publicly is "to our employees, to our clients, to our shareholders first and foremost in terms of our performance".

## 5. Tailwinds / headwinds / challenges

**Tailwinds**
- **Pandemic-era client activity (ADMITTED)**: 2021 had record IB and markets activity, record revenue and EPS. "the pandemic has created an economic structure that really benefited our clients and our business" (2022).
- **Durability shift (ADMITTED)**: >$10bn management fees on ~$54bn revenue. Markets revenue volatility fell from 24% to 6% standard deviation. Financing revenues are "stickier" (2025).
- **Pent-up refinancing and M&A (ADMITTED as a forecast)**: the maturity wall in 2026–27 forces companies to refinance. 18 months of "decade-low transaction volume" won't last. Sponsors are reactivating (2024).
- **Scale in asset management (ADMITTED)**: active managers need to be "broad, global, deep, multi product". NN IP adds European scale, distribution and ESG capabilities (2022).
- **Consolidation (INFERRED)**: "The strong are getting stronger." Mid-size M&A drove the record 2021.

**Headwinds**
- **Capital-markets-heavy mix (ADMITTED)**: Solomon (2023): "We have a mix of businesses that is very capital markets heavy" †. This amplified the 2022 downturn.
- **Balance-sheet-intensive asset management (ADMITTED)**: the ~$10bn revenue swing was the "biggest drag". The firm is "halfway through" the shift to a client-capital model (2023).
- **Consumer losses (ADMITTED)**: higher credit-loss provisions "as we've been scaling them" (2023). Ambitions then "narrowed materially" (2024).
- **Russia (ADMITTED)**: "We were an investor in Russia." Significant markdown in 2022.
- **Organisational Balkanization (ADMITTED)**: Waldron blames 10–15 years of post-crisis "defense" and says Bear Stearns "would never have been able to achieve" a One GS culture shift.
- **Geopolitics (ADMITTED)**:
  - US–China: Waldron 2019 calls it "far and away" the biggest implication; Solomon 2019 foresees a "two-sphere world".
  - Brexit: ~6,000 UK staff, "35 percent of our … business" in that region (2019).
  - Three geopolitical "hot spots" in 2024.
  - Tariffs in 2025.
- **Fiscal deficits / long-end rates (ADMITTED)**: "6 plus percent deficits" are "unsustainable"; the "bond market is starting to be heard" (Waldron 2025).
- **Cyber risk (ADMITTED)**: Solomon "worried about for a long time" (2023).
- **Regulation (ADMITTED, mild)**:
  - Tougher regulatory climate for large transformative M&A (2022).
  - AI must meet "regulatory standards" in a regulated industry (2024).
  - "structural and regulatory" change over the decade forced diversification (2019).
- **Tech-execution risk at the top (INFERRED)**: in both 2019 and 2025 Waldron says technology is not his strength. Yet in 2025 he names it the "definitional success" of the firm.
- **Talent/partner attrition narrative (ADMITTED as press narrative, disputed)**: Solomon says the press "over-interprets" the 2019 departures.

## 7. Market-structure links

- **Electronification (Solomon 2019)**: trading has moved from a "much more over-the-counter, bespoke, person-to-person business" toward business "done through machines and through platforms". His response: GS must "have the best platforms… and the best tools for our clients", while keeping its edge in "bespoke risk intermediation" and capital commitment.
- **Marquee** is named as the platform "getting more visibility" (2019).
- **Dealer balance sheet as differentiator**: Waldron (2025) ties lower volatility to higher wallet share plus financing (prime and FICC financing are implied, not named). Mark-to-market discipline means asking "What's the third-party bid for that asset?", which implicitly depends on market liquidity and venues.
- **Named venues and counterparties**: **none**. Tradeweb, MarketAxess, Trumid, ICE, CME, BNY and Digital Asset/Canton are absent. **Tokenization and stablecoins are absent.** The only "digital" mentions are crypto as a CEO talking point in 2022, Marcus/the digital consumer bank, and transaction banking.
- **Apple as platform partner**: Apple wanted to avoid the regulatory moat. GS supplied the bank charter and a greenfield stack with no legacy platform. This is a bank-as-a-service precursor and a useful case of distribution versus balance-sheet economics.
- **GreenSky**: bought as a merchant/point-of-sale distribution network. GS estimated 7–10 years to build an equivalent, against ~15 years for GreenSky.
- **Alternatives fundraising as a platform**: $180bn raised over 3 years (2023) and >$225bn over 3.5 years (2024). The pivot from GS's own balance sheet to client capital is the closest this bucket gets to **private credit**; the term itself never appears.

## 8. Surprises / note-worthy theses

1. **One GS began as a fix for "organizational calcification", not as a slogan.** Waldron (2019) admits the firm had become "Balkanized" after the crisis. Day-one memo, cross-divisional pilot, then the 2022 merger of IBD and Global Markets into GBM so it "could be benchmarked against" peers. By 2025 it is measured by **client rankings and wallet share, not revenue**. This makes a clean study arc: diagnosis (2019) → structure (2022) → metric (2025).
2. **Durability can be quantified.** Markets revenue standard deviation fell from 24% to 6%. Management fees of >$10bn on ~$54bn revenue. This shows how GS moved the narrative from "volatile trading house" to "durable franchise" with a specific statistic. Worth checking against 10-Ks, because the measurement window and method are not given.
3. **The consumer arc in the CEO's own words.** "Open field" plus "no legacy business" (2019). GreenSky's network bought to save 7–10 years (2022). Provisions as a drag (2023). "Narrowed materially" (2024). The self-criticism is framed as nimbleness ("if things aren't working, to adjust"); it is never framed as a strategic error. A good case on how leaders narrate a retreat.
4. **The most candid self-assessment is about the balance sheet, not consumer.** Solomon (2023) says the **biggest** drag in 2022 was ~$10bn less revenue from the on-balance-sheet asset-management book. Consumer, which drew the headlines, was smaller. This is the real motive for the "client capital / fee-based" AWM pivot.
5. **"Capital is a commodity" versus "bespoke risk intermediation".** Solomon in 2019 holds both views at once: balance sheet is not a differentiator in itself, but risk-taking judgment is. Waldron (2025) later credits *financing* (balance-sheet use) with stability. This tension is worth tracking across the corpus.
6. **Plan-driven management is new for GS.** Solomon is the "first CEO" to lay out a public strategy with KPIs. Waldron moved budgeting from a 1-year to a 3-year cycle. For a former private partnership known for being "nimble", this is a cultural shift from "adaptive" to "accountable to targets". It also exposed GS to public scorekeeping, for example the 2023 consumer pre-tax profitability goal.
