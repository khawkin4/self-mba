Let's say I take $1,000 and go to public.com. I can open a bond account where I invest my $1,000 in 10 investment-grade and high-yield corporate bonds. And that's just a fancy way of saying " loans taken out by companies." For simplicity, let's assume that all of these corporate bonds have a maturity of 5 years and a yield of 6.7% . Now I can invest my money, earn a 6.7% return every year, and make back my initial investment in 5 years if nothing changes. We all know that the Fed just started cutting rates, taking a 50 basis point step at the September meeting. So what happens if interest rates fall by 1%? I am now earning a 6.7% return on my bonds, while market rates are 5.7%, and we can do some simple calculations to estimate how much my bonds have increased in value. But first, I need a conversion factor to find out how much it costs to change the yield by one percentage point for my $1,000 5-year bond with a yield of 6.7%. We use a measure called the bond's modified duration, or DV01, which depends on the maturity, coupon, price, and optionality, if any. So, let's say the duration of my bond is 4.3. This means that for every basis point —or 1/100 of a percent— change in rates, I gain or lose 4.3 basis points on my investment. So for every $1,000 invested, I make or lose 43 cents.
