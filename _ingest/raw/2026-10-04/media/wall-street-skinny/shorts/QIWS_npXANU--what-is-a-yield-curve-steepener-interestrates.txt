# What is a yield curve steepener? #interestrates
# https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QIWS_npXANU | NA | NAs | 3200 views

We got this great question asking what a yield curve steepener is. It's a great example of a basic trade we see all the time in the fixed income market. So first of all, what is a yield curve? Imagine a graph with bond maturities along the x-axis and interest rates along the y- axis. On the lefth hand side, you have the shortest maturity bond, overnight rates, 3-month rates, 2-year rates, etc. And as you move further out the x-axis, we have longer maturities, 5, 10, 30, or even 50ear rates. In the past, we've talked about the time value of money. Lenders charge borrowers interest to compensate them for not having access to their money over the life of a loan. If I lend you money for two years, I'm getting my money back much sooner than if I lend it to you for 10. So, under normal circumstances, you'd expect interest rates for 2-year loans to be lower than interest rates for 10-year loans. The graph plotting out interest rates that correspond to various maturities along the x-axis is called the yield curve. And investors trade the difference between points on the yield curve all the time in what are called curve trade. Say you think that the Fed is going to start a cycle of easing monetary policy by lowering short-term interest rates. You might just go and buy two-year notes, which would benefit from the Fed cutting rates, causing yields to go down and prices to go up. But what if instead you want to express the more nuanced view that two-year rates are going to go down relative to 10-year rates? That is called a yield curve steepener. You're expressing a view on the shape of the yield curve changing. In order to put on a yield curve steepener in treasuries, you would buy the shorter maturity instrument, in this case the 2-year Treasury, and you would sell the longer maturity instrument, in this case the 10-year Treasury. If you duration weight your long twos and short 10's position, you're indifferent to whether or not the whole market rallies or sells off. All you care about is the slope of the yield curve changing. Once you have the trade on, you can make money in one of two ways. Maybe you're right about monetary policy. The Fed lowers rates and two-year treasuries rally more than 10. This is called a bull steepener when an increase in that spread between the two points on the curve is led by the front-end rallying. Or maybe the whole market sells off and 10ear rates sell off more than two-year rates. In that case, you'd still make money, but now the spread widening is driven by the sell-off in long-end rates. This is called a bare steepener. Either way, you're making money from the difference in those two points on the yield curve increasing. There's a variety of ways to express steepeners, but this is one of the most basic examples of the trick.
