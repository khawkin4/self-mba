Why do the markets look like this? The headlines just say tariffs. But let's explain what's really going on. Think about the reciprocal tariffs the US just announced on our trade partners. In our last two videos, we explained that this cost either gets absorbed by foreign manufacturers who cut their profits to stay competitive, American companies cutting their profits, or prices for the US consumer just going up across the board. Foreign countries may not want to cut the price on the goods they sell, and some have even announced reciprocal tariffs on the US. American companies can't turn around new manufacturing capability that quickly and many don't have the excess profit margin for tariffs of this magnitude. Growth for American companies who rely on imports from trading partners or those who might be hurt by them enacting tariffs on us should slow. When growth projections slow, the equity markets tend to do this. But it also stands to reason that most of the cost of these tariffs in the near term will get passed along to consumers like you and me. Raising prices in what we call inflation. Slow growth plus high inflation equals stagflation. The last time we really had stagflation was in the 1970s due to surging oil prices which was an exogenous factor beyond our control. And the Fed went after the flation side, not the stag, and raised interest rates north of 20% to bring prices down. Ultimately though, we had to grow our way out of it. So now investors are moving out of risky assets like equities into safer assets like treasuries in anticipation of the Fed not tackling the inflation side, but rather the stagnant or decreasing growth. The market is now pricing in a much higher likelihood of rate cuts at the next few Fed meetings. Now, you may hear theories floating around that this was the real aim of the tariffs, to pressure the Federal Reserve to cut rates so that the US can refinance $9.5 trillion of its debt at lower rates. Does that hold water? I don't know. But let's break it down. The US government has $36.2 trillion in debt. That debt doesn't all just come due on one day. The Treasury issues bills, notes, and bonds that mature anywhere from one week to 30 years. And this makes sense, right? because you don't want to have this massive concentration risk around a specific day or a year. Every month, the US Treasury holds a series of staggered auctions where it issues new debt. Each auction consists of tens of billions of new bonds. But that doesn't mean that the net debt of the country is growing by that amount. As each of these new bonds are getting issued, some old ones are maturing and getting paid back. Kind of like two steps forward, one step back. These bonds are not callable, meaning the US can't just pay them back early and reissue them with a lower interest rate if rates drop like you can say with your mortgage. When you hear that the US has to refinance 9.1 trillion worth of debt this year, they're talking about this process. It's not unique to this year. It's not some cliff. It's just the order of magnitude of bonds rolling off and new bonds that need to be issued. Sure would be nice to issue those bonds at lower interest rates, right? And if the Fed cuts rates, doesn't that translate into lower rates on our debt?
