You need to understand how Treasury auctions work if you ever want to understand the global market. The US Treasury regularly auctions off bonds to fund our government, weekly, monthly, or quarterly, depending on the maturity of the bond. But one cool thing about Treasuries is that you can trade them before they even exist. Two-year auction bonds trade as what are called WIs, which stands for when issued bonds. And they trade as a yield spread relative to the current on-the-run, aka the most recently issued bond of that mature. This allows traders to take a view on what the roll will be. How much richer or cheaper will a new bond be relative to the on-the-run? There are three types of bidders in Treasury auctions. You've got direct, indirect, and dealers. But to be an eligible direct bidder, you have to have an account set up with the Treasury. So, for simplicity's sake, a lot of buy-side market participants will just call up a bank and ask them to bid in an auction for them as direct bidders. Dealers make up the remainder of the bids. And there's two types of bids. Non-competitive, which are limited to only 10 million in size, so this is really only for retail buyers, and competitive bid, which can bid for as much as 35% of the auction size. And that's a huge amount, by the way. So, just like at Christie's or Sotheby's, supply and demand drives Treasury auction pricing. Competitive bidders submit the lowest yield, therefore the highest price, they are willing to pay for that issue. But unlike an art auction, where the highest bidder wins the whole thing, a Treasury auction is a Dutch auction. What that means is that if the Treasury's auctioning off 25 billion 10s, the bid for the 25th billionth 10 is where the whole thing clears. Going into the auction, the WI yield is the market's estimate for where that bond will be issued. And once all the bids have been tallied up at 12:59, the auction results come out just after 1:00 p.m. that same day. If the yield is higher than where the WI was trading going into the auction, the auction is said to have stopped short, or tailed, and those bonds will be issued at a lower price. If the yield is exactly the same, the auction came on the screws. And if the yield is lower, the auction came through, meaning was issued at a higher price. So, why do we even care about Treasury auctions in the first place? Well, they can be super giant sovereign wealth funds and hedge funds and pension funds and dealers are all competing for a piece of the action. And now, listen, if the auction comes through, there may be a huge rally as people who didn't get any allocations scramble to buy whatever they can. Or, if the auction tails, you might see a big sell-off as people who bought bonds suddenly realize there's less demand for these securities than they anticipated. These massive supply and demand events are a big catalyst for volatility in the rates market, which right now we are seeing a ton of.
