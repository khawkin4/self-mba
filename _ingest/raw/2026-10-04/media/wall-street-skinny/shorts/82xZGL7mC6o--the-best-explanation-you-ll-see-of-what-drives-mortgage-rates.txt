Did you know? A 30-year mortgage has almost nothing to do with 30-year Treasury yields. I know that sounds wrong, but let me explain. And once you understand why, you will never look at interest rates the same way again. Let's start with the basics. When a bank makes a 30-year conventional mortgage, it doesn't just sit on that loan forever. It bundles it up with a bunch of other loans, securitizes the whole pool of mortgages, and sells them off to investors. That frees up capital so the bank can turn around and make more loans. It's a cycle. Now, the investors buying these mortgage-backed securities are taking on a few different types of risk. There's interest rate risk, obviously. Then there's credit risk, right? The likelihood that you'll actually pay that loan back. Although, that mostly goes away if the loans are guaranteed by Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac. And then there's the big one that people forget about, especially those outside the US, prepayment risk. In America, borrowers can refinance with no penalty or just sell their homes and pay off the loan early. And that completely changes the return profile for whoever holds that security. So, when you put it all together, a mortgage rate is basically a risk-free rate that captures things like term premium, growth expectations, and inflation expectations. Hey, that kind of sounds like a Treasury yield. Plus a spread on top for those extra risks. Okay, but which Treasury should we use? It's a 30-year mortgage, so the 30-year Treasury, right? Nope. Here's why. Most borrowers don't actually keep a 30-year mortgage for 30 years. People move, they refinance, life happens. So, the actual duration of a typical mortgage-backed security, meaning both the average expected life and its sensitivity to changes in interest rates, ends up being far shorter than 30 years. In fact, when you break down what's called the partial duration profile, the cash flows of a pool of 30-year mortgages actually line up much more closely with something in the 7 to 10-year range. The way that partial durations work is you decompose the total interest rate sensitivity of a bond across different points on the yield curve. The 2-year point, the 5-year point, the 10-year point, the 30-year point, and so on. And you measure how much the price moves when each of those specific rates shifts by one basis point. When you do that math for a typical mortgage-backed security, the partial duration at the 10-year point dominates. Meaning, the price of that security is far more reactive to moves in the 10-year yield than to anything happening further out the curve. Think of it like a heat map of sensitivity. The hotspot for a pool of 30-year mortgages sits right around that 7 to 10-year bucket. And that's why mortgage rates are effectively priced off of 10-year Treasuries plus a spread, not 30 years. You know what else can be misleading? The concept of duration as a whole when it comes to bonds.
