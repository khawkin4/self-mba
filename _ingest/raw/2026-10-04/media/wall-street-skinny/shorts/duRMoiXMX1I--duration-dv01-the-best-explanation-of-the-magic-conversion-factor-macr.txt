Want to know why the war on Iran is causing all these macro hedge funds to blow up? Let's talk about it. The fixed income market, it is very different than buying into equities or golds or oil, where if the oil market goes up by 1%, you are going to make 1%. Bonds don't work that way. So, to calculate how much money you make or lose on a trade, you actually have to use a conversion factor. And the conversion factor is called duration. A textbook will tell you that duration is your weighted average time to recoup your initial investment. So, for like a 10-year zero coupon bond, you would have a duration of 10 because there is no cash interest that's getting made over the course of the life of that bond, and you get paid at maturity. If you're looking at a cash interest bond, then all of a sudden now you're getting cash over the course of the 10 years, and so the time to recoup the investment is earlier. So, it pushes the number down from call it like 10 to 8. Regardless, duration is also like this magic conversion factor that allows us to calculate how much money you make or lose being invested in bonds. Okay, so how does it work? Well, let's say you had two macro traders who each bought a hundred million dollars worth of Treasuries right before this whole war on Iran kicked off. So, we have one guy or gal who bought two-year notes, and one bought 10-year bonds. In the past 30 days, if you look at two-year yields, they went from 3.4% to 4%. So, that's basically about like a 60 basis point jump. 10-year yields on the other hand went from like 396 to 445. And so, that is essentially a 50 basis point jump. So, who lost more money? Well, to calculate the P&amp;L, we have to take the duration times the change in rates times the principal. Like, that's how you calculate your P&amp;L. So, we need to find out what is the actual duration. Let's assume for the two-year note it's 1.8, and for the 10-year bond it's 8. Well, our two-year trader basically had the duration of 1.8 times the 60 basis point move times a hundred million dollars, which is a loss of about 1 million, 1.1 to be more exact. Our 10-year trader, duration of 8 times 50 basis points times a hundred million dollars, that is a loss of 4 million dollars. So, we can see that the trader who bought the 10-year notes, even though their moving rates was smaller, they actually lost four times as much money because of duration. But, this is exactly why in practice traders don't think that way. Instead, they size their trades based on how much money they want to make or lose per basis point move in the market. So, instead of asking how much money did I make owning a hundred million dollars worth of bonds, they flip the question and they ask, "How many bonds do I need to buy so that for every one basis point move, I make or lose, say, a hundred thousand dollars?" Or with a fund that's down billion dollars, might have been asking is, "I want to put on a trade that's sized as a million and a one." And so, the notional that then they're buying is going to be much larger. And the numbers can get very big very quickly.
