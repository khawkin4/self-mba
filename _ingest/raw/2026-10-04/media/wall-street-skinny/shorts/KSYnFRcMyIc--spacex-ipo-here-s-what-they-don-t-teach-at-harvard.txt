SpaceX shares rose 10% this week, and that was entirely predictable . Let me explain. After any IPO, only a tiny fraction of shares are outstanding. SpaceX has a market cap of $2 trillion, but that's based on just 639 million of its 13.1 billion shares outstanding. In other words , 95% of that $2 trillion will begin to unlock and enter the market within the next 12 months. Where will all these buyers come from? Well, there is a solution. There are two types of buyers: active ones, who consciously want to own SpaceX, and passive ones—those who buy blindly to provide investors with access to the so-called market. So, how do we define what a market is? When compared to other G7 companies, SpaceX ranks sixth with a market capitalization of $2 trillion. Is this enough? Well, size alone is not enough to get you added to certain indexes. There are criteria, and they vary depending on which index we are talking about. So, the S&amp;P 500, Nasdaq 100, and Russell are the three most important ones, and each has its own requirements . You must trade on the exchange for a certain amount of time. S&amp;P has an additional profitability requirement: you must remain profitable for the past four quarters. Finally, all three at one time had requirements regarding the percentage of shares in free circulation (free float). So Elon Musk approached these indexes with a proposal to revise the criteria to allow SpaceX to enter them. S&amp;P confirmed its rules, which meant that SpaceX did not qualify. They didn't trade long enough, weren't profitable, and their free float was only 4.9% compared to the required 10%. However, other indices have changed their rules, meaning SpaceX either has to join them or is about to join. So, what share of the index does SpaceX actually make up? It comes down to two things. First, the actual basket of assets, and secondly, their weights. Regarding weighting: some indices adjust indicators according to free float; if you only have 4.9% of your shares in free float, they will only consider that 4.9%, but it also depends on the total number of companies in the basket. For example, the Russell 1000 contains 1,000 names, while the S&amp;P 500 contains only 500. Other indices weight companies by total market capitalization. So, in the case of SpaceX, instead of being weighted by $100 billion in free float, it would actually be weighted by a market cap of two trillion dollars. That's exactly what Nasdaq does. And , by the way, with only 100 companies in the basket. In fairness to Nasdaq, when they changed the rules to allow SpaceX to enter, they also changed the rules so that a new company with a low free float would receive the weight of its full market capitalization. Instead, they apply a three-fold multiplier to the free float of the shares. So, for SpaceX, 4.9 % turns into 14.7 %. Whatever the case, the day SpaceX is added to the Russell and Nasdaq, passive buyers, such as ETFs and mutual funds that track these indexes, should start buying up SpaceX shares. So what is the question? This is how the situation looks now. Yesterday, June 29th, SpaceX was added to the Russell indices, and it is estimated that this resulted in only three to four billion in passive buying. On July 7, SpaceX will be added to the Nasdaq, which is expected to generate at least eight billion dollars in forced purchases. But here's a trick that Elon Musk pulled off. Whenever a portion of the shares are unlocked, these indices are forced to buy them. The first unlock is expected in early August.
