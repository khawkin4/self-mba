The cost of equity is not readily observable in the market. So, what do we do? Well, we use something called the capital asset pricing model or CAPM. What the model says is that the cost of equity is equal to the risk-free rate plus the levered beta times the market risk premium. And let's actually tick through each of these one by one. The most straightforward is the risk-free rate. So for a US company, you can use the rate on a US Treasury. Now, typically you use a 10 or 20-year. If you're working in another country, you would basically just get what is the rate on the long-term government bond for that country. Although, fun fact, if you're trying to value a company that is generating cash flows in euros, you would actually use the safest country in the Euro zone. So, you'd use the German bund. Next, we get to the beta, which tells us how much a company's equity moves relative to the overall market. So, for example, if our company's beta is 1 and a quarter and the market benchmark, say the S&amp;P, goes up 1%, our company share price we expect to go up 1.25%. There's a lot that goes into choosing this beta, and I won't get into all the details, but the TLDDR is that different banks have different standards about where they get their betas. And finally, the last input is what's called the market risk premium. And this tells us how much an equity investor demands for taking risk over the risk-free rate.
