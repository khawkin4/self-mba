# Shades of 2008, anyone?
# https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yI5D81YtDYk | NA | NAs | 25000 views

I worked on the trading floor at Lehman Brothers in 2008 when it collapsed. And what I'm seeing right now looks quite familiar. In '07 and '08 we had stress cracks showing in the credit markets. We had oil prices spiking and mass layoffs happening. But, we kind of had a mixed response from global central banks. Now, remember here in the US the Federal Reserve has a dual mandate to maximize employment and to maintain price stability, which means keep inflation close to 2%. They do this by raising or lowering short-term interest rates and or using their balance sheet, meaning buying US Treasuries, mortgages, or corporate bonds to impact longer-term interest rates. Now, when inflation is high, they raise rates and or shrink their balance sheet to cool things off. So, what's happening now? Well, since the war in Iran started, oil prices have shot up. And even though the Fed looks at core inflation readings that strip out energy and food prices, the price of oil flows into everything. Higher oil translates into higher prices and inflation. Since last week, the market has said, "Okay, there's no way the Fed is going to cut rates anytime soon." And that makes sense from an academic standpoint. Why lower rates and add a boost to the economy when inflation is on the horizon? But, here's the thing. Higher inflation because of an energy price shock isn't the same as a frothy economy where everyone is Scrooge McDuck diving into their pile of money because things are going so well. An oil price shock creates tighter economic conditions. So, are you really going to hike interest rates into that environment? Think about all the terrible sentiment you've seen in the private credit markets. Rising interest rates might just be the nail in the coffin for all the BDCs who lent to SaaS companies that everyone has kind of lost faith in. And now, for the cherry on top, this morning we got a disastrous nonfarm payrolls report. The market was expecting an increase of 55,000 jobs, but instead we got a decrease of 92,000 with additional downward revisions of 69,000 for the last 2 months. I do not envy the job of central bankers trying to figure out the best course of action, but a bias towards higher interest rates creates a little bit of a perfect storm right here.
