# The best explanation of why everyone is worried about Private Credit
# https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OPrGTX6D7_4 | NA | NAs | 15000 views

What would happen if you used the same house to get two different mortgages from two different banks? That's fraud, right? Well, that's exactly what's been happening in the private credit markets. So, the real question is, how deep does it go? Listen, remember back in 2008 when bad lending practices in the mortgage market took down the global economy? We learned a big lesson, that lending risk probably shouldn't be concentrated in an oligopoly of 10 banks. So, we regulated legacy lending institutions practically into oblivion, replacing the traditional investment banks with what was then termed the shadow banking industry, and later given a facelift and rebranded as private credit. Private credit isn't some complex mysterious thing. At its core, we're just talking about loans that don't end up traded in the public markets. If you're a big company like Meta and you went to an investment bank 15 years ago to borrow money, they'd get together a bunch of other banks in what's called a syndicate. They'd then underwrite that loan, securitize it into little packages called bonds, and distribute those to thousands of investors all over the world, where people can buy individual identical slices and are entitled to the cash flows and the risk. Those bonds trade frequently in the public markets with a visible dollar price based on where buyers and sellers are willing to trade them at any given hour on any given day. But, in the past five or six years, the sexiest thing instead has been to go to a Blackstone or Blue Owl and say, "Hey, I want to borrow that money from you directly instead." The benefit to me as Meta, is that these giant private capital firms awash with cash can close these loans in a matter of days versus in the traditional capital markets, that can take weeks or months. And if things go wrong, I only have to renegotiate with like one investor or maybe a small handful versus countless grandmas in Idaho who have my bonds locked up in their retirement account. The downside? Well, I theoretically have to pay a higher interest rate as the borrower. It's more expensive. But the reality is with so much capital chasing so few lending opportunities, more and more investors have been competing to lend money at thinner and thinner margins with less and less diligence. And amongst those chasing the last marginal dollar, you guessed it, the very same banks who'd been regulated into a corner. So now who's in the news? Banks like Jefferies who made bad loans to companies like First Brands and MFS who committed fraud by double pledging collateral. Like I said before, when you borrow money from the bank to buy a house, the house is the collateral for that loan. So now imagine if you promised the same house to two different lenders and borrowed twice the money. That's fraud and that's what these companies have been doing. So the question, which I don't have the answer to, is how isolated are these instances of fraud? How systemic is the risk of bad loans? And quite frankly, how much does it all even matter if sentiment remains this bearish and it becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy?
