SpaceX, Google and OpenAI. Each of them has broken records, raising over a hundred billion dollars in the last few months. But where does all this money actually go? I'll explain AI funding in less than 3 minutes. They drove. NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang called the AI ​​ecosystem a five-layer cake. The fifth and fourth layers are the most attractive and visible, and OpenAI and Claude are probably their main faces. But to build these models, they need layers one through three. Energy, chips and infrastructure. These are what Jensen called AI factories. It's worth noting that each of the large hyperscalers— companies whose core business is operating computing infrastructure on a massive scale— has different parts of these five layers, and Google is the only hyperscaler that has all five layers in place, at least for now. In any case, computing power is required to run the models . So, computing power is what we produce in these AI factories. Think of how it works as an assembly line. You get some raw material—fuel. It is then converted into energy that flows into the grid and from there into the data center, which itself is just a shell connected to power. This shell is useless without chips, and it is from the data center that computing power comes. But the part of the system that is the limiting factor is actually energy. So where does the energy for these data centers come from? Well, historically many people took it from the internet. And although we call it a " network," it's not the only one. In the US, there are actually three of them. Western union, Eastern union, and Texas has its own network. Despite everything, 66 different balancing agencies try to maintain electricity supply, operating under different rules in different states—some regulated, some not—it's a complicated, interconnected system. But the most important task of the network is to match supply and demand in real time. And the best way to visualize this is through a generation stack. We take every power plant in the region and build them from the cheapest to the most expensive. Below we have nuclear power, and here we add coal, since its power is difficult to regulate. Next up is solar and wind energy, which are renewable sources that are free to operate after construction. Most of the demand, however, is likely to be met by gas-fired power plants, where the cost of operation depends on the price of gas. And it depends on geography. The closer you are to the pipeline, the cheaper it is and the higher the efficiency of the station . The least efficient are peaking power plants, which can only operate for a few hundred hours per year. Grid operators compare supply with total demand, and the price of electricity must be high enough to cover the operation of the least efficient and most expensive plant. Less efficient stations remain off, and the cost of the marginal station determines the price for all others. But here's the thing. In residential areas, demand is not static. It changes hourly, with peak consumption usually occurring between 2:00 PM and 9:00 PM each day. Data center loads are virtually static, so they operate continuously, 24/7. So when a data center connects to the network, it not only raises prices, but also puts pressure on a network that is already operating at capacity. Last week, the Department of Energy declared a state of emergency in New York City's power system as heatwaves sent demand to record levels. This explains why new laws in many states require that if you want to build a data center, you provide your own energy sources.
