# What is "DV01" and how is it used? An intuitive explanation.

Want to think like a bond trader and understand how they make and lose money? Then you must understand the concept of duration. So, what is duration and why does it matter? Well, when you go to Vegas and sit down at the blackjack tables, you first need to figure out if you're sitting down at the $10 table or the $1,000 a hand table. You are sizing your risk. Traders think this way in order to size their trades, and duration is the conversion factor that helps them do this. Now, a textbook's probably going to tell you that duration is your cash-weighted average time to recouping your initial investment in a bond. But why does that matter if you're who's just trying to buy something at a low price and sell it 5 minutes later at a higher price? Well, mathematically, duration is also how you measure a fixed-rate bond's sensitivity to a change in market rates. It's the conversion factor you use to calculate what impact every incremental move in market rates has on the value of the security that you own today. Okay, so how do you calculate duration? Bloomberg and Excel make it easy, so you just need to understand the concept. Think about it this way. If you had a 10-year zero-coupon bond that doesn't pay you any interest until maturity, the duration of that bond is effectively going to be 10. A 10-year bond that pays you semi-annual coupons every year at 5% interest, well, that might have a duration of, say, 7 and 1/2 because more of our cash flows are weighted heavily towards today. Once we have our duration, we now have our magical conversion factor that tells us how much money we make or lose with every tiny change in market rates. Because we're already talking in percentages, we need to talk about changes in units of basis points, which is 1 / 10,000. Our conversion factor of 7 and 1/2 tells us that for every one basis point running change in yield over the life of a bond, we make 7 and 1/2 basis points up front on the nominal value. So, if I have 100 million 10-year US Treasuries and the market rate on 10s goes from 5 to 4.99, I've made 7 and 1/2 * 1 / 10,000 * 100 million, which is $75,000. Traders convert all their risk into this exposure to move in market rates and size their trades to achieve that exposure. They aren't going to the blackjack tables like, "Hmm, maybe I'm going to bet a hundred million dollars or a billion dollars and hope for the best." They think, "I believe in this trade. I want to make or lose a hundred thousand dollars for every one basis point move in the market." So, in order to figure out how many bonds they need to buy or sell, they just plug it into that basic formula. If I want to make or lose a hundred thousand dollars per basis point, we say a hundred K and no one, and I know that my duration is seven and a half, I simply divide a hundred thousand by seven and a half times ten thousand, and get a hundred and thirty-three million for the size of my trade. Of course, this is an oversimplified example. Okay, not only is the duration for every instrument different, but the duration for each instrument changes as rates move because of something called convexity.
