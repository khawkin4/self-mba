Here's exactly how AI data centers are impacting the power grid and your utility bills, in less than 3 minutes. The truth is not at all what you think. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang called the AI ​​ecosystem a five-layer cake. The fifth and fourth layers are the most attractive and noticeable. Where OpenAI and Claude are probably the two main examples. But to create these models, layers one through three are needed : energy, chips, and infrastructure. These are what Jensen called “AI factories.” It is worth noting that each of the large hyperscalers, that is, companies whose core business is large-scale computing infrastructure, has different parts of these five layers. At the same time, Google is the only hyperscaler that has all five layers of its own production , at least for now. In any case, computing power is required to run models . So, computation is what we produce in these AI factories. A way to understand how this works is to imagine an assembly line. You get some raw material, such as fuel. It is then converted into energy that enters the network and from there to the data center, which itself is just a powered “shell .” This shell is useless without chips, and ultimately the computing power comes out of the data center . But the part of the system that is the limiting factor is actually energy. So where does the energy for these data centers come from? Historically, many people took it from the network. And although we call it a "network," it is not alone. In the US, there are actually three of them. Western United Power System, Eastern United Power System and Texas. ERCOT has its own system. In any case, with 66 different balancing entities trying to maintain electricity supply, operating under different rules in different states—some regulated, some not—it's a complex, interconnected system. But the most important task of the network is to match supply and demand in real time. And the best way to visualize this is through a “generation stack.” Take every power plant in the region and build them from the cheapest to operate to the most expensive. Down below we have nuclear power, and coal is added there because its power is difficult to regulate. Also, solar and wind energy are renewable sources that operate for free after construction . Most demand, however, is likely to be met by natural gas-fired power plants, where the cost of operation depends on the price of gas. This, of course, depends on geography. The closer you are to the pipeline, the cheaper it is, and it also depends on the efficiency of the station . The least efficient are peaking power plants, which can only operate for a few hundred hours per year. Grid operators match supply with overall demand, and the price of electricity must be high enough to cover the operation of the least efficient and most expensive plant. Less efficient stations remain off, and the price of the marginal station determines the cost for everyone. But there is a nuance here. In residential areas, demand is not static. It changes hourly, with peak demand usually occurring between 2:00 PM and 9:00 PM each day. The load on data centers is actually static, so they operate almost constantly, 24/7. So when a data center consumes power from the grid, it not only pushes up prices, but also puts strain on a grid that is already operating at its limits. Last week, the Department of Energy declared a state of emergency on New York City's grid as the heat pushed demand to record levels. This explains why new laws in many states require that if you want to build a data center, you must provide your own power source.
