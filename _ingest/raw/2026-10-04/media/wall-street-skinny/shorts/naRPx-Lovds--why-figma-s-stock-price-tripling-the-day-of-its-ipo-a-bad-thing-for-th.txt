# Why Figma's stock price tripling the day of its IPO a BAD THING for the company
# https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=naRPx-Lovds | NA | NAs | 2700 views

Figma just went public and shares tripled on day one. So why is that bad for the company and some early VCs? Well, it sounds counterintuitive, but here's the deal. When a company goes out and does an IPO or an initial public offering, the goal is twofold. Number one, to help the company raise money from the public markets for the very first time. And number two, to help existing investors like venture capital firms and early backers sell out some of their shares and cash out. Now, in Figma's case, the company itself sold 12 a.5 million primary shares and existing investors sold another 24.5 million secondary shares for a total offering of roughly 37 million shares. Now, it's worth noting that when a company does an IPO, you're only taking a small fraction of the actual equity public. So, again, they sold 37 million shares in the initial public offering, but there are 585 million shares that are going to be outstanding, what's called on a fully diluted basis post IPO. So that equates to roughly 6% of the equity that was taken public ignoring the green shoe which we'll talk about in the caption. So Figma's IPO priced at $33 a share which implies a $19.3 billion valuation. Typically underwriters so the investment banks that are you know taking the company public they want to price so that there's a small pop in the share price of roughly about 15%. So, in other words, they try to actually go out and the $33 share price is at a usually like 15% discount to where they think the company shares will trade at the end of that first day. What actually happened, however, is Figma share price jumped at the end of day one to $115 a share, which is roughly like 250%. Which sounds amazing. So, what's the problem? Well, let's do a little math. So, the company and the VCs took 37 million shares public. At $33 a share, it means that they raised roughly $1.2 $.2 billion in cash proceeds. However, because the share price traded up to 115, that's almost $82 more than where they priced in the IPO. And so, if we apply the $82 difference to the shares sold in the IPO, the company and investors collectively left roughly $3 billion on the table. So, in other words, by pricing the IPO to conservatively, they sold shares at a huge discount compared to where the market actually valued them just hours later. That's clearly not ideal for Figma from a purely financial perspective. There is a silver lining. That massive first day pop is still fantastic news for existing investors who didn't sell in the IPO and for the company who can still raise money by issuing equity via follow-on offerings and secondary offerings, etc. Now, they will have to wait the lockup period, which is usually about 180 days, before they can cash in. But for now, they're holding shares that are worth almost triple what the IPO priced
