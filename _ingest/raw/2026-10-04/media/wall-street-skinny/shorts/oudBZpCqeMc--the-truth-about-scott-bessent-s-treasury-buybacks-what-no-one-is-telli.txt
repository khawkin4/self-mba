As a former Bloomberg terminal "bro," I know that many people don't understand what Scott Bessent's Treasury bond buyback is. I'll explain it in less than 3 minutes. Treasury bonds are simply debt instruments issued by the U.S. government. Let's draw a graph where the X-axis is the time it takes for the government to pay you back. And the Y-axis is the interest rate that investors demand for lending this money. All other things being equal, the longer you freeze your money, the higher the interest rate you demand as compensation for risk, lost profit, and inflation. This is called the yield curve. If we build a heat map of investor demand, we will see a huge number of buyers for short-term securities, such as money market funds. But after 10-year notes, demand drops sharply, and it is lowest for 20- and 30- year bonds. To be precise, by 20- and 30-year bonds we mean "on-the-run" —recently issued bonds with a maturity date similar to the latest version of the iPhone. The current 30- year bond is a paper with a coupon of 5 and 1/8 , maturing in August 2056. But there are also "off-the-run"—old releases. Let's take a 1 and 3/8 coupon bond dated August 2050, issued 6 years ago during COVID, when rates were zero. With rates up more than 400 basis points, these securities are trading at 46 cents on the dollar, unlike the new ones, which are trading near par. Think of them as an older iPhone model. If you go to an Apple store, you'll have a hard time finding someone to sell you an iPhone 12. Same with our old August 2050 bonds. If someone wants to buy them, the bank will have a hard time finding them. And if someone sells them to a bank, who will they then give them to? They just lie there, cluttering up bank balance sheets . Since 2024, the Ministry of Finance has launched a buyback program, under which it buys several billion of such illiquid securities every month. Last month, they more than doubled their purchases on the 10- to 30-year portion of the curve. I like to compare it to a "cash for junk" program. But where do they get the cash for this "car junk"? Three options. Option one: sell treasury bills . Supply is moved from the long-term segment to where there is the greatest demand, without affecting duration. To help you understand better, let me lay out the relative sizes of the auctions at which the Ministry of Finance sells debt obligations per month. I can't even display the Treasury bills here to the proper scale. They just won't fit on the chart. Taking $6 billion out of long-term bonds is something, but adding $6 billion to bills is a barely noticeable error. The second option is the issuance of two- and ten-year Treasury notes. There is a high demand for them, but this will also affect the duration of the debt. Long-term rates may fall a little, but the greater supply will push short-term rates up, flattening the yield curve and making short-term borrowing more expensive. The third option is cash payment from the Main Treasury Account, which acts as the current account of the Ministry of Finance. Taxpayers and bondholders provide the Treasury with cash to cover its debt obligations, which currently exceed revenues. But when the Ministry of Finance buys back bonds and removes them from circulation with funds that should not have entered the system, it stimulates the economy. Not as much as quantitative easing, but it is similar to it and is a continuation of the same activities that led to the current high rates that we now need to correct.
