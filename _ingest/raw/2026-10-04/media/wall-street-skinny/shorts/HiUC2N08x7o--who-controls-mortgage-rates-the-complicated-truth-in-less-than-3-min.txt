# Who Controls Mortgage Rates? The Complicated Truth in Less Than 3 min
# https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HiUC2N08x7o | NA | NAs | 7400 views

Everyone's been focusing on oil prices, but the Iran war is also causing mortgage rates to spike. 30-year fixed-rate mortgages just hit 6 and 1/2% which is all the way up from just 5 and 3/4 at the end of February. That might seem counterintuitive. I mean, didn't the Fed just hold rates steady last week? The answer is yes, but here's the thing. There are lots of different interest rates and the one that the Fed just held flat is the overnight Fed funds rate. They actually don't control the longer end of the yield curve. That is set by the market and right now with oil prices spiking, it's stoking inflation fears which is pushing up rates in the long end of the curve. Those long-term rates, by the way, is what we actually care about for mortgages. Now, the Fed can in fact influence those longer-term rates through something called quantitative easing or QE. The Fed steps in as a buyer buying long-dated Treasury bonds and mortgage-backed securities. They create new money deposit into the accounts of banks like JP Morgan or Morgan Stanley. That additional demand pushes yields down so causes the longer end of the curve to drop. Is the Fed doing that? No. The Fed is an independent body with two goals. Number one, maximize employment, and number two, keep inflation around 2%. By quantitative easing when there are real inflation issues, that would be like pouring gasoline on the fire. However, housing affordability and mortgage rates have been a huge issue for Americans. So, enter something that isn't QE but that rhymes with QE. Trump directed Fannie and Freddie to acquire $200 billion in mortgage-backed securities to try to get those mortgage rates down. The Fed isn't doing this. Remember, it's independent or at least it's supposed to be. But Fannie and Freddie are by definition government-sponsored entities. So, Trump can direct them and he has. This isn't out of the blue. The GSEs have been expanding what's called their retained portfolio significantly since May of last year, but the $200 billion in those extra purchases is pushing them closer to the caps that were put in place after the 2008 financial crisis, meaning they don't really have unlimited room to run. And so, that bears the real question which is is it going to actually impact mortgage rates? Well, the agency MBS market, what Fannie and Freddie is buying, is a little bit over $9 trillion. Whether $200 billion meaningfully moves the needle in a $9 trillion market, I'm a little skeptical. And honestly, I think that might be besides the point. This is a signal. It is a move so when the midterms hit, the administration can say, "We did something about it." But whether that something results in lower mortgage rates is another question entirely.
