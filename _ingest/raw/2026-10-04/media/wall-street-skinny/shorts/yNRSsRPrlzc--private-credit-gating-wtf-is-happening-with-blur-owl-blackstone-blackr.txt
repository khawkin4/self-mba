It feels like every day we are getting a new headline about someone gating a private credit fund. Two weeks ago it was Blue Owl. Earlier this week it was Blackstone, then it was BlackRock, and then yesterday it was Morgan Stanley. What exactly is happening? It boils down to investors who are on one side of like this private credit equation panicking, and then you have the assets on the other side, loans made to various companies, and whether those underlying assets are bad. For the past 15 years, private credit has been seen as super sexy. Although to be honest, it's actually quite boring. Private credit just simply means debt that isn't traded on the public markets. But, because that debt is illiquid, investors demand a higher return. And so if you could borrow, say at 5% in the public markets, you might have to borrow, say 6% if you go to a private credit fund. However, investors in these private credit funds often want more than a yield of just say like 6%. So, if you have investors who are putting in $100 of their own cash, the fund is then going to go out borrow an additional $100, and then it'll make $200 worth of loans against that. All fine and good, especially if you're looking at institutional investors like Harvard and Dalmanor CalSTRS who have long-term investment horizons and don't need the money back tomorrow. The problem is Wall Street created a product for retail investors. Enter something called the business development company or BDC. BDCs come in two flavors, publicly traded BDCs and then the private type of BDC. We're going to skip over the publicly traded BDC. It's actually very similar to an ETF, but the private ones are not traded, and so while investors can request solid like 5% redemptions per quarter, if there were too many people who try to get their money back at once, the fund does something called gating. They basically don't want to have to sell at fire sale prices, but it does then result in people freaking the F out because they can't get their money back. Not to panic anyone, but there are some real similarities to CDOs here. Back in 2008, the issue was not corporate credit, but rather mortgages. All of these loans that were bad got pooled together into something called mortgage-backed securities, which then often got pulled into CDOs, which added additional leverage and sometimes even into CDO squared. The underlying reason the floor fell out is those loans were bad. Now, in 2007, I was working in the CLO group, which was collateralized loan obligations, and the underlying corporate loans were good. So, the question everyone should be asking is whether the underlying assets are like the corporate loans of 2008 that ultimately were good, or whether the lending standards to companies have gotten so lax that the underlying loans are toxic.
