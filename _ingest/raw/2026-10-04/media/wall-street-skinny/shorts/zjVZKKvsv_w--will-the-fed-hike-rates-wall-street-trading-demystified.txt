# Will the Fed Hike Rates? Wall Street Trading Demystified 👀
# https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zjVZKKvsv_w | NA | NAs | 18000 views

Do you have that one annoying friend who works on Wall Street and says things like the market's pricing a 68% chance of a Fed hike? How do they know that? Are they just checking Cal-She? Nope. There's trillions of dollars riding on this number and I'm going to teach you the math in less than 3 minutes so you know exactly as much as your annoying friend and maybe even more. The Fed funds rate touches everything from student loans to mortgages to corporations borrowing a fortune to build out AI and it's set by one institution with two jobs, keeping inflation in check and maximizing employment, the Federal Reserve. When bank A has spare cash, they can lend it to the bank B overnight and charge them an interest rate. The Fed sets the target range for that interest rate. Right now, it's 350 to 375. The average across all the trades that actually happen, which today is right in the middle at 363, is called the Fed funds effective rate. Now let's take the month of September, which has 30 days. There's a Fed meeting on the 16th where they'll either change rates or keep them the same. 363 we already know is the effective rate for the first 16 days, barring some crazy liquidity crisis between now and then. But we don't know the rate for the last 14 days of the month. So we look at the Fed funds futures market. Unlike your favorite betting app where the buy-in might be $20, the minimum notional here is $5 million. So this is a market reserved for banks, hedge funds, and big asset managers. Each contract covers one calendar month and it settles to the average of the effective rate across every single day of the month. It's quoted as a dollar price and the implied market rate for that month is just 100 minus the price. For the Sep contract, 100 minus 96.29 is 3.71, the implied rate for all of September, which is higher than 3.63, so we can use it to calculate the odds of a rate hike. First we take 30 days of the month times 3.71 minus 16 known days at 3.63 divided by the 14 unknown days gives us 3.80, the market implied rate after the Fed meeting. Next, 3.80 minus 3.63 tells us the size of the expected change. Divide that by 25 basis points, which we assume is the minimum increment of a Fed hike and we get a 68% chance of a rate hike at the Fed meeting. What about when people say there's almost two full rate hikes priced in by year-end? You can think of these prices as representing a decision tree at each Fed meeting where they can either cut, hold, or hike rates. Let's draw a heat map of the likeliest path based on the futures market right now. The likeliest path is in yellow, and you see the market's pricing in no chance of a cut. To calculate our year-end prediction, we take our January Fed funds contract, which doesn't have a Fed meeting till the end of the month, but captures all of December, and we see that our implied market rate at the end of Jan is 410. Looks like almost two hikes, but not quite. Take 410, our market-implied Fed funds effective rate for Jan, minus today's of 363, divide by the 25 basis point rate hike increment, and we get 1.88 rate hikes priced in by year-end.
