# Ray Dalio - How The Economic Machine Works
# https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PHe0bXAIuk0

How the economy works, in 30 minutes. The economy works like a simple machine. But many people don't understand it —or disagree with how it works —and this has led to a lot of unnecessary economic suffering. I feel a deep responsibility to share my simple, yet practical economic model. Although unconventional, it helped me predict and avoid the global financial crisis, and it has worked well for me for over 30 years. Let's begin. Although economics seems complex, it works in a simple, mechanical way. It is made up of a few simple parts and many simple transactions that repeat themselves over and over again. These transactions are primarily driven by human nature and create 3 main forces that drive the economy. Number 1: Productivity Growth Number 2: Short-Term Debt Cycle and Number 3: Long-Term Debt Cycle. We'll look at these three forces and how layering them together creates a good model for tracking economic movements and understanding what's happening right now. Let's start with the simplest part of economics: transactions. An economy is nothing more than the sum of the transactions that constitute it, and a transaction is a very simple thing. Transactions are made continuously. Every time you buy something, you create a transaction. Each transaction consists of a buyer exchanging money or credit with a seller of goods, services, or financial assets. Credit acts as money, so by adding together the money spent and the amount of credit used, you can figure out your total spending. The total amount spent pulls the economy. If you divide the amount spent by the quantity sold, you get the price. That's all. This is a transaction. It is the basis of the economic machine. All cycles and all forces in an economy are driven by transactions. So if we can understand transactions, we can understand the entire economy. A market consists of all the buyers and sellers transacting for the same thing. For example, there is a grain market, a car market, a stock market, and markets for millions of other things. An economy consists of all the transactions in all its markets. If you add up the total expenditure and the total quantity sold in all markets, you have everything you need to understand economics. It is extremely simple. People, corporations, banks, and governments all engage in transactions in the manner described: exchanging money and credit for goods, services, and financial assets. The largest buyer and seller is the public sector, which consists of two important parts: a central government that collects taxes and spends money… …and a central bank, which is different from other buyers and sellers because it controls the amount of money and credit in the economy. It does this by manipulating interest rates and printing new money. For these reasons, as we will see, the central bank is an important player in the flow of credit. I want you to be careful about credit. Credit is the most important part of the economy and probably the least understood. It is the most important part because it is the largest and most volatile part. Just as buyers and sellers go to the market to transact, so do creditors and debtors. People who lend money usually want to turn what they have into more money, and people who go into debt usually want something they can't afford, like a house or a car, or they want to invest in something like starting a business. Credit can help both lenders and borrowers get what they want. Borrowers promise to repay the amount borrowed, called principal, plus an additional amount called interest. When interest rates are high, people borrow less because it is expensive. When interest rates are low, people borrow more because it costs less. When debtors promise to repay and creditors believe them, credit is created. Two people can agree to create credit out of thin air! This seems simple enough, but credit is difficult, because it has different names. As soon as credit is created, it immediately converts into debt. Debt is both an asset for the lender and a liability for the borrower. In the future, when the borrower repays the loan plus interest, the asset and liability disappear and the transaction is closed. So why is credit so important? Because when a debtor receives credit, his spending capacity increases. And remember, spending drives the economy. This happens because one person's spending is another person's income. Think about it, every dollar you spend, someone earns. and every dollar you earn, someone spent. So when you spend more, someone earns more. When someone's income goes up, lenders are more inclined to lend them money because they now deserve more credit. A debtor with good credit has two things: the ability to repay and collateral. Having a lot of income in relation to his debt gives him the ability to repay. If he can't repay, he has marketable assets of value to use as collateral. This puts lenders at ease when lending money. So a higher income allows for greater debt, which allows for increased spending. And since one person's spending is another person's income, this leads to increasing debt and so on. This self-reinforcing model brings economic growth, and that's why we have cycles. In a transaction you have to give something to get something. In the long run, how much you receive depends on how much you produce. Over time we learn and that accumulated knowledge increases our standards of living. We call this productivity growth. The inventive and industrious increase productivity and living standards faster than the indolent and lazy, but this is not always true in the short term. Productivity is especially important in the long term, but credit is especially important in the short term. This is because productivity growth does not fluctuate much, so it is not a driver of large economic swings. Debt exists – because it allows us to consume more than we produce when we acquire it and makes us consume less than we produce when we repay it. Its fluctuations occur in two major cycles. One takes about 5-8 years and the other about 75-100 years. Most people feel the fluctuations, but they typically don't see them as cycles, because they see them too closely -- day by day. In this chapter we take a step back and look at these three great forces and how they interact to shape our experiences. As mentioned, swings along this line aren't due to how much innovation or hard work there is, they're mostly due to how much credit there is. Let's imagine for a moment an economy without credit. In this economy, the only way to increase my spending is to increase my income, which requires me to be more productive and do more work. Increased productivity is the only way to grow. Since my spending is income for someone else, the economy grows whenever I or someone else is more productive. If we follow the transactions and let them unfold, we see a progression like the productivity growth line. But since we go into debt, there are cycles. This is not due to any law, but to human nature and the way credit works. Think of borrowing as an easy way to boost spending. To have something you can't afford, you have to spend more than you earn. To do this, you essentially have to go into debt with yourself in the future. By doing this, you create a period in the future where you will spend less than you earn to pay it off. It soon takes on the appearance of a cycle. Simply put , every time you go into debt you create a cycle. This is true for an individual and for the economy. This is why understanding credit is so important, because it sets in motion a mechanical and predictable series of events that will occur in the future. This makes credit different from money. Currency is what you close transactions with. When you buy a beer from a bartender with cash, the transaction is closed immediately. But when you buy it on credit, it's like opening a bar tab. You say you promise to pay in the future. Together, you and the barista create an asset and a liability. Now you have created credit out of thin air. Until you pay the bill, the assets and liabilities don't disappear, the debt doesn't go away, and the transaction doesn't close. The reality is that most of what people call money is credit. The total amount of credit in the United States is about $50 trillion, and the total amount of money is only about $3 trillion. Remember, in a creditless economy the only way to increase your spending is to produce more. But in a credit economy, you can also increase your spending by borrowing. As a result, a credit-rich economy has more spending and allows incomes to rise faster than productivity in the short term, but not in the long term. Now, don't get me wrong, credit isn't always a bad thing that just causes cycles. It is bad when it inflates consumption that cannot be repaid. However, it is good when you allocate resources efficiently and generate income to repay the debt. For example, if you go into debt to buy a large TV, you don’t generate income to repay the debt. But, if you take out a loan for a tractor -- and that tractor lets you grow a bigger crop and earn more money -- then you can pay off your debt and improve your standard of living. In a credit economy, we can follow transactions and see how credit creates growth. Let me give you an example. Suppose you earn $100,000 a year and have no debt. You have enough credit to borrow $10,000 —say, from a credit card —so you can spend $110,000 even though you only earn $100,000. Since your expense is someone else's income, someone earns $110,000. The person who earns $110,000 with no debt can borrow $11,000, so he can spend $121,000, even though he only earned $110,000. His spending is someone else's income, and by following the transactions we see how this process works in a self-reinforcing model. But remember, borrowing creates cycles, and if the cycle goes up, sooner or later it has to go down. This brings us to the short-term debt cycle. As activity increases, we see an expansion – the first phase of the short-term debt cycle. Spending continues to increase and prices are starting to rise. This happens because the increase in spending is fueled by credit – which can be created instantly out of thin air. When spending and incomes grow faster than the production of goods, prices rise. When prices rise, we talk about inflation. The central bank doesn't want too much inflation, because it causes problems. Seeing prices rising, he raises interest rates. With higher interest rates, fewer people can afford to borrow. And the cost of existing debt is rising. Think about it when your monthly credit card payments go up. As people borrow less and have higher debt repayments, they have less money left to spend, so spending decreases … and since one person's spending is another person's income, income decreases… and so on. When people spend less, prices go down. We call this deflation. Economic activity decreases and we have a recession. If the recession becomes too severe and inflation is no longer a problem, the central bank will lower interest rates to make everything rise again. With low interest rates, debt repayments are reduced and borrowing and spending pick up and we see another expansion. As you can see, the economy works like a machine. In the short-term debt cycle, spending is limited only by the willingness of creditors and debtors to give and receive credit. When credit is readily available, there is economic expansion. When credit is not readily available, there is a recession. And note that this cycle is mainly controlled by the central bank. The short-term debt cycle usually lasts 5-8 years and repeats itself over and over for decades. But notice that the base and peak of each cycle end with more growth than the previous cycle and with more debt. Why? Because people push it - they have a tendency to borrow and spend more, rather than repay. It's human nature. Because of this, in the long run debts increase faster than incomes, creating the long-term debt cycle. Even as people become more indebted, lenders are also extending credit more freely. Why? Because everyone thinks things are going great! People only focus on the latest events. And what happened lately? Incomes have increased! Asset values ​​are rising! The stock market is roaring! It 's a boom! It is worth purchasing goods, services and financial assets with borrowed money! When people overdo it, it's called a bubble. So, even as debts have been growing, incomes have been growing almost fast enough to offset them. We call the debt-to-income ratio the debt burden. As long as incomes continue to rise, the debt burden remains manageable. At the same time, asset values ​​are rising. People borrow huge sums of money to buy assets as investments, causing prices to rise even further. People feel rich. Thus, even with the accumulation of a lot of debt, the increase in income and asset values ​​​​helps debtors enjoy credit for a long time. But this, of course, cannot continue forever. And he doesn't. Over decades, debt burdens slowly increase, creating ever-increasing debt repayments. At a certain point, debt repayments begin to grow faster than incomes, forcing people to reduce their spending. And since one person's spending is another person's income, incomes start to decline... ...which makes people less creditworthy and causes lending to decline. Debt repayments continue to rise, which leads to further declines in spending... ...and the cycle reverses. This is peak long-term debt. Debt burdens have become too great. For the United States, Europe, and much of the rest of the world, this happened in 2008. It happened for the same reason it happened in Japan in 1989 and in the United States in 1929. Now the economy is beginning to deleverage. In a deleveraging crisis, people cut spending, incomes fall, credit disappears, asset prices fall, banks come under pressure, the stock market crashes, social tensions rise, and everything begins to feed itself in the other direction. As incomes fall and debt repayments rise, debtors are under pressure. No longer creditworthy, the credit lapses and debtors can no longer borrow money to repay the debt. Struggling to fill this hole, debtors are having to sell off assets. The rush to sell goods is taking over the market. This is when the stock market crashes, the housing market tanks, and the banks run into trouble. As the prices of goods fall, the value of the collateral that the debtor can offer decreases. This makes debtors even less creditworthy. People feel poor. Credit disappears quickly. Less spending › less income › less wealth › less credit › less loans and so on. It's a vicious cycle. This appears similar to a recession, but the difference here is that interest rates cannot be lowered to save the situation. In a recession, lowering interest rates pushes people into debt. However, in a deleveraging scenario, lowering interest rates doesn't work because interest rates are already low and will soon hit 0%—and then the stimulus ends. Interest rates in the United States hit 0% during the deleveraging of the 1930s and again in 2008. The difference between a recession and a deleveraging is that in a recession, debt burdens are simply magnified and cannot be alleviated by lowering interest rates. Lenders realize that the debts have become too large to be repaid in full. The debtors have lost their ability to repay and their collateral its value. They feel paralyzed by debt – they don't want to know anything about it anymore! Creditors stop lending. Debtors stop borrowing. Think of the economy as uncreditable, just like an individual. So what do you do about deleveraging? The problem is that debt burdens are too high and need to come down. There are four ways this can happen. 1. People, businesses and governments cut their spending. 2. Debts are reduced through bankruptcies and restructurings. 3. Wealth is redistributed from the haves to the have-nots. And finally 4. the central bank prints new money. These 4 ways have happened in every deleveraging in modern history. Spending is usually cut first. As we have just seen, people, businesses, banks, and even governments are tightening their belts and cutting spending to pay off their debt. This is often referred to as austerity. When borrowers stop taking out new loans and start paying off old debts, the debt burden is expected to decrease. But the opposite happens! Because spending is cut —and one person’s spending is another person’s income—it drives down incomes. They fall faster than debts are repaid and the debt burden actually worsens. As we have seen, this spending cut is deflationary and painful. Companies are forced to cut costs... which means fewer jobs and higher unemployment. This leads to the next step: debts must be reduced! Many debtors cannot repay their debts - and a debtor's debts are a creditor's assets. When borrowers don't repay the bank, people think the bank can't repay them, so they rush to withdraw their money from the bank. Banks are under pressure and people, businesses and banks are defaulting on their debts. This severe economic contraction is a depression. Much of a depression consists of people discovering that much of what they thought was their wealth is no longer there. Let's go back to the bar. When you bought a beer and put it on your tab at the bar, you promised to reimburse the bartender. Your promise has become a barista's activity. But if you break the promise—if you don't pay him back and default on your bar tab— then the "business" he has is effectively worthless. It's essentially disappeared. Many lenders do not want the businesses to disappear and accept debt restructuring. Debt restructuring means that creditors are repaid less or over a longer period of time or at a lower interest rate than previously agreed. Somehow the contract is breached to reduce the debt. Lenders would rather have a little of something than all of nothing. Even if the debt disappears, debt restructuring causes income and asset values ​​to disappear more rapidly, so the debt burden continues to worsen. Like cutting spending, debt reduction is also painful and deflationary. All of this impacts the central government, because lower incomes and less employment mean the government collects less taxes. At the same time, it needs to increase spending because unemployment has risen. Many of the unemployed have inadequate savings and need financial support from the government. Governments also create stimulus plans and increase their spending to compensate for the weakening economy. Government budget deficits explode in deleveraging because they spend more than they earn in taxes. This is what happens when you hear about the budget deficit on the news. To finance their deficits, governments must raise taxes or borrow money. But with declining incomes and so many unemployed, where does the money come from? From the rich. Since governments need more money and wealth is highly concentrated in the hands of a small percentage of people, governments naturally raise taxes on the wealthy, which facilitates a redistribution of wealth throughout the economy— from the haves to the have-nots. The have-nots, who are suffering, are beginning to dislike the have-nots. The wealthy, pressured by a weak economy, falling asset values, and higher taxes, reciprocate the antipathy toward the have-nots. If depression continues, social disorder may erupt. Not only do tensions increase within countries, but they can arise between countries – especially debtor and creditor countries. The situation can lead to political change that can sometimes be extreme. In the 1930s this brought Hitler to power, war in Europe and depression in the United States. The pressure to do something to stop the depression increases. Remember, most of what people thought was money was actually credit. So when credit disappears, people don't have enough money. People are desperate for money and remember who prints money? The central bank. Having already lowered interest rates to nearly 0 - it is forced to print money. Unlike spending cuts, debt reduction, and wealth redistribution, printing money is inflationary and stimulative. Inevitably, the central bank prints new money – out of thin air – and uses it to buy financial assets and government bonds. It happened in the United States during the Great Depression and again in 2008, when the US central bank – the Federal Reserve – printed over two trillion dollars. Other central banks around the world that had the power to do so printed a lot of money. By purchasing financial goods with this currency, you encourage the prices of goods to rise, which makes people more creditworthy. However, this only helps those who have financial assets. The central bank can print money, but it can only buy financial assets. The central government, on the other hand, can purchase goods and services and put money into the hands of the people, but it cannot print it. So, in order to stimulate the economy, the two must cooperate. By buying government bonds, the central bank essentially lends money to the government, allowing it to run a deficit and increase spending on goods and services through its stimulus programs and unemployment benefits. This increases public income and government debt. However, the economy's overall debt burden is decreasing. This is a very risky time. Policymakers must balance the four ways in which debt burdens decline. Deflationary methods must balance with inflationary methods to maintain stability. If balanced correctly, there can be a nice deleveraging effect. Now deleveraging can be horrible or wonderful. How can deleveraging be beautiful? While deleveraging is a challenging situation, managing it as best as possible is wonderful. Much nicer than debt-fueled, unbalanced excesses of the leverage phase. In a successful deleveraging, debt decreases relative to income, real economic growth is positive, and inflation is not a problem. It was achieved with the right balance. The right balance requires some combination of spending cuts, debt reduction, wealth transfer, and money printing so that economic and social stability can be maintained. People ask whether printing money will increase inflation. No, if it compensates for the decline in credit. Remember, the expense is what matters. A dollar of spending paid for with money has the same effect on price as a dollar of spending paid for with credit. By printing money, the central bank can compensate for the disappearance of credit with an increase in the quantity of money. To change things, the central bank needs not only to stimulate income growth, but to increase the rate of income growth more than the interest rate on accumulated debt. So what do I mean by this? Basically, income needs to grow faster than debt. For example: Suppose a country undergoing deleveraging has a debt- to-income ratio of 100%. This means that the amount of debt it has is the same as the amount of income the entire country produces in a year. Now think about the interest rate on this debt, let's say it's 2%. If your debt rises to 2% because of this interest rate and your income only rises about 1%, you will never be able to reduce your debt burden. Enough money needs to be printed to push the rate of income growth above the interest rate. However, money printing is easy to abuse because it is so instant and people prefer it to alternatives. The key is to avoid printing too much money and triggering rampant inflation, as Germany did during deleveraging in the 1920s. If policymakers strike the right balance, deleveraging isn't that dramatic. Growth is slow, but debt burdens are falling. That's a nice deleveraging. As incomes rise, debtors begin to look more creditworthy. And when borrowers appear more creditworthy, lenders start lending again. Debt burdens are finally coming down. By being able to borrow money, people can spend more. Eventually, the economy begins to grow again, leading to the reflation phase of the long-term debt cycle. While the deleveraging process can be horrific, if managed poorly, if managed well, it will eventually solve the problem. It takes about a decade or more for debt burdens to decline and economic activity to return to normal – hence the term “lost decade.” Of course, the economy is a bit more complicated than this model suggests. However, placing the short-term debt cycle on top of the long-term debt cycle and then placing both above the productivity growth line gives a reasonably good model for seeing where we have been, where we are now, and where we are likely going. In summary, there are three rules of thumb I would like you to take away from this. First, don't let your debt rise faster than your income, because your debt burdens will eventually crush you. Second, don't let income rise faster than productivity, because you will eventually become uncompetitive. And third: do everything you can to increase productivity, because, in the long run, that's what matters most. This is simple advice for you and policy makers. You might be surprised, but most people – including the majority of policymakers – don't pay enough attention to this. This model worked for me and I hope it will work for you. Thank you.
