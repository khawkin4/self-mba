Why do some industries mint money while others bleed for decades? And once you've picked a fight, how do you win it? This lesson teaches the two questions at the heart of competitive strategy — where to compete (Porter's Five Forces) and how to be different (positioning, trade-offs, fit) — then puts real numbers behind them with Netflix vs. Disney. Tap the cards, the diagrams, and the quizzes.

1 · Strategy is not "be the biggest" — it's "be the most profitable"

Most people picture competition as a tug-of-war between direct rivals. Porter says that's far too narrow. Tap each idea 👇

You're in a fight for profits with five parties, not one: rivals, customers (who want to pay less), suppliers (who want to charge more), new entrants, and substitutes. Each one can drain profit from your industry.

Porter jokes about "five-star industries" where every force is gentle (soft drinks — "a license to mint money") and "zero-star industries" where every force is brutal (airlines — bottom of the profitability chart for decades). "Sexiness has nothing to do with industry profit."

Profitability is set by the industry's underlying economic structure — not by who has the most market share. Two firms in a great industry can both thrive; two firms in a terrible one can both starve.

"It's not about who's the biggest, it's about who is the most profitable. And profitability is defined by five competitive forces."

— Harvard Business Review, "The Explainer: The 5 Forces That Make Companies Successful" · in your corpus

3 · The Five Forces, drawn

Picture your firm in the center, squeezed by five forces. Strong forces (red, wide) drain profit; weak forces (green) leave it for you. Tap each bar.

"There's Five Star Industries where all the forces are attractive like soft drinks, there's zero Star Industries where all the forces are unfavorable like Airlines."

— Harvard Business Review, "The Five Competitive Forces That Shape Strategy" · in your corpus

Worked example — score the coffee-shop industry (from the Sim Institute lecture):

① Rivalry: HIGH — most cities are flooded with cafes. ② Buyers: medium — individuals can't haggle, but can switch shops and post reviews. ③ Suppliers: LOW — beans, milk, sugar have many interchangeable sources. ④ Entry: HIGH — easy and cheap to open a cafe. ⑤ Substitutes: medium — brewing at home, but few alternatives for "a place to meet friends." Verdict: structurally tough. The fix isn't to compete harder on price — it's to change a force: loyalty cards (lower buyer switching) or differentiation (soften rivalry).

4 · Two ways to win: raise willingness-to-pay or lower willingness-to-sell

Strategy is "a plan to create value." Felix Oberholzer-Gee draws it as a value stick: willingness-to-pay (WTP) at the top, willingness-to-sell (WTS) at the bottom. The gap is the value created — and it splits three ways. Tap each band.

Value created  =  Willingness-to-Pay  −  Willingness-to-Sell

"Strategy's simple. It's a plan to create value. ... Value is the difference between willingness to pay and willingness to sell."

— Harvard Business Review, "What Is Strategy? It's a Lot Simpler Than You Think" · in your corpus

5 · Operational effectiveness is NOT strategy

The most common mistake: confusing running faster with running somewhere different. Being efficient (lean, benchmarked, low-defect) is necessary — but everyone is chasing the same "productivity frontier," and that frontier keeps moving. You're on a treadmill.

"All those companies obsessed with operational effectiveness are sprinting towards that line, but the problem is the line keeps moving."

— CEO Corner Podcast, "What is Strategy? M. Porter | Harvard Business Review Insights" · in your corpus

Real strategy is positioning + trade-offs + fit. Southwest Airlines is the classic case: no assigned seats, no meals, one type of plane, short hops from secondary airports. Each choice is a trade-off, and they all fit — they all serve "low cost." A rival can copy any one piece, but copying the whole interlocking system is what's "very hard."

"It's very hard to copy fit if it's working really well."

— Kunal Cholera, "What is strategy - Michael porter - HBR article summary" · in your corpus

⚠️ The straddling trap

When Continental launched Continental Lite to fight Southwest while keeping its full-service business, it failed. Its hub-and-spoke system delayed the cheap flights; frequent-flyer perks couldn't apply cleanly. Trying to occupy two positions at once meant non-complementary activities and sub-optimal trade-offs — "straddling is potentially very expensive." Saying no is part of the strategy.

6 · Real numbers: Netflix vs. Disney

Same broad industry — entertainment — but very different competitive positions. Disney earns 2× Netflix's revenue ($94.4B vs $45.2B, FY2025), yet look at how much of each sales dollar survives as profit. Tap each bar.

Do the math yourself (real EDGAR figures):

Netflix net margin = 10.98 ÷ 45.18 = 24.3%. Disney net margin = 12.40 ÷ 94.42 = 13.1%.

Return on equity (ROE) = net income ÷ equity. Netflix: 10.98 ÷ 26.62 = 41.2%. Disney: 12.40 ÷ 109.87 = 11.3%. Netflix turns each dollar of owner capital into nearly 4× the profit Disney does.

Return on assets (ROA) = net income ÷ assets. Netflix: 10.98 ÷ 55.60 = 19.7%. Disney: 12.40 ÷ 197.51 = 6.3%. Netflix is asset-light (no theme parks to maintain); Disney's $197.5B asset base must work harder for thinner returns.

Strategy read: Netflix's tight positioning — one global streaming activity system with network effects — produces a structurally higher-margin business than Disney's deliberately broad, capital-heavy portfolio. Different positions, different economics.

7 · Check yourself

An industry has many equal-sized firms competing almost only on price, low barriers to entry, and powerful suppliers. What does the Five Forces model predict?

Strong rivalry + easy entry + powerful suppliers all drain profit — a "zero-star" structure like airlines. Market share doesn't rescue a bad structure.

Continental launched Continental Lite to fight Southwest while keeping full service, and failed. The core lesson is:

Trying to occupy two positions at once created non-complementary activities. Trade-offs and fit are what make a position defensible.

Netflix and Disney FY2025: Netflix earns $10.98B on $45.18B revenue; Disney earns $12.40B on $94.42B. Whose business keeps more of each sales dollar?

Netflix: 10.98 ÷ 45.18 = 24.3%. Disney: 12.40 ÷ 94.42 = 13.1%. More revenue ≠ better margin; competitive position drives profitability.
