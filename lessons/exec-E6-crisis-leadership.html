A crisis is any moment where the stakes are high, the information is incomplete, and the clock is running. The old image of the leader — the all-knowing captain with every answer — actually fails here. This is a hands-on lesson: tap the cards, the diagrams, and the quizzes to learn the playbook real operators, firefighters, and CEOs use when everything is on fire.

1 · The instinct that backfires

Under pressure, almost every leader reaches for the same three things: more control, more certainty, faster answers. The corpus is unanimous that this instinct is a trap. Tap each to see why 👇

Hoarding control turns your team into order-takers who fear mistakes instead of solving problems. The counter-move is empowerment: trust the person closest to the problem to act on it.

When a situation is genuinely new, pretending to know isn't reassuring — it's brittle. Humility ("here's what I know, here's what I don't") paradoxically creates more psychological safety, not less.

Crisis distorts time, pushing you into reactive, impulsive choices. The fix isn't to delay — it's to protect the pause between stimulus and response, the place where judgment lives.

"We follow this new kind of leader through upheaval, because we have confidence not in their map but in their compass."

— TED, "How to lead in a crisis" · in your corpus

3 · Flip the playbook: the four moves

The TED framework distills crisis leadership into four counter-intuitive moves. Notice they are the opposite of the gripping instinct from Section 1:

Humility  +  Urgency  +  Steady Purpose  +  Give Power Away

"Amidst upheaval, leaders must share what they know and admit what they don't know. Paradoxically, that honesty creates more psychological safety for people, not less."

— TED, "How to lead in a crisis" · in your corpus

In the wild: When the pandemic erased airline demand overnight, Delta CEO Ed Bastian increased employee communication despite having almost no clarity — at one point losing over $100 million a day. His rule: it is more important to communicate when you don't have the answers than when you do.

New Zealand PM Jacinda Ardern set a four-level COVID alert system early, placed it at level two, then moved it to four two days later as cases rose — acting on incomplete information, then updating. Her steady purpose throughout: protecting human life, even as the goal shifted from preventing illness to bolstering the economy.

4 · When the clock is the enemy: do the next fastest thing

The CIA's answer to overload is brutally simple. First, know your limit — and stay under it:

Tasks you think you can do at once  −  2  =  what you should actually take on

If you cross the line anyway and hit task saturation, you can't think your way out by solving the hardest problem. You climb out one fast win at a time. Tap each step:

"However many tasks you think you can confidently carry out simultaneously, subtract two."

— Big Think, "The CIA method for making quick decisions under stress" · in your corpus

⚠️ Beware head trash

Once you're overloaded and stop thinking rationally, the brain's freed-up capacity turns on you — "I should never have done this," "I'll never get this right." This head trash convinces you to quit at exactly the moment a single fast task would restart your momentum. Name it, ignore it, and do the next simplest thing.

5 · How experts actually decide under fire

You might assume good crisis decisions come from carefully weighing options. Gary Klein studied firefighters and found the opposite: experts don't compare options at all. They recognize the situation from years of patterns, then simulate one action in their head. If they like how it plays out, they go.

The classic "list every option, score every criterion" method takes 30+ minutes — time a burning building doesn't give you. Experts pattern-match in seconds: "Oh, this is one of those situations — I know how to handle it." They evaluate by mental simulation, not comparison.

Klein says failures cluster at the team level, not the individual. You see one cue, I see another; you assume I know something I don't. Common ground erodes silently until someone misreads the whole situation. The fix is practicing together and watching for early signs of confusion.

"Most of the time we're in this mode even for tough decisions — we're using our pattern matching abilities to size up the situation."

— Lootok, "How can leaders make good decisions under a crisis? — Gary Klein" · in your corpus

6 · The hidden enemy in long crises: a tired brain

Even great judgment degrades with sheer volume of choices. In a 2011 study, imprisoned people who met the parole board in the morning were far more likely to be granted parole than those reviewed in the afternoon — same crimes, same sentences. The difference was decision fatigue: by afternoon the judges were depleted, more impulsive, less confident.

So protect the decision-maker, not just the decision. Make fewer choices per day, remove rote decisions, and reserve energy for what truly matters. One trick from the research: imagine the decision is someone else's before weighing how it hits you — advising is less draining than choosing.

"Imprisoned people who met with the board in the morning were far more likely to receive parole than those whose cases were reviewed in the afternoon, even if their crimes and sentences were practically identical."

— TED-Ed, "How to make smart decisions more easily" · in your corpus
