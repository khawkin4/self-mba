When you stop doing the work and start running the people who do the work, the rules change. This lesson distills Andy Grove's "High Output Management" — the manual that shaped a generation of CEOs — plus the leaders who separate managing from leading. It's hands-on: hover the cards, scan the diagrams, and take the quizzes. The big idea: at scale, your output is no longer what you produce — it's what your organization produces.

1 · The one equation that redefines your job

A new manager's instinct is to measure themselves by their own activity — meetings run, emails sent, decks built. Grove says that's a trap. Those are activities, not output. Click to see the shift

Not the courses you taught or the commitments you negotiated — those are activities. Your output is the result your team delivers. A wafer-plant manager's output is finished wafers; a school principal's output is educated students. You are measured by their results, not your effort.

A manager's output  =  output of your team  +  output of the teams you influence

This is the heart of leading at scale: the "+ teams you influence" term is what grows as you rise. A manager of managers produces almost nothing with their own hands — their entire output flows through other people.

"a manager's output equals the output of the managers organization plus the output of the neighboring organizations that are under the managers influence"

— Superobservation, "HIGH OUTPUT MANAGEMENT PT 1" · in your corpus

2 · Leverage — the multiplier on everything you do

If your output flows through others, the key question becomes: which of my activities produce the most output per hour? Grove calls this leverage. A high-leverage activity touches many people or much output for a small amount of your time. A low-leverage activity does the opposite.

"the single most important resource that we allocate from one day to the next is our only time"

— Superobservation, "HIGH OUTPUT MANAGEMENT PT 1" · in your corpus

His signature example: the one-on-one. Ninety minutes every two weeks shapes a subordinate's work for the full two weeks — about 80+ hours of output. That's a roughly 50-to-1 return on your time. Watch the leverage

"90 minutes of your time can enhance the quality of your subordinates work for two weeks or for some 80 plus hours"

— Superobservation, "HIGH OUTPUT MANAGEMENT PT 1" · in your corpus

4 · Manage things, lead people

Grove gives you the machinery of scale. The leadership lecturers add the human half: the two jobs are different, and most people get promoted into the second without ever being trained for it.

Sinek's observation: senior leaders can be visionary and frontline workers eager — yet the org still fails. The break is in middle management, the hardest job there is. Middle managers must be partly strategic and partly tactical, translating direction down and reality up — with almost no training in how to lead, listen, or give feedback.

"most things break in the middle"

— Simon Sinek, "Why Middle Management is the Hardest Job" · in your corpus

"you can't manage people you can manage a process you can manage a project you can manage a schedule but you lead people"

— Simon Sinek, "Management vs. Leadership: What's the Real Difference?" · in your corpus

5 · Match your style to the person: the supervision spectrum

So how closely should you supervise? Grove's answer is precise and counter-intuitive: it depends on the person's task-relevant maturity — their experience with this specific task, not their age or tenure. Low maturity on a task → supervise closely. High maturity → step back and delegate. Click each zone:

"the most effective management style in a specific instance varies from very close to very loose supervision as the subordinates task maturity increases"

— Superobservation, "HIGH OUTPUT MANAGEMENT PT 1" · in your corpus

Worked example. Maria is a senior engineer (high general seniority) who has just been asked to run her first hiring loop (a brand-new task). Tenure says "delegate." Task-relevant maturity says the opposite: on this task she is a beginner, so you start at the left of the spectrum — structured, close, hands-on. As she runs her third loop, you slide right toward delegation. Same person, different task, different style.

6 · Grove's deeper lesson: treat management like production

Before the people-skills, Grove insists managers borrow the discipline of manufacturing. In his "breakfast factory," you build the workflow around the limiting step — the egg, because it takes longest — and stagger everything else around it. The same logic scales: when Intel recruited graduates, the costly limiting step was the candidate's plant visit, so they used phone screens first to protect it. Find your limiting step; design the flow around it.

The new-manager trap: doing instead of multiplying

Freshly promoted managers keep doing the old job they were great at, because it feels productive. But your individual output is now a rounding error next to your team's. Every hour you spend doing a report's task is an hour not spent on the high-leverage work — the 1-on-1, the clear objective, the unblocking decision — that lifts the whole team. Activity is not output.

7 · A real-world dilemma from the field

From the practitioner corpus: a team lead's report refuses to attend 1-on-1s, insisting they already talk daily so the meeting is "useless." Grove would frame this directly: the daily chatter is reactive firefighting; the 1-on-1 is the high-leverage, scheduled space for teaching, context, and surfacing problems before they explode. Skipping it doesn't save time — it quietly removes your single biggest point of leverage over that person's output.

"One of my reports refuses to join 1:1s, says we talk daily. How to i handle this?"

— r/Leadership thread · in your corpus

8 · Check yourself

According to Grove, what is a manager's true "output"?

Meetings and hours are activities. Output is the result that flows through your organization and the neighboring teams under your influence.

A 25-year veteran is assigned a task they have never done before. Where should you START on the supervision spectrum?

Style follows task-relevant maturity, not tenure. On a brand-new task even a veteran is a beginner, so begin with close, structured supervision and loosen as they master it.

Sinek draws the line between management and leadership as:

Management handles things — processes, projects, schedules. Leadership is the human responsibility to see the people around you rise.

One subtlety → your bridge to the next course

Leverage and the 1-on-1 keep an existing business running well. But Grove's other warning is that the ground itself can shift: a strategic inflection point — a "10x force" — can make your whole way of competing obsolete overnight. Leading at scale eventually means sensing those shifts before the numbers do. That move from running the machine to redirecting the strategy is the territory of the next exec course.

"Strategic inflection point is my way of visualizing and describing a fundamental change"

— MIT Video Productions, "Andrew Grove, 'Strategic Inflection Points'" · in your corpus

Your move

Audit one week of your calendar (or your manager's). Tag each block high-leverage (1-on-1s, setting clear objectives, unblocking decisions, training) or low-leverage (doing a report's task yourself, status meetings with no decision). Then, for each direct report, write their task-relevant maturity on their current main task — and decide whether to supervise closer or delegate more. That two-column list is the output of this lesson.
