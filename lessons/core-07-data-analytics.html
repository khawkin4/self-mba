"Given what usually happens, what should I expect?" Before trusting your own analysis, ask what happened to everyone else in this same situation. Then think in ranges and probabilities, not single guesses.

3 · Why randomization is the magic ingredient

Imagine you redesign your homepage and conversions go up. Did the redesign cause it? Maybe — but payday, weather, a competitor's outage, and another team's ad campaign all moved at the same time. Those are confounders. Randomization splits users by coin-flip, so all those hidden factors land equally in both groups. The only thing left different is your change.

Correlation ("rose after")  ≠  Causation ("rose because")

"experimentation is the least arrogant method of gaining knowledge"

— Wharton Human-AI Research, "The Art & Science of A/B Testing for Business Decisions" · in your corpus

4 · The five steps of a clean experiment

The lecturer lays out a repeatable process. Skip a step and your result is suspect.

1. Hypotheses — define the versions you'll test (e.g. orange button vs. green button).
2. Evaluation criteria — pick your one metric (e.g. conversion rate) before you start, so you can't cherry-pick later.
3. Sample size / duration — decide how long it runs up front.
4. Run it — randomly assign each visitor to a version and record what they do.
5. Evaluate — judge against the criteria you set, then ship the winner.

The p-value trap

Most tools report "95% confidence" when the p-value hits 0.05 — and that phrasing is misleading. A p-value answers a backwards question: "if there were no real difference, how often would I see a result this extreme?" It is not the probability that A beats B. In the lecturer's large e-commerce dataset, a p-value of 0.05 corresponded to only a ~42% chance of a true difference. If you genuinely need high confidence, demand p-values closer to 0.001 — not 0.05.

6 · The other engine: base rates & the outside view

Experiments tell you what works when you can run a test. But many decisions can't be A/B tested — you only get one shot. For those, the corpus offers a second tool from investor Michael Mauboussin: anchor on the base rate.

Your instinct is to gather facts about this case and project forward — the inside view. The base rate forces the outside view: treat your situation as one instance of a larger reference class and ask what happened to everyone else.

Your inside view says a hot startup trading at 20× sales has amazing growth and is worth buying. The outside view asks: "Historically, what returns did investors get on ALL companies trading at 20× sales or more?" The answer is sobering — that reference class has performed poorly. You might still be right, but the base rate tells you the odds you're fighting.

"I'm going to basically ask what happened when other people or organizations were in this position before"

— Excess Returns, "Practical Lessons from Michael Mauboussin" · in your corpus

Pair the base rate with expected-value thinking: don't forecast a single number. Build a bear / base / bull set of scenarios and weight them by probability — because, as Mauboussin puts it, "more things can happen than will happen."

7 · Check yourself

Conversions rose the week after you redesigned the homepage. Why can't you conclude the redesign caused it?

"Rose after" is correlation. Without randomly splitting users, you can't separate the redesign from every other factor moving at the same time. That's why randomization is the gold standard.

Your A/B tool reports a p-value of 0.05 as "95% confident." What does the lecturer warn?

A p-value answers a backwards question. For true high confidence you need values closer to 0.001 — "95% confidence" from p=0.05 overstates how sure you really are.

You have one month of traffic. Which approach does the corpus favor for maximizing results?

In the optimization mindset, big effects show up fast and small ones barely matter. More quick experiments beat one slow precise one — you're hunting for the rare big winner.

One subtlety → your bridge to the rest of the program

Notice both engines fight the same enemy: overconfidence in a single story. A/B testing replaces "I know which design is best" with a coin-flip and a measurement; base rates replace "this time is different" with "what usually happens?" That humility — thinking in probabilities and ranges — is exactly the discipline you'll carry into Marketing (testing campaigns), Strategy (scenario planning), and any decision where the data is noisy.
