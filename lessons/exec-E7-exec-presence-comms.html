You can be the smartest person in the room and still get passed over. Executive presence is the signal you send about whether you're ready for the next big thing — and unlike raw talent, it can be learned. This is a hands-on lesson: tap the cards, the diagrams, and the quizzes. We'll build it from the research and the practitioners who teach it.

1 · What executive presence actually is

It is not about doing your job well — that's a given. It's about how you come across. Tap each idea 👇

Presence is what you signal about your preparedness for the next chance — not whether you hit the numbers. Researcher Sylvia Ann Hewlett: it's "not about whether you really do deliver the goods… it's about what you signal about your preparedness for the next big chance."

Coach Kshitij Sharma defines it as "the impact that a leader creates in its environment" — the feeling people are left with. Kara Ronin frames it as how you show up, how you communicate, and how you make others feel.

Its absence "can severely limit your career progression." Sharma was told flatly by his manager: people admire your work but "they hardly remember you." That diagnosis was "you lack executive presence."

3 · The three pillars — and which one wins

Hewlett's team surveyed hundreds of senior leaders across 14 sectors and found presence rests on three pillars. The surprise: their weights are wildly uneven. Gravitas dominates; appearance is a sliver. Tap each bar.

"You can have the most amazing heft but if you can't be compelling in how you talk about it it remains locked up, becomes a wealth kept secret."

— Talks at Google, "Executive Presence | Sylvia Ann Hewlett" · in your corpus

4 · Competence isn't enough — the 2×2 of presence

Sharma maps presence on two axes: your real internal growth (how much you've actually developed) versus your comfort projecting it (whether you can talk about it). Most stuck high-performers live in the bottom-right — strong, but invisible. Tap each quadrant.

Worked example — the "wolf" who became an "elephant." Sharma coached a VP stuck for eight years: brilliant and respected, but 360° feedback revealed colleagues found him so blunt one labeled him a wolf. He consciously shifted from an aggressive to a relaxed, polite style — "from a wolf… to an elephant, elegant yet powerful" — and was promoted within a year. Presence is a habit you can re-shape.

"Great leaders are never hesitant to talk about their great work to the world."

— TEDx Talks, "Demystifying Executive Presence | Kshitij Sharma" · in your corpus

5 · Communication is the way in

If gravitas is the prize, communication is how you earn it. The modern bar is the "mini-TED-Talk": concise, compelling, seemingly extemporaneous. Hewlett warns that leaning on notes and slides is "the kiss of death." Tap to unpack what great delivery looks like 👇

Posture, how you sit and stand, and removing barriers — podiums, notes, devices — between you and the audience. Then: "eye contact, eye contact, eye contact."

Hewlett's top communicator was Sheryl Sandberg — clear message plus a sliver of her own struggle, so people relate. The caution: share a little, but "you probably shouldn't overshare."

Hewlett confesses she was once "a very boring professor" clinging to 3×5 cards, who fixed it by studying her own bad interview and practicing "like crazy." Ronin agrees: people with presence "have spent years honing" it.

6 · The audience-first flip (and beating self-doubt)

Ronin's most practical move: stop running a me-first mental script ("how do I look? am I being judged?") and switch to an audience-first one ("what do I want them to think and feel about my message?"). It directs your delivery and it starves self-doubt of attention.

How it works in practice. Want the room to feel urgency? You focus on being compelling. Want them inspired? You work on being inspiring. Because the spotlight is on the message and the audience — not on you — there's no room left for the "am I good enough?" spiral that, Ronin notes, leaks straight into closed body language and swallowed ideas.

"Some people do say that executive presence has nothing to do with you, but it has everything to do with how others experience you."

— Kara Ronin, "How to Develop Executive Presence for Senior Leaders & Directors" · in your corpus

Which mindset shift does Ronin say both directs your delivery AND defuses self-doubt?

Focusing on what you want the audience to think and feel moves the spotlight off yourself — so there's no room for "am I being judged?" while also guiding how you show up.

7 · Think fast: the spontaneous-speaking process

Stanford's Matt Abrahams teaches that most real-world speaking is spontaneous — and that little adjustments beat striving for greatness. First manage anxiety, then run a simple process. Tap each stage.

What?  →  So What?  →  Now What?

State what it is, why it matters, and the next step. It works for answering a question, giving feedback, even introducing someone (swap "what" for "who"). A second go-to: Problem → Solution → Benefit.

"Structure sets you free... if you have that structure then you are free to think about what you are going to say."

— Stanford Graduate School of Business, "Think Fast, Talk Smart: Communication Techniques" · in your corpus

⚠️ The trap: striving for greatness freezes you

Abrahams' maxim is "dare to be dull." When you set greatness as the target in a spontaneous moment, "you over-evaluate, you over-analyze, you freeze up." Aim to simply respond well; the polish comes from the structure and from practice — not from white-knuckling perfection.

8 · Check yourself

In Hewlett's research, which pillar of executive presence matters most?

Gravitas — confidence, poise, decisiveness, grace under fire — is "the biggie" across every sector. Communication is second; appearance is the smallest.

A talented manager does great work but "nobody remembers them." On Sharma's 2×2, where are they?

Mild presence — real depth trapped by an unwillingness to convey it. The fix is comfort projecting your competence, not more competence.

According to Abrahams, why does using a structure like "What? So What? Now What?" help?

Structure boosts processing fluency (~40%) for both speaker and audience — "structure sets you free" to focus on content, not scaffolding. Anxiety is managed, never erased.

🔎 One subtlety → your bridge to the next course

Notice the research's recurring undertone: presence is judged through a biased lens. Hewlett found the same forceful "showing teeth" reads as strong on a man and "abrasive" on a woman; tears that humanize a man can undermine a woman. Presence isn't only a personal skill — it interacts with how organizations perceive leadership. That tension between authentic self and organizational expectation is the doorway to Leading Teams & Influence (Exec track).

🎯 Your move

Pick a recurring spontaneous moment you dread — a status update, a cold call, a Q&A. Write a one-line answer using What? So What? Now What? Then record yourself delivering it once (Ronin's "analyze how you show up"): watch only your eye contact, posture, and whether your hands are open or closed. Re-record once. That 10-minute loop — structure + self-review — is exactly how presence is built.
