1 · The teardown playbook — outside in, then inside out

A company analysis is not a pile of facts. It's a sequence: understand the world the business lives in first, then judge the numbers in that light. Tap each stage 👇

What forces act on the company that it can influence but not control? Politics, economics, technology, regulation — and the five competitive forces of its industry. This becomes the Opportunities & Threats half of your SWOT.

What does the company have and do — its physical, financial and human resources, and its actual way of winning? This becomes the Strengths & Weaknesses half of your SWOT.

The income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement are the scoreboard of the strategy. Strong margins and clean cash are the fingerprint of a real competitive advantage; weak ones expose a strategy that only sounds good.

Finally, ask what those future cash flows are worth (intrinsic value) and whether today's price gives you a margin of safety. A great business at a terrible price is a bad investment.

3 · Plan vs. strategy — the trap every teardown must avoid

Before judging a company, be sure you've found its strategy, not just its to-do list. A plan is a set of activities you control (build a plant, hire people). A strategy is a theory of how you'll win customers — who you can't control.

Strategy  =  Where to play  +  How to win

"a strategy is an integrated set of choices that positions you on a playing field of your choice in a way that you win."

— Harvard Business Review, "A Plan Is Not a Strategy" · in your corpus

While big airlines planned routes, Southwest had a theory of winning: fly point-to-point, one aircraft type (737s), no meals, book direct — all reinforcing a structurally lower cost so it could undercut everyone. That coherence is strategy. Berkshire's version: own businesses with moats, let cash compound, and allocate it centrally.

4 · Apply it: Berkshire's strategy in one breath

Berkshire Hathaway is a holding company. Its strategy is an integrated set of choices: collect cheap, long-duration cash ("float") from its insurance businesses, buy whole companies and stocks with durable moats, and let one tiny central team allocate all the capital. Map it to the value stick — it raises willingness-to-pay through trusted brands (GEICO, BNSF, See's) and lowers cost through scale and cheap float.

6 · What the numbers actually tell you

Net margin = 66.97 ÷ 371.44 = 18.0% → fat for a conglomerate that includes a railroad and utilities, a fingerprint of pricing power across its moated businesses.

Return on Equity = 66.97 ÷ 717.42 = 9.3% and Return on Assets = 66.97 ÷ 1,222.18 = 5.5% → solid, and earned with very little leverage (Assets ÷ Equity = just 1.70×, vs. 10×+ for a typical bank).

Earnings-quality check: operating cash flow was $45.97B — meaningfully below the $66.97B net income. For most firms that's a red flag, but for Berkshire it's the opposite: accounting rules force unrealized gains on its huge stock portfolio through net income, inflating it in up years. Cash flow is the steadier read. Always reconcile profit to cash.

⚠️ Don't do the SWOT first — and don't read net income naively

Two capstone traps. First, a SWOT done before the external and internal analysis is just guessing — "do the other frameworks first, do this one last." Second, for holding companies like Berkshire, headline net income is distorted by mark-to-market swings in its stock portfolio. The same discipline as Course 01: profit is an opinion, cash is a fact.

7 · Check yourself

In a SWOT, where does "a new government tariff on imports" belong for the company being analyzed?

A tariff is an external force the firm can influence but not control — it lives in Opportunities/Threats, here a Threat. Internal/external is the line you must never blur.

Berkshire's equity is $717.4B and assets are $1,222.2B. Roughly how leveraged is it (Assets ÷ Equity)?

1,222.2 ÷ 717.4 ≈ 1.70× — owners fund most of the balance sheet. Very low leverage, the "fortress" Berkshire is known for.

Berkshire's net income ($67.0B) is well above its operating cash flow ($46.0B). What's the most likely capstone explanation?

Accounting rules push mark-to-market unrealized gains through net income, inflating it in good years. Cash flow strips that out — which is why you always reconcile the two.

🔎 The last move — value vs. price

You've now scanned the market, mapped the strategy, and read the numbers — but you're not done. The final step is valuation: forecast the cash these businesses throw off, discount it back (Course 02), and compare your intrinsic value to the market price. Buy only with a margin of safety. A wonderful business at a foolish price is still a foolish purchase — that judgment is where every other course you've taken converges.
