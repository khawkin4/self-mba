Most business advice stops at "be data-driven." This lesson is the math underneath that slogan. Four ideas do almost all the work in applied quant: a fitted line, an R², a p-value, and a confidence interval. Together they answer two questions every operator faces — "how are these two things related?" and "is this difference real, or did I just get lucky with my sample?" We'll build all four StatQuest-style: least squares fits a line by rotating until the squared errors are smallest; R² says how much that line explains; the p-value scores how surprised you should be if nothing's really going on; the confidence interval draws that test as a picture. Real numbers, real formulas, no hand-waving. Tap the cards, the regression line, and the quizzes as you go.

1 · Regression is just fitting a line — by least squares

Strip away the jargon and linear regression is fitting a straight line through a cloud of points so you can predict one thing from another. StatQuest's running example: you measure a bunch of mice — their weight and their size — and you want weight to predict size. The recipe is three steps, and only the first one fits the line:

"The first thing you do in linear regression is use least squares to fit a line to the data. The second thing you do is calculate R squared. Lastly, calculate a p-value for R squared."

— StatQuest, "Linear Regression, Clearly Explained!!!" · in your corpus

How does "least squares" actually find the line? You draw a line, measure the vertical gap from each point to the line — that gap is called a residual — square each one, and add them up. Then you rotate the line a hair and do it again. Keep rotating; plot the total against the angle; pick the rotation with the smallest sum of squared residuals. That's the fit.

"Terminology alert: the distance from the line to the data point is called a residual… rotate the line a little bit, measure the residuals, square them, and then sum up the squares… lastly you find the rotation that has the least sum of squares."

— StatQuest, "Linear Regression, Clearly Explained!!!" · in your corpus

The output is an equation for a line, ŷ = intercept + slope · x. Least squares hands you two numbers: a y-intercept and a slope. The slope is the whole point — it's how much y moves for each one-unit move in x. Tap the line and the residuals below.

2 · R² — how much does the line actually explain?

A line always exists, but is it any good? R² answers that in one number between 0 and 1. StatQuest builds it from variation. First, ignore weight entirely and just take the average size — measure how far every point sits from that flat average line, square those gaps, and call the total SS(mean). Then measure the gaps from the points to your fitted line and call that SS(fit). R² is the share of variation the line removed:

R² = ( SS(mean) − SS(fit) ) / SS(mean)

"In this example the variation around the mean equals 11.1 and the variation around the fit equals 4.4… the result is that R squared equals 0.6, which is the same thing as saying 60 percent. This means… mouse weight explains 60 percent of the variation in mouse size."

— StatQuest, "Linear Regression, Clearly Explained!!!" · in your corpus

Run the numbers: (11.1 − 4.4) / 11.1 = 6.7 / 11.1 ≈ 0.60. So weight explains 60% of why some mice are bigger than others; the other 40% is everything you didn't measure (diet, genes, luck). R² = 1 means a perfect fit; R² = 0 means the line is no better than guessing the average. But — and this is the third step everyone skips — a high R² on a tiny sample could be a fluke. That's why regression ends with a p-value for R²: the chance you'd see an R² this big if there were really no relationship at all. Which means we need to understand p-values.

3 · Hypothesis testing — start by assuming nothing's going on

Here's the mental move that confuses everyone the first time: in statistics you don't try to prove your idea is right. You set up a null hypothesis — "there is no difference, nothing's going on" — and then see whether the data is weird enough to make you abandon it. StatQuest dramatizes it with two drugs whose recovery times keep flipping:

"After doing all of these repeated experiments where we double-checked every little step, we can confidently reject this hypothesis… But when we have data that is similar to the hypothesis but not exactly the same, the best we can do is fail to reject the hypothesis."

— StatQuest, "Hypothesis Testing and The Null Hypothesis" · in your corpus

Two outcomes only: reject the null (the data is too surprising for "nothing's going on" to survive) or fail to reject it (the data is consistent with chance — note you never "accept" or "prove" the null, you just couldn't knock it down). This asymmetry is the grammar of every A/B test, every clinical trial, every "is this campaign actually working?" question.

Because absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence. When drug C's repeated experiments gave 12 hours, then 13.5 hours, those results were different from the 13-hour hypothesis but also from each other — not different enough to reject, but not tight enough to confirm 13 either. The honest verdict is "I couldn't rule out chance," which is weaker than "they're identical." A bigger, cleaner sample might still reveal a real effect. Saying "no difference" claims more than your data earned.

4 · The p-value — how surprised should you be?

The p-value puts a number on "how surprising." Formally: if the null were true (no real difference), how often would you see a result at least this extreme, just from random noise? Small p = "this would rarely happen by chance" = reject the null. The single most-used threshold is 0.05:

"The threshold value actually used most often is 0.05… if there is no difference between drug A and drug B, only 5% of the trials will have a p-value less than 0.05. This 5% experiment (wrong experiment) is called a false positive."

— StatQuest, "p-values: What they are and how to interpret them" · in your corpus

So p < 0.05 doesn't mean "95% sure I'm right." It means: if nothing were going on, I'd only see data this extreme 5% of the time — surprising enough to act on. And 0.05 isn't sacred. It's a cost-of-being-wrong dial. Tap each tier.

Worked example — your landing-page A/B test. The old page converts at 4.0%; the new one shows 4.6% over a week. Real lift or noise? Frame it StatQuest-style. Null hypothesis: "the two pages convert identically; the 0.6-point gap is random." Pick your threshold first: a page change is reversible and low-cost, so 0.05 is fine. Run the test (a two-proportion test) and it returns p = 0.03. Read it literally: if the pages were truly identical, you'd see a gap this big only 3% of the time. That's below 0.05, so you reject the null and ship the new page — while remembering you've just accepted a 1-in-33 chance you're chasing noise. Now flip it: if p had come back 0.24, you'd fail to reject — "a difference this size happens ~24% of the time by chance, I can't tell it from luck." You don't declare the pages equal; you keep the old one and, if it matters, gather more data. Same logic StatQuest used to say "p = 0.24, so we cannot be certain there is a difference between drug A and drug B."

5 · Confidence intervals — the hypothesis test, drawn as a picture

A p-value is a verdict; a confidence interval is the same verdict you can see. A 95% confidence interval is simply the range that captures 95% of plausible values for the true number you're estimating. StatQuest's punchline:

"Confidence intervals are useful because they are statistical tests performed visually. Because the interval covers 95% of the means… anything outside of it occurs less than 5% of the time — that is to say, the p-value of anything outside of the confidence interval is less than 0.05 and thus significantly different."

— StatQuest, "Confidence Intervals, Clearly Explained!!!" · in your corpus

So a CI is a built-in significance test: anything outside the 95% interval has p < 0.05. Comparing two groups? The eyeball rule: if their 95% intervals don't overlap, you know they're significantly different. But there's a catch worth memorizing:

"If the confidence intervals overlap, there's still a chance that the means are significantly different from each other, so in this case you still have to do the t-test. But when the confidence intervals do not overlap, then you can rest assured that there's a statistically significant difference."

— StatQuest, "Confidence Intervals, Clearly Explained!!!" · in your corpus

Non-overlap is conclusive (significant); overlap is inconclusive (run the real test). And a 99% interval is wider than a 95% one — more confidence costs you precision. That trade-off is the whole personality of statistics.

7 · Two jobs regression does: measure, and forecast

Why care about all this? Because regression does two distinct jobs, and confusing them is a classic error. The business-stats corpus draws the line cleanly:

"Regression analysis can be used to achieve two goals: you can measure the influence of one variable or several variables on another variable, or you can predict a variable based on other variables."

— "Regression Analysis: An Easy and Clear Beginner's Guide" · in your corpus

Job one is explanation: does parents' education influence a child's outcomes — and by how much? Job two is forecasting: given a new store's local competition and nearby population, what profit should we expect? Same machinery, different question. And a sharp test of whether you understand regression is knowing when the answer should be "no relationship at all":

"Another example that I hope you find odd is the relationship between a student's height and that same student's exam scores. We should expect no relationship to exist — and we can use regression models to test that."

— "Introduction to Simple Linear Regression" · in your corpus

A flat slope and an R² near zero is a real, useful finding: height does not predict test scores. Statistics earns its keep as much by killing false stories as by confirming true ones.

8 · Check yourself

Your regression of size on weight gives R² = 0.60. What does that mean?

R² = (SS(mean) − SS(fit)) / SS(mean) = (11.1 − 4.4)/11.1 ≈ 0.60. It's the share of variation in Y the line accounts for — not a confidence level and not a count of points on the line. Whether it's statistically significant is a separate p-value.

Your A/B test returns p = 0.24 at a 0.05 threshold. The right call is:

p = 0.24 > 0.05, so you fail to reject the null. Exactly StatQuest's "p = 0.24, so we cannot be certain there is a difference." You don't prove they're equal — you just couldn't distinguish the result from random noise.

Two groups' 95% confidence intervals do NOT overlap. You can conclude:

Non-overlapping 95% CIs guarantee p < 0.05 — "you can rest assured there's a statistically significant difference." (Significance isn't causation, though.) The catch runs the other way: if they DO overlap, it's inconclusive and you still need the t-test.

⚠️ "Statistically significant" ≠ "important," and p < 0.05 ≠ "95% sure I'm right"

Two errors sink most data-driven decisions. First, the misread p-value: p < 0.05 does not mean "95% probability my hypothesis is true." It means "if nothing were going on, I'd see data this extreme only 5% of the time." Subtle, but it's the difference between sound and superstitious reasoning. Second, significance is not magnitude: with a huge sample, a conversion lift of 0.001% can be "statistically significant" and still worthless to the business — always look at the effect size (the slope, the actual gap), not just whether p cleared a line. And remember the false-positive math: run twenty independent tests at 0.05 and, by chance alone, about one will look "significant." Mine enough data and you'll always find a sparkle that's really just noise.

🔎 One subtlety → your bridge to the rest of the MBA

Notice the throughline: every tool here exists to separate signal from sampling noise. You never see the whole population — only a sample — so the real question is always "would this pattern survive if I drew a different sample?" That single idea is the hidden engine under the rest of the shelf: a finance NPV is a forecast with an unstated confidence interval; a marketing test is a null hypothesis about whether the campaign moved anything; an ops control chart is a p-value drawn in real time; the behavioral-decision lessons warn you that humans see signal in pure noise (the gambler's fallacy, the hot hand). The deepest quant skill isn't computing a p-value — it's the reflex to ask, before you trust any number, "how much of this could just be luck?"

🎯 Your move

Grab one real relationship you suspect in your work (ad spend → signups, headcount → output, price → churn). On paper or in a spreadsheet: (1) name your X and Y — which is the predictor, which is the outcome? (2) Plot the points and let the tool fit a least-squares line; read the slope (does Y really move with X?) and the R² (how much does X explain — 0.1 or 0.8?). (3) Write the null hypothesis out loud ("X has no effect on Y"), pick your threshold before looking (0.05 unless the stakes are unusual), and check the p-value and the confidence interval on the slope — does the interval include zero? If it does, you can't rule out "no effect." You'll almost always discover either a relationship you can now quantify, or a story you were telling yourself that the data won't support.
