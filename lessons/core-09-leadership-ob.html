Leadership isn't a title — it's a set of behaviors that change how people around you think, speak, and risk. This lesson teaches the two ideas that show up in almost every modern leadership talk: building a workplace where people feel safe to speak up, and making the painful shift from doing the work to leading the people who do it. Tap the cards, the diagrams, and the quiz as you go.

1 · The moment that defines a workplace

Picture a nurse on a night shift. She thinks a drug dose looks too high. Does she call the doctor — or stay silent? Tap each to reveal what's really happening 👇

Nobody wants to look ignorant, incompetent, intrusive, or negative. Staying quiet is the safe move for your image. Edmondson calls this impression management — and we get good at it by grade school.

Every withheld question or concern is a lost moment of learning. The org misses a game-changing idea, or an early warning of a threat — because someone "felt unable to bring the bad news to their boss."

A shared belief that it's expected to speak up with questions, concerns, ideas, and mistakes — a "sense of permission for candour." NOT about being nice or avoiding conflict. It's the means to a goal: excellence.

"no one wakes up in the morning and jumps out of bed because they can't wait to get to work today to look ignorant incompetent intrusive or negative"

— TEDx Talks, "Building a psychologically safe workplace | Amy Edmondson" · in your corpus

4 · How a leader actually builds safety

This isn't vague. Edmondson gives three concrete moves any leader — or any teammate — can make:

1 · Frame the work as a learning problem, not an execution problem. Make the uncertainty and interdependence explicit: "We've never been here before — we need everyone's brains in the game." That creates the rationale for speaking up.

2 · Acknowledge your own fallibility. Say plainly: "I may miss something — I need to hear from you." A leader who apologizes for not making it safe in the past creates safety fast.

3 · Model curiosity — ask a lot of questions. Every thoughtful question is "the gift of your interest, the gift of your respect," and it creates a necessity for voice.

Uncertainty  +  Interdependence  ⟹  Psychological Safety is vital

The flip side: if work has no uncertainty and no interdependence, safety is "fun to have but not necessary." Modern knowledge work has both in spades — which is why this matters now.

"you have to distinguish between leader which is a role and leadership which is a behavior... any one of us can exercise leadership at any point"

— Global Leadership Network, "Amy Edmondson on Leading Up & Creating Psychological Safety" · in your corpus

5 · The other big shift: from doing to leading

The second theme across the corpus is a transition almost nobody is trained for. Your career moves through three phases — and each demands a different skill set. Tap each stage.

"why on earth do we think that we can just promote someone to a leadership position and expect that they know what to do without showing them how to do it"

— Simon Sinek, "Why Middle Management is the Hardest Job" · in your corpus

6 · The counter-intuitive part: subtract, don't add

Kara Ronin's framework for stepping up: take stock of what you do now vs. what the next role needs. The surprise — it's often about removing tasks and skills, not piling on more. You stop optimizing individual tasks and start crafting and communicating a vision.

Practical moves she teaches: ask your boss what the organization's goals are, sit in on high-level meetings as an observer, and coach someone junior — because coaching forces you to practice big-picture thinking and mapping paths to a goal.

"you might think that you need to do more or that you need to be more when in reality it's really about removing tasks or removing skills that aren't going to serve you when you are a leader"

— Kara Ronin, "How to Move from Manager to Leader or Executive Level" · in your corpus

⚠️ The "Buddy to Boss" trap

A common failure mode: you get promoted over former peers, then — terrified of seeming arrogant — you shy away from actually managing them. Abbey Louie's fix is three A's: Acknowledge the awkward, Ask for their input, and (the curveball) Act like their manager. Avoiding the authority damages the relationship more than using it does.

📚 From the Canon — Grove, Marquet, Bezos & the power realists

The lesson so far taught the climate of leadership — safety, candour, the doer-to-leader shift. The canon adds the machinery: where one hour of yours moves the most output, how to push decisions to where the information lives, how to scale culture past your own presence — and the uncomfortable truth about who actually holds power. These ten books are the operating manual underneath the behaviors above.

Managerial leverage — your output is your team's output

Grove's High Output Management reframes the manager's job entirely: you are not paid for what you do, you are paid for what your team does. The lever is choosing the few high-leverage acts — 1-on-1s, training, direction — over busy monitoring.

"a manager's output is equal to the output of their organization plus the output of the neighboring organizations under their influence… You are not paid for what you do. You are paid for what your team does. Think of it like a sound system. You are the amplifier."

— Insight Atom, "Andrew Grove's Secret: 10x Your Output with Managerial Leverage" · in your corpus

Grove's most counter-intuitive claim is that training is the highest-leverage act a boss can do — and his 1-on-1 is "the employee's meeting," where you "hear the low-level rumbling before the volcano erupts." Drucker's Effective Executive supplies the personal version: track where your time actually goes, then concentrate on the few contributions only you can make — "first things first, and one at a time."

Intent-based leadership — push authority to the information

Marquet's Turn the Ship Around! replaces leader-follower with leader-leader: a tiny change of language ("I intend to…" instead of "may I…") relocates ownership downward. But it only works on two pillars — competence and clarity.

"another submarine had one guy in charge, one guy giving orders, one guy thinking and 134 people doing what they're told… on my submarine I got 135 thinking, active, passionate, creative, proactive people"

— Leadership Academy, "Leadership on a Submarine" (L. David Marquet) · in your corpus

Mechanisms & OKRs — scaling intent past your presence

Once a team outgrows what you can personally enforce, you need mechanisms, not exhortation. Bezos: "good intentions don't work; mechanisms do." Doerr's OKRs make focus legible; Larson manages orgs by state, not motivation; Walsh obsesses over the daily standard and lets the score follow.

The uncomfortable truth of power

Greene's 48 Laws and Pfeffer's Power are the lesson's missing dark half: leadership behavior runs inside a political system that doesn't reward being right. Pfeffer's diagnosis is that the just-world reflex is what disarms good people.

"the biggest barrier I think for us having power is of course ourselves — we are often times not willing to do what it takes or to make the tradeoffs required"

— Stanford GSB, "Jeffrey Pfeffer: Why Cultivating Power is the Secret to Success" · in your corpus

Greene himself frames the laws as defense, not offense — "a lot of it's about defense, knowing that there are malevolent, aggressive, toxic people out there." Used as a how-to it corrodes the alliances power actually rests on; used as a diagnostic, naming the move strips its power. The honest synthesis: refuse to engage at all and you get out-maneuvered by those who do.
