Leadership isn't a title — it's a set of behaviors that change how people around you think, speak, and risk. This lesson teaches the two ideas that show up in almost every modern leadership talk: building a workplace where people feel safe to speak up, and making the painful shift from doing the work to leading the people who do it. Tap the cards, the diagrams, and the quiz as you go.

1 · The moment that defines a workplace

Picture a nurse on a night shift. She thinks a drug dose looks too high. Does she call the doctor — or stay silent? Tap each to reveal what's really happening 👇

Nobody wants to look ignorant, incompetent, intrusive, or negative. Staying quiet is the safe move for your image. Edmondson calls this impression management — and we get good at it by grade school.

Every withheld question or concern is a lost moment of learning. The org misses a game-changing idea, or an early warning of a threat — because someone "felt unable to bring the bad news to their boss."

A shared belief that it's expected to speak up with questions, concerns, ideas, and mistakes — a "sense of permission for candour." NOT about being nice or avoiding conflict. It's the means to a goal: excellence.

"no one wakes up in the morning and jumps out of bed because they can't wait to get to work today to look ignorant incompetent intrusive or negative"

4 · How a leader actually builds safety

This isn't vague. Edmondson gives three concrete moves any leader — or any teammate — can make:

1 · Frame the work as a learning problem, not an execution problem. Make the uncertainty and interdependence explicit: "We've never been here before — we need everyone's brains in the game." That creates the rationale for speaking up.

2 · Acknowledge your own fallibility. Say plainly: "I may miss something — I need to hear from you." A leader who apologizes for not making it safe in the past creates safety fast.

3 · Model curiosity — ask a lot of questions. Every thoughtful question is "the gift of your interest, the gift of your respect," and it creates a necessity for voice.

Uncertainty  +  Interdependence  ⟹  Psychological Safety is vital

The flip side: if work has no uncertainty and no interdependence, safety is "fun to have but not necessary." Modern knowledge work has both in spades — which is why this matters now.

"you have to distinguish between leader which is a role and leadership which is a behavior... any one of us can exercise leadership at any point"

— Global Leadership Network, "Amy Edmondson on Leading Up & Creating Psychological Safety" · in your corpus

6 · The counter-intuitive part: subtract, don't add

Kara Ronin's framework for stepping up: take stock of what you do now vs. what the next role needs. The surprise — it's often about removing tasks and skills, not piling on more. You stop optimizing individual tasks and start crafting and communicating a vision.

Practical moves she teaches: ask your boss what the organization's goals are, sit in on high-level meetings as an observer, and coach someone junior — because coaching forces you to practice big-picture thinking and mapping paths to a goal.

"you might think that you need to do more or that you need to be more when in reality it's really about removing tasks or removing skills that aren't going to serve you when you are a leader"

— Kara Ronin, "How to Move from Manager to Leader or Executive Level" · in your corpus

⚠️ The "Buddy to Boss" trap

A common failure mode: you get promoted over former peers, then — terrified of seeming arrogant — you shy away from actually managing them. Abbey Louie's fix is three A's: Acknowledge the awkward, Ask for their input, and (the curveball) Act like their manager. Avoiding the authority damages the relationship more than using it does.
