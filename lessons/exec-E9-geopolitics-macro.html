For thirty years, smart executives could mostly ignore world politics — trade flowed, borders blurred, and the cheapest factory on Earth won. That world is over. This lesson teaches you to read geopolitics like a strategist: not as scary headlines, but as a force that swings the value of your company. Tap the cards, the diagrams, and the quizzes as you go.

1 · What "geopolitics" actually means for business

Most people hear "geopolitics" and picture war. That's only a sliver. The strategist's definition is broader 👇

Geopolitics is the competing strategic priorities of nations — and the tools they use to pursue them: trade policy, industrial policy, sanctions, export controls, and yes, sometimes security and conflict. War is the loud part; tariffs and subsidies are the part that quietly reprices your supply chain.

After decades when geopolitics was benign for most global companies, there's been a trend break. Nations are competing harder, and their actions now create big, frequent "value swings" for firms. It's no longer a topic for the foreign-ministry desk — it's a board-level agenda.

"Companies now say leaders now say that this is the number one agenda that they believe could change value or create disruption for their enterprises."

— McKinsey & Company, "How business leaders can proactively navigate geopolitics" · in your corpus

3 · The big map: a multipolar world

For decades the world had one or two superpowers running the show. The strategist's base case for the 2030s is three pulling forces, not one. Think of it as a spectrum from "aligned with the West" to "aligned with the East," with powerful swing players in the middle. Tap each block:

5 · The playbook: five moves to build the muscle

The BCG framework boils the response down to five exercises — a "corporate workout." Tap to reveal each:

Retire the single lowest-cost mega-factory. Replace it with a network of smaller factories that can flex when a pandemic, war, or tariff hits overnight.

The cheap money of the 2010s is gone. Plan investments and pricing for continued inflationary pressure from military spending, energy transition, and fragmentation.

One global HQ no longer works across rival economic systems and tech stacks. Run a set of agile regional command centers that can adapt locally.

In a world of AI and asymmetric warfare, harden at every level — systems, employees, and culture — not just the firewall.

The core move: talent, decision-making, and leadership. Hire people who sense shifts and think in scenarios, and inject a geopolitical lens into every big decision.

"To develop what I call the geopolitical muscle. No worries, I have no shares in any gym chain."

— TED, "5 Ways Leaders Can Adapt to Shifting Geopolitics | Nikolaus S. Lang" · in your corpus

6 · A worked example: the Strait of Hormuz

Watch the chain reaction the macro analysts traced live. A flare-up in the Middle East threatens the Strait of Hormuz — the chokepoint for a huge share of seaborne oil. Follow the dominoes:

① Chokepoint risk → ships fired on, a shipping lane in doubt. ② Commodity shock → crude oil spikes (Brent up ~5% in a session). ③ Inflation channel → pricier energy feeds into inflation. ④ Rates & markets → the Fed looks less likely to cut, long-dated yields hit 2026 highs, and equities fall from records.

One regional conflict, four steps, and suddenly it's on the balance sheet of a company that sells nothing in the region. That is why you can't take geopolitics out of finance.

"You cannot take geopolitics out of finance right now."

— Bloomberg Podcasts, "Business Leaders Talk Geopolitical Risk at Milken | Balance of Power" · in your corpus

⚠️ The #1 trap: confusing "decoupling" with "de-risking"

It's tempting to read every headline as the end of globalization and rip out a rival market entirely. But the data the lecturers cite shows global trade and foreign direct investment are not collapsing — and rivals like the US and China remain conflictually interdependent. The smart move is diversify and add buffers, not blindly decouple from a partner you can't actually replace.

7 · Check yourself

In the strategist's definition, which of these is NOT a normal tool of geopolitics?

Geopolitics is about the tools nations use — trade policy, industrial policy, sanctions, security. A firm's own marketing budget isn't a tool of statecraft.

Why is a multipolar world considered more uncertain than a bipolar one?

More poles = more independent actors (including swing middle powers), so outcomes are harder to predict. The lecturer was explicit that it means more uncertainty, not an inevitable war.

A flare-up near the Strait of Hormuz spikes oil prices. What's the most likely knock-on effect the analysts described?

Pricier energy feeds inflation, which makes the Fed less likely to cut rates — rippling into bond yields and equities far beyond the region.
