Most of the hard calls you've practiced so far had a right answer hiding in the numbers — find it, defend it, move. Ethics is what's left when the spreadsheet runs out. The two marquee books on this shelf split the problem cleanly. Joseph Badaracco's Defining Moments says the decisions that actually test you are not right vs. wrong but right vs. right — two genuine goods that can't both be honored. Michael Sandel's Justice hands you the three lenses every moral argument is secretly using, so you can name which one you're standing in. This lesson builds one habit: before you decide, name the clash and name the lens — then choose like someone who'll read about it on the front page tomorrow. 1 · The decisions that actually test you are right vs. right

The everyday picture of ethics is a fork: the right thing on one side, the profitable (or easy, or safe) thing on the other. Badaracco's wager in Defining Moments is that those are the cheap dilemmas — the ones where you already know the answer and just need nerve. The decisions that reveal and shape character are the ones where both roads are defensible: truth vs. loyalty, the individual vs. the community, the short term vs. the long term, justice vs. mercy. The corpus's ethics-training video puts the same split in operational language:

"Most ethical dilemmas fall into one of two categories: situational or not-so-situational. A situational dilemma is a matter of judgment, moral reasoning and conscience… not-so-situational dilemmas happen despite your best efforts to prevent them… they require a decision-making model that allows you to clarify the situation and offer constructive alternatives."

— Global Ethics Solutions, "Ethical Decision Making: Kinds of Ethical Dilemmas" · in your corpus
Right vs. wrong → you need courage.  Right vs. right → you need judgment.

Badaracco's tools for a right-vs-right call aren't formulas; they're questions of identity. What do my deep feelings tell me? Which option could I defend on the front page of the newspaper — the newspaper test? Which choice best expresses who I am and who I want to become, and which will I be most proud of in five years? The rest of this lesson is about not letting those introspective questions become a mirror that simply flatters what you already wanted.

2 · Sandel's three lenses — what "the right thing" even means

Before you can resolve a clash, you have to see that the people arguing are usually standing in different moral worlds. Sandel's whole Justice course is built to surface three:

(1) Maximize welfare — the greatest good for the greatest number (utilitarianism). (2) Respect freedom — individual rights and consent come before collective outcomes (Kant / libertarianism). (3) Cultivate virtue — what promotes human flourishing and the common good (Aristotle). Most real arguments, Sandel shows, are people talking past each other from different lenses without naming them. He frames the first split as the engine of the course:

"Consequentialist moral reasoning locates morality in the consequences of an act, in the state of the world that will result from the thing you do… Categorical moral reasoning locates morality in certain absolute moral requirements, in certain categorical duties and rights, regardless of the consequences."

— Harvard University (Michael Sandel), Justice Ep. 01 "The Moral Side of Murder" · in your corpus

The skill isn't picking the "correct" lens. It's noticing which one you're using — especially in the moment your intuition flips between two cases that look the same.

3 · The trolley problem — watch your own intuition flip

Here's the experiment Sandel opens with, and it's the cleanest demonstration of the whole field. A runaway trolley will kill five workers. Tap each cell to see what most people choose — and why the same person answers the two versions differently.

"At the end of the day better that five should live even if one must die. That's an example of consequentialist moral reasoning… People thought it was just wrong, categorically wrong, to kill a person, an innocent person, even for the sake of saving five lives."

— Harvard University (Michael Sandel), Justice Ep. 01 "The Moral Side of Murder" · in your corpus

In the lever case the death is a side effect of saving five. In the bridge case the man's body is the instrument — you are using him as a means to an end, which Kant's categorical lens treats as wrong regardless of the good result. Sandel's point isn't that one answer is right; it's that the same person switches lenses without noticing. Once you can feel the switch in your own gut, you can ask the real question: which lens should govern this decision, and why?

4 · Mind your motive — Kant on dignity, and Rawls on a fair start

The rights lens gets its deepest statement from Kant, whom Sandel devotes a full lecture to. For Kant, the worth of an action isn't in its results at all:

"What makes an action morally worthy has to do with the motive, with the quality of the will, with the intention for which the act is done. What matters is the motive, and the motive must be of a certain kind."

— Harvard University (Michael Sandel), Justice Ep. 06 "Mind Your Motive" · in your corpus

This is why doing the right thing for a self-serving reason feels hollow — and why the rights lens forbids using people "as means merely, even for good ends." But it leaves a question welfare and rights both dodge: what makes a fair starting line? Sandel's answer comes from John Rawls' thought experiment — choose society's rules from behind a veil of ignorance, not knowing whether you'll be born rich or poor, strong or weak. Rawls argues you'd refuse pure utilitarianism the moment your own neck was on the line:

"Once the veil goes up and real life begins, we will each want to be respected with dignity — even if we turn out to be a member of a minority, we don't want to be oppressed. And so we would agree to reject utilitarianism."

— Harvard University (Michael Sandel), Justice Ep. 08 "What's a Fair Start?" · in your corpus

The veil of ignorance is a portable governance test: would I accept this policy if I didn't know which side of it I'd land on? It's the rights lens with a fairness engine bolted on.

6 · A process for the not-so-situational call

Lenses diagnose; they don't decide. For the dilemmas that arrive "despite your best efforts," the corpus offers a four-step model — input, study, analyze, choose — and warns that skipping it is how you end up rationalizing. Tap each stage.

"You usually make decisions about situational dilemmas based on what's right for you at the time of the crisis… be aware of those areas in your life that open you up to temptation, and fortify your character and principles to stand up and say no."

— Global Ethics Solutions, "Ethical Decision Making: Kinds of Ethical Dilemmas" · in your corpus

Worked example — the loyal report who's underperforming. A long-time employee who once carried your team is now missing the bar, and a reorg gives you cover to let them go quietly. This isn't right vs. wrong — it's right vs. right: loyalty to a person who earned it vs. fairness to the team and the mission. Collect: the actual performance facts, the team's load, what the person knows of their own slide. Study the lenses: the welfare lens says cut for the greatest good; the rights lens says they're owed dignity and an honest conversation, not a convenient ambush; the virtue lens asks what a good manager does. Analyze: the newspaper test kills the "quiet during the reorg" plan instantly — you couldn't defend hiding it. The veil of ignorance asks whether you'd accept this treatment not knowing if you were the manager or the report. Choose: the honest, harder path — a direct conversation, a real chance, a dignified exit if it doesn't turn — is the one you'll be proud of in five years. The clash didn't vanish; you just resolved it like the person you want to become.

7 · Check yourself

According to Badaracco, the decisions that truly test and shape character are:

Badaracco's "defining moments" are right-vs-right: truth vs. loyalty, short vs. long term, individual vs. community. Right-vs-wrong needs nerve; right-vs-right needs judgment, and reveals who you are.

Most people will divert the trolley with a lever but refuse to push a man off a bridge — even though both kill one to save five. Why?

The body count is identical. Pushing uses a person as a mere means — which the categorical/rights lens forbids regardless of outcome. Sandel's point is that we change lenses unconsciously.

Rawls' "veil of ignorance" asks you to choose society's rules:

Behind the veil you don't know if you'll be rich or poor, majority or minority — so you'd reject pure utilitarianism and protect everyone's dignity, including the people who might turn out to be you.

⚠️ The introspective tests can launder what you already wanted

Badaracco's questions — "what do my feelings say," "which option expresses who I am" — are powerful and dangerous in the same breath. "Which option expresses who I am" sits one inch from "which option I already wanted," and a skilled rationalizer passes the newspaper test easily because it rewards what's defensible, not what's right. The corpus names the trap precisely: deciding by "what's right for you at the time of the crisis" is how self-interest gets dressed up as judgment. The guardrails are structural — run the four-step process, state the case in all three of Sandel's lenses, and treat your feelings as one input, not the verdict. And beware the opposite failure of Sandel's survey: naming the lenses can leave you articulate and still stuck, a sophisticated relativism that diagnoses without ever deciding. Diagnose with Sandel; decide with the process.

🔎 One subtlety → your bridge to the rest of the shelf

Notice the throughline: every tool here is really about which game your character plays when no rule tells you what to do. Right-vs-wrong calls test courage — Simon Sinek's "most ignored" leadership trait, "the courage to speak truth to power… the courage to do the right thing and have integrity." Right-vs-right calls test judgment. But both compound the same way the repeated-game logic does in strategy: the choice you make today quietly becomes the person you are tomorrow, and reputation is just the accumulated record of those choices. That's the seam into leadership (whether a team trusts you), governance (the veil of ignorance is a board-room test), and culture — Horowitz's "what you do is who you are." The deepest skill isn't knowing the three lenses; it's noticing, in the unscripted moment, which clash you're actually in and choosing like it's being written down.

🎯 Your move

Take one real "right thing to do" you're wrestling with at work. On paper: (1) decide if it's right vs. wrong (then you need courage) or right vs. right — and if it's the latter, name both goods in tension (e.g. loyalty vs. truth). (2) State the case three ways — welfare, rights, virtue — and circle which lens you are defaulting to, and which the people who disagree are using. (3) Run two tests: the newspaper test (could you defend it publicly?) and the veil of ignorance (would you accept it not knowing your role?). (4) Finally, ask the only question that survives the years: which choice will you be proud of in five years — and note where it disagrees with the choice you first wanted.
