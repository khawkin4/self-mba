Every other course on this shelf quietly assumes the numbers are there — that "revenue" means one thing, that the sales figure and the inventory figure came from the same place, that someone can pull the report. Information systems are the plumbing that makes that assumption true — or, when neglected, false. This is the IT backbone of a modern firm: the system that turns scattered keystrokes into decisions (MIS), the spine that makes every department share one set of books (ERP), the structured store underneath it all (the relational database), and the rulebook that keeps it trustworthy (data governance). The single habit of this domain: before you trust a number, ask where it lives, who owns it, and whether it means the same thing to everyone. We build that habit from a pet store that outgrew its spreadsheets, a books-and-authors table, and two analysts who each defined "revenue" their own way. 1 · What an MIS actually is: data → information → decision

Start with the word that sits over the whole field. A Management Information System is not "the IT department" and not "a database" — it's the organized pipeline that converts raw data into something a manager can act on.

"MIS is a computer-based system that provides managers with the tools to organize, evaluate, and manage departments… It's designed to transform raw data into useful information for decision-making. It's more than just technology; it's the process of turning data into insights."

— "What is Management Information System (MIS)?" · in your corpus
Management Information System = organized flow of data → information → decision

The distinction that matters: data is the raw fact (a timestamped sale of one dog-food bag); information is data shaped for a purpose ("dog-food unit sales are down 8% week-over-week in the Northeast"). A manager can't act on the first and can't avoid acting on the second. MIS is the machinery between them — collecting, processing, storing, and disseminating to the right person at the right time. The practitioner pitch is blunt: the people who run this are the ones who "take the data Beast" and turn it into competitive advantage, and the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics projects information-systems-manager roles growing 15% from 2022 to 2032, faster than most fields.

"Businesses are drowning in data… it's kind of like having a Ferrari but not knowing how to drive it. And that's where management information systems professionals come in — they're the ones who turn raw data into actionable insights."

— "Is Management Information Systems Worth It in 2025?" · in your corpus

2 · ERP: making every department share one set of books

The biggest, most expensive information system most firms ever buy is the ERP — Enterprise Resource Planning system. Its whole reason to exist is to kill the disease of disconnected systems: when finance, inventory, HR, and sales each keep their own data, "no one in the business ever sees the full picture."

"ERP stands for enterprise resource planning. It's a software platform that combines all the important aspects of running your business… Without an ERP, your teams are stuck in separate systems that don't talk to each other. With ERP, everyone from the warehouse to the boardroom is working off the same data."

— "ERP Systems Explained in Under 8 Minutes" · in your corpus

The corpus tells the story as a growing pet store. You start with just accounting. Add a product line — now you need inventory. Hire staff — add HR/payroll. Make your own treats — add manufacturing to trace batches and expiration. Run promotions — add a CRM. Soon you're "juggling five different tools that don't talk to each other," re-keying the same data and getting inconsistent answers. Tap each layer to see what ERP integrates — and the tier diagram below it shows who each class of system is built for.

Two practical axes decide which ERP a firm buys. Deployment: cloud (browser-based, subscription, low IT overhead, the common default) vs. on-premise (your own servers, full control, you own maintenance and security — favored in large or heavily regulated firms). Tier: Tier 1 (SAP S/4HANA, Oracle Cloud — multinationals, multi-currency, long and costly to implement); Tier 2 (NetSuite, Acumatica, Infor — mid-market and growing firms); and entry-level systems for the smallest businesses.

Because the hard part isn't the software, it's forcing every department onto one shared definition of products, customers, and processes. ERP grew out of 1960s manufacturing MRP (material resource planning); by the 1990s it spread to "syncing operations across an entire organization." That sync is organizational surgery: when warehouse and procurement have called the same product two different names for a decade, the ERP makes that conflict unavoidable and forces a decision. The Tier 1 systems carry "long implementation times and significant costs" for exactly this reason — you're not installing software, you're re-plumbing the company.

3 · Under the hood: the relational database

Every MIS and ERP sits on a store of data, and for ~50 years the dominant design has been the relational database: data organized into tables (each table is an entity — a noun like Books or Authors), with columns as attributes and each row as one record. Two ideas make it powerful: every row has a unique primary key (an ID), and a foreign key in one table points at the key of another — that's how the "relations" are formed.

"Every table is going to have an ID field, some way to uniquely identify it… one of these fields can be an actual number that relates to the ID of [another] table — and this is what's known as a foreign key. When I do my query, I can reference both these tables… using this foreign key."

— IBM, "What is a Relational Database?" · in your corpus

Worked example — two tables, one join. A bookstore models Books(book_id, title, year, author_id) and Authors(author_id, name). The author_id in Books is a foreign key pointing at the primary key of Authors. To list every book with its author's name, you don't store the name twice — you JOIN:

SELECT b.title, a.name FROM Books b JOIN Authors a ON b.author_id = a.author_id;

This is why the design is called normalized: each fact (an author's name) is stored once, in one place, and referenced by key everywhere else. Change a name in Authors and every query reflects it instantly — no hunting through duplicated copies. The four everyday operations are CRUD — Create (INSERT), Read (SELECT), Update (UPDATE), Delete (DELETE) — all written in SQL, the English-like language for talking to the database.

The corpus flags three production features worth knowing by name. An index on a frequently-queried column (say, name or date) keeps a fast-lookup cache so queries don't scan the whole table. Transactions give you consistency: bundle several writes so that if one fails, you can roll back the whole set — vital for, say, an e-commerce inventory update that must not half-complete. And stored procedures / views let you save complex query logic once instead of re-writing big SQL blocks. Together these are roughly the "ACID" guarantees that make relational stores the default for anything that touches money.

"If one of those queries fails, I want the option to be able to roll back the whole set… just to make sure that my data always maintains some consistency there."

— IBM, "What is a Relational Database?" · in your corpus

4 · Data governance: who owns the number, and what it means

You can have a flawless ERP on a clean relational database and still ship the wrong number — because nobody agreed what the number means. Data governance is the rulebook that closes that gap: the rules, processes, and accountability around data.

"Data governance is the rules, processes, and accountability around data… The main difference is data governance outlines the overall structure that should exist — the rules, the processes, the accountability — while data management is the hands-on everyday work to ensure that governance is being followed."

— "Data Governance Tutorial" · in your corpus

The IBM "clean-out-the-house" analogy gives governance its working vocabulary, and it maps cleanly onto a firm's data: discovery (find every data asset you have — the easy part is the data you know about; the hard part is the attic), classification (label it: customer data, financial data, PII), policies and rules (the standard plus its enforcement — e.g., "a Social Security number is PII and must be masked"), and metadata (the labeled bin / library card catalog that says where data came from and what it is, so it's findable later).

"One policy that is very common yet critical is around personally identifiable data… such as a Social Security number must be masked. The rules help you enforce that — if it is a Social Security number, you must mask it."

— IBM, "Data Governance Explained in 5 Minutes" · in your corpus

Accountability has a name too: the data owner (or sponsor) holds "ultimate decision-making ability… and ultimate accountability for that data being correct and up-to-date." A firm has many — a sales-data owner, a manufacturing-data owner — each responsible for one domain. This is the unglamorous answer to the most common data crisis, which the practitioners describe vividly:

"Two analysts had written two different calculations for 'Revenue.' One was calculating gross revenue (total order amount). The other was net revenue (order amount minus returns). Both named the metric the same… The biggest data problem I keep running into isn't dirty data. It's teams defining the same metric differently."

— r/BusinessIntelligence practitioner thread · in your corpus

6 · The three-tier stack: how it all fits together

Step back and the whole backbone is a stack. Raw events land in a governed database; an ERP / application layer integrates and processes them across departments; an MIS / reporting layer turns the result into information a manager reads. Governance isn't a layer — it's the rulebook wrapping all three.

What is the core problem an ERP system solves?

ERP's value is integration: without it, "teams are stuck in separate systems that don't talk to each other." Sell one bag of dog food and inventory, revenue, and analytics all update at once — entered once, not five times.

In a relational database, a foreign key is:

The unique ID in a row's own table is the primary key. A foreign key is a field pointing at another table's primary key — that pointer is what lets a JOIN combine Books with Authors.

Two dashboards show different "revenue" numbers though the underlying data is clean. The governance fix is:

The data was clean; the conflict was two definitions (gross vs. net) sharing one name. Governance assigns an owner and a documented definition so the metric means the same thing everywhere — the single source of truth.

⚠️ Buying the system is not the same as fixing the data

The expensive mistake is treating an ERP purchase, a database migration, or a new BI tool as the answer. The corpus practitioners are scathing about this: clean pipelines and a tidy Snowflake warehouse still produced mismatched revenue because governance — agreed definitions and ownership — was missing. "Why don't we have a single source of truth?" is a people-and-rules question, not a software question. Tools enforce policy; they don't invent it. Decide what a metric means and who owns it before you build the dashboard, or you'll automate the disagreement.

🔎 One subtlety → your bridge to the rest of the MBA

Notice the throughline back to every other course: a strategy is only as good as the number it's built on, an accounting statement is only as true as the governed data feeding it, and an operations decision is only as fast as the system that surfaces it. The deepest reframe here is that "the data" is not a neutral given — it is manufactured by systems and conventions you can design or neglect. When marketing's CAC and finance's CAC disagree, when the board sees a different revenue than the warehouse, the failure is almost never the analyst — it's the absent owner and the undefined metric. Information systems is the quiet discipline that makes all the loud disciplines trustworthy.

🎯 Your move

Pick one number your team or business actually decides on (revenue, active users, churn, gross margin). On paper, run the governance drill: (1) Discovery — list every system it currently lives in (the ERP, a spreadsheet, a CRM, a "secret" export). (2) Definition — write the exact formula in one sentence; if two people would write it differently, you've found your bug. (3) Owner — name the single person accountable for it being right. (4) Source of truth — circle the one system everyone should pull from. If you can't fill all four boxes for even one metric, you've just located why your reports argue with each other — and the cheapest fix on the whole shelf.
