Great marketing isn't louder ads — it's positioning (what you are, who you beat, why you matter) plus a deep read on the job customers hire you to do. Get those right and a brand can charge more than its rivals. This is a hands-on lesson: tap the cards, the diagrams, and the quizzes. 1 · Positioning: the context you set before any message lands

April Dunford has done positioning work with 200+ companies. Her core idea: positioning is not a tagline — it's the context that makes a buyer think "oh, I get what this is, and I want it." Tap each piece 👇

How your product is the best in the world at delivering some value that a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about. It defines who you beat, how you're different, the value only you deliver, and who that value is for.

Vision = the all-singing-all-dancing future that doesn't exist yet. Strategy = your plan to overcome the obstacles between today and that future. Positioning = how you win in the market today, as you actually are right now.

In B2B, a truly undifferentiated product just fails — positioning can't rescue it. Far more often, the team has differentiation but doesn't recognize it. The fix is to find it, not to invent value that isn't there.

4 · Jobs to Be Done: why people actually buy

Clayton Christensen's classic example: McDonald's tried to improve its milkshake by asking milkshake-buyers what they wanted. Sales didn't budge. Then a researcher just watched — and discovered half of milkshakes sold before 8:30 a.m., bought alone, taken to the car. The job? Survive a long, boring commute and stay full till lunch. The competition wasn't other shakes — it was bagels, bananas, and donuts.

You don't want a milkshake — you want progress in a situation. You "hire" the shake for the commute job and "fire" the banana (gone in a minute) or the bagel (you're steering with your knees putting on cream cheese). The framing forces you to study the context and outcome, not the product.

Henry Ford's line: ask customers what they want and they describe a better version of what exists ("a faster horse"). The job — get from A to B quickly — points past the current solution. So observe the job; don't only take feature requests.

"the job that all of these people were trying to get done was I have a long and boring drive to work and I needed something that would just keep me engaged with life while I'm driving the car"

— HubSpot Marketing, "Clay Christensen: The Jobs to be Done Theory" · in your corpus

⚠️ Don't confuse features with jobs

Leather seats and touchscreen nav are features — bells and whistles. The job a car is hired for is "commute safely and reliably," sometimes with social and emotional layers on top. Build for the job; the features only matter as far as they serve it.

5 · Where brand shows up in the numbers: Nike

Positioning and a well-served job build a brand — and a brand's payoff is pricing power: customers pay a premium for the swoosh on otherwise-similar shoes. You can read that power straight off the income statement as gross margin. Here are Nike's real FY2025 figures (10-K, fiscal year ended 2025-05-31). Tap each bar:

Gross Margin  =  Gross Profit  ÷  Revenue

Cost of sales = 46.31 − 19.79 = $26.52B → it costs Nike about 57¢ to produce every $1 of product it sells.

Gross margin = 19.79 ÷ 46.31 = 42.7% → nearly 43¢ of every sales dollar survives making the product. A no-name shoe factory competing on price alone runs far thinner. That gap is the brand.

Net margin = 3.22 ÷ 46.31 = 7.0% → after marketing, overhead, interest and tax, 7¢ is left. Notice the drop from 42.7% to 7.0%: sustaining a brand (advertising, athletes, retail) is expensive — brand power lives in the gross line, and the cost of defending it lives below.

6 · Check yourself

In Dunford's method, what is the very first step of positioning?

You can't claim you're "different" until you've defined different from what. Alternatives (including the status quo) come first; value and category flow from there.

A customer buys a morning milkshake for a boring commute. In JTBD terms, what is the milkshake competing against?

The job defines the competitive set. For the commute job, the milkshake competes with bagels, bananas and donuts — not just other shakes.

Nike's FY2025 gross margin is ~42.7% while a generic factory's is far lower. What does that gap mostly reflect?

Gross margin = gross profit ÷ revenue. A higher margin on a similar physical good signals pricing power — the financial signature of a strong brand.

🔎 One subtlety → your bridge to the next course

Nike's net margin (7.0%) is far below its gross margin (42.7%) because the marketing and SG&A that build the brand sit below the gross line. Deciding how much to spend defending a brand — and what return it earns — is a question of marketing ROI and capital allocation. Positioning tells you what to say; the finance course tells you how much it's worth spending to say it.
