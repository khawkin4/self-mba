Every executive now faces the same question: what do we do about AI? The surprising answer from the people who lead these transformations is that the question itself is wrong. Transformation isn't a technology project — it's a business project. This is a hands-on lesson: tap the cards, the diagrams, and the quizzes as we turn hype into a real playbook.

1 · The core mistake: starting with the technology

Almost every failed AI effort begins the same way — with the tool. Tap each reframe to see how the leaders in your corpus flip it 👇

The AWS playbook calls the first version "a solution searching for a problem." Start with the business problem, then work backwards to whether AI even helps. Airbus put it bluntly: "We are always investing in a business problem," not in AI.

MIT's George Westerman: the hard part isn't adopting the technology, it's changing the way you do business. "It's not the digital. It's the transformation." Technology by itself creates zero value — what you do with it does.

Bolting AI onto an unchanged, step-by-step process produces tiny gains — sometimes it even slows things down. The real value comes from rethinking the process around what AI now makes possible.

"Technology is not the problem. Transformation is. So it's not really a technical problem."

— MIT Corporate Relations, "Integrating Generative AI Into Business Strategy: Dr. George Westerman" · in your corpus

2 · Westerman's Law — the engine of every transformation

If you remember one idea from this lesson, make it this. It explains why transformations are hard and where leaders should aim their effort:

Technology changes quickly  ·  Organizations change slowly

The gap between those two speeds is exactly where transformation lives. The tool can be installed in a week; the new habits, incentives, and processes take months or years. That's leadership work, not IT work.

"Technology changes quickly. Organizations change much more slowly... The hard part is not adopting the technology. It's changing the way you do business."

— MIT Corporate Relations, "Dr. George Westerman on Generative AI & Business Strategy" · in your corpus

5 · Start small, then compound

Andrew Ng has watched more companies fail by starting too big than too small. A grand vision that flops can set a company back years and drain its faith in AI. His sequence:

"It's better to start small than to start too large. I've seen more companies fail by starting too big than fail by starting too small."

— CXOTalk, "Andrew Ng: Enterprise AI Strategy (with Landing AI)" · in your corpus

6 · The deeper shift: AI as coordination infrastructure

Sangeet Paul Choudary warns that "AI as automation" is the shallow view. His shipping-container parable shows why. The container's first effect looked like automation (cranes replacing dock workers) — but its real impact was reorganizing the whole economy: reliable shipping let factories source parts from anywhere, and global supply chains were born.

The lesson for AI: when the cost of a task collapses, the assumptions your business was built on quietly break. Translating a document used to be slow and expensive; now it's near-free. Choudary's question isn't "how do we apply AI to our business?" but "how does a world with AI change our playing field — who we compete with and how we win?" Strategy answers two questions: where do we play, and how do we win?

"Strategy is fundamentally about answering two questions: where do we play, how do we win."

— CXOTalk, "Why Your AI Strategy Is Failing — Sangeet Paul Choudary" · in your corpus

No. Choudary treats AI fluency as table stakes: "It's just going to help you run. It's not going to tell you where to run." Knowing how to use the tool gets you in the game; reimagining the business is what wins it.

7 · Humans stay at the center

A recurring theme across the whole corpus: the goal is not to replace people but to redesign roles. AWS frames the leader's new job as moving from task definer ("run this report") to objective setter ("resolve this within policy, by this deadline") — managing AI by goals, success metrics, and escalation triggers. And keep humans firmly in control of one-way-door (irreversible), high-stakes, or relationship-sensitive decisions.

⚠️ The "tools, not processes" trap

In a satirical talk on how to fail, Hamel Husain and Greg Ceccarelli nail the most common death spiral: "focus on tools not processes," then "just buy a new more expensive Vector database" every time something breaks. Throwing tools at problems you never diagnosed — and never looking at your own data — is how strategies quietly collapse. Diagnose first; tools come last.
