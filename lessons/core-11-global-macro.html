When a business crosses a border, it picks up a new kind of risk that has nothing to do with its products: the risk of the place itself. And because the future of any place is uncertain, smart firms don't bet on a single forecast — they plan for several futures at once. This lesson teaches two linked skills: how to read country risk (the macro lens) and how to run scenario planning (the strategy lens). Hover the cards, scan the diagrams, and take the quizzes as you go.

1 · Why a country is itself a risk

The same company is riskier to run in some countries than others — and the difference shows up in costs, predictability, and the chance of catastrophe. Aswath Damodaran isolates four dimensions that make one country riskier than another. Click each to see why it matters to a business

Not a moral judgment, a business one. Democracies bring continuous risk: elections change the rules constantly. Authoritarian regimes can promise stable rules for decades — until the regime itself collapses, which brings discontinuous, catastrophic risk. Neither is "safer"; they fail differently.

Violence from without (war) or within (terrorism, unrest) makes operating more expensive — you buy insurance, you buy security guards. The same red regions show up on chart after chart.

Corruption acts like a tax, except the money goes into a bureaucrat's pocket instead of the public purse. You either pay the bribes (higher costs) or refuse and lose business to less scrupulous rivals.

A business runs on contracts and ownership. Courts must enforce both — and do it quickly. A legal system that takes decades to enforce your rights is, in Damodaran's words, no better than one with no rights at all.

"corruption is a hidden tax in what way it adds costs that are to almost everything you do as a business even routine things..."

3 · From many risks to one number

How do you turn four messy dimensions into a number you can use? You start from a mature-market equity risk premium, then add a country-specific layer built from default risk. Because stocks are riskier than government bonds, the default spread gets scaled up before it's added. The relationship Damodaran uses:

Country ERP  =  Mature-Market Premium  +  (Default Spread  ×  Equity/Bond Scalar)

Two ways to measure that default layer: sovereign ratings (the agencies' grade) and the sovereign CDS spread (the market's live price). They usually agree — but the market reacts in minutes while agencies lag.

"My biggest problem with ratings agencies they're slow to catch on. and it takes them years before they adjust the rating to reflect the changes..."

4 · Two rules that trip up beginners

Rule 1 — Risk follows operations, not the headquarters. A US company building a plant in Brazil is exposed to Brazilian risk, not US risk. To value a multinational like Coca-Cola, you take a weighted average of the equity risk premiums of every country it earns revenue in — not just where it's incorporated.

Rule 2 — Be currency-consistent. You can value any project in any currency. Switching currencies just swaps in a different expected-inflation assumption, which shows up in the risk-free rate. High-inflation currency? High discount rate — but also high growth in your cash flows. The two must match.

"you can pick any currency you want and as long as you stay consistent the value you get should be unchanged if you switch currencies"

— Aswath Damodaran, "Country Risk: A 2023 Mid-year Update" · in your corpus

Don't mix and match currencies

The classic error: forecasting cash flows in one currency (say, rupees, with high inflation baked in) while discounting at a rate built for another (dollars). You end up double-counting or omitting inflation and the valuation breaks. Pick one currency and use the same expected inflation in both the cash flows and the discount rate.

6 · Now flip the telescope: planning for an uncertain future

Country risk measures the world as it is today. But strategy lives in the future — and the future is uncertain. The disciplined response is scenario planning: instead of one forecast, you build several internally-consistent stories of how the world could unfold, then stress-test your strategy against each. The goal isn't prediction — it's readiness.

"It's not about predicting the future. It's about being ready for whatever the future might bring."

— Strategy Mastery Hub, "Scenario Planning: How to Future-Proof Your Business Strategy" · in your corpus

The most popular tool is the 2×2 driving-forces matrix: take the two forces that are both most uncertain and most impactful, cross them, and you get four distinct futures. Click each quadrant below — here we cross technological change against market regulation:

Two other classics. Shell scenarios (1970s): loose narratives that separate predetermined elements (trends that happen regardless) from critical uncertainties, using "intellectual Pathfinders" to imagine the fringes. Dator's four archetypes: every future fits one of continuation, collapse, discipline, or transformation. The 2×2 is the most structured and beginner-friendly of the three.

7 · A plan is not a strategy

Here's the trap scenario planning guards against. A plan is a list of activities you control — build a plant, hire people, launch a brand. It feels comforting because you control it. A strategy specifies an outcome you don't control: customers choosing you over rivals. Roger Martin's distinction is the spine of this whole module.

"a strategy is an integrated set of choices that positions you on a playing field of your choice in a way that you win"

— Harvard Business Review, "A Plan Is Not a Strategy" · in your corpus

Shell vs. Kodak. In the 1970s, Shell ran scenario planning on oil prices and was ready when the 1973 shock hit — while rivals were blindsided. Kodak invented the first digital camera in 1975 but planned around a single assumed future (film stays dominant), ignored the future it had foreseen, and declined. Same era, opposite outcomes: one prepared for multiple futures, the other bet on one.

Where macro and strategy meet → your bridge ahead

Notice the through-line: country risk feeds the discount rate (Course 02's valuation machine), and scenario planning feeds the cash-flow forecasts you discount. A high country-risk premium and a "Locked Down" regulatory scenario both push value down — from opposite ends of the model. 8 · Check yourself (strategy)

Which pair of forces makes the BEST axes for a 2×2 scenario matrix?

Scenario axes must be both uncertain and impactful. Things you're sure about are predetermined elements — they don't generate distinct futures.

"We'll open three new stores, hire 50 people, and launch a loyalty app." According to HBR, this is…

It's a plan: activities you control, on the cost side, with no theory of why customers will choose you. A strategy specifies a competitive outcome you don't fully control.
