Finance valued the business. Microeconomics & strategy asks the prior question: how do people, firms, and prices actually behave when everyone is chasing their own self-interest at the same time? The answer turns out to be surprisingly orderly. Two engines run the whole show — incentives (what makes one person act) and games (what happens when your best move depends on someone else's). Master those two and you can read almost any market or competitive standoff. Tap the cards, diagrams, and quizzes as you go.

1 · The atom of economics: incentives

Economists don't assume people are greedy — they assume people respond to incentives: the rewards and punishments attached to each option. Change the incentive and you change the behavior, often overnight, even when speeches and laws failed. The cleanest illustration in the corpus is the British convict ships of 1787.

"Instead of paying captains for every prisoner who set sail for Australia, the government would only pay captains for prisoners who reached Australia alive. Overnight, the incentives of sea captains changed. The survival rate of prisoners reached 99%."

— Marginal Revolution University, "What are Incentives?" · in your corpus

Newspapers, clergy, and Parliament all demanded better treatment and got nowhere. One change to the payment rule fixed it. That is the economist's lens: don't ask people to be better — change what's rewarded.

That forgone alternative is the opportunity cost — "what you give up to get something," as Nick Huntington-Klein puts it. Every real decision weighs benefits against costs including opportunity costs.

2 · Prices are messages: signals & the invisible hand

In a market nobody is in charge — yet skinny jeans get made and tablets reach the people who want them. The coordinator is price. A high price signals producers to make more and signals buyers to consume less; the two forces meet at a new equilibrium. CrashCourse names the mechanism:

"If people pay high prices for skinny jeans, that tells manufacturers, 'The company is very interested in skinny jeans, start making them.' If no one wants skinny jeans, manufacturers will start making something else instead."

— CrashCourse, "Markets, Efficiency, and Price Signals: Crash Course Economics #19" · in your corpus

Watch the loop in action. When demand for strawberries jumps, the price rises — and that single rising price does two jobs at once, pulling supply up and demand down until the gap (the excess demand) closes:

"This higher price first acts as a signal to communicate to producers that strawberries have gotten more expensive therefore this price acts as an incentive for producers to produce more strawberries."

— economiics, "Price Mechanism: Price as Signals & Incentives" · in your corpus

4 · When your move depends on theirs: game theory

Markets coordinate strangers through price. But what about a handful of rivals watching each other — two airlines, two phone makers, two ice-cream carts? Now your best choice depends on their choice, and theirs on yours. That's game theory.

"Game theory is a mathematical theory that attempts to make sense of how it is that people interact in these strategic situations... the study of decision making under conditions of uncertainty over time."

— Big Think, "3 game theory tactics, explained" · in your corpus

The classic trap is the prisoner's dilemma: two players each do what's individually rational and both end up worse off. The corpus has a beautiful real case — the 1971 U.S. ban on TV cigarette ads. You'd expect a ban to hurt tobacco firms. It did the opposite.

The advertising game (two firms, A and B). Payoffs from 365 Financial Analyst's example: if neither advertises, each earns $50M. If both advertise, each earns only $40M (ads cancel out, but the spending is wasted). If just one advertises, it grabs share — $60M for the advertiser vs. $25M for the one who sat still.

Each firm reasons: "Whatever my rival does, I'm better off advertising" (60 > 50 if they don't; 40 > 25 if they do). So both advertise and both land at $40M — worse than the $50M they'd share if both stayed quiet. That stuck-but-worse outcome is the Nash equilibrium. The 1971 ban acted like a referee forcing the cooperative cell: ads off for everyone → profits rose.

"Both players are better off if they cooperate however they cannot be certain the other party will adhere so they opt for a solution that guarantees them the best possible outcome... companies will choose to advertise and that's precisely what happened in practice."

— 365 Financial Analyst, "Game theory lessons - Historical example: Tobacco companies" · in your corpus

5 · Why rivals huddle together: Nash equilibrium on the beach

Ever notice competing gas stations, coffee shops, and car dealers cluster on the same corner? Game theory explains it with the Hotelling beach model. Two ice-cream sellers on a mile-long beach should (for customers) sit at the quarter-marks. But each keeps inching toward the center to steal the other's customers — until both stand back-to-back in the middle. Neither can now improve by moving: that's a Nash equilibrium, even though customers at the ends now walk farther.

"You and your competitor's relative are in what game theorists call a position. Nash equilibrium is the point at which neither of you can improve the situation by changing the strategy from the original one."

— TED-Ed, "Why do competitors open their stores next to one another? - Jac de Haan" · in your corpus

6 · Two strategic mindsets: finite vs. infinite games

Zoom out from any single game and a deeper distinction appears. Simon Sinek splits all strategy into two types. A finite game has fixed rules, known players, and a winner — baseball. An infinite game has changing players and rules, and no finish line; the only goal is to keep playing. Business, he argues, is infinite — and firms that play it as if it were finite eventually burn out.

"The game of business is an infinite game... the number of companies that are playing finite they're playing to win they're playing to beat the quarter or the year and they're always frustrated by that company that has an amazing Vision a long-term Vision... over the long term that player will always win and the other player will run out of resources or the will."

— TED Archive, "What game theory teaches us about war | Simon Sinek" · in your corpus

The practical takeaway: know which game you're in before you pick a strategy. Optimizing for this quarter (finite) against a rival optimizing for the next decade (infinite) is how strong players get caught in a quagmire.

Strategy  =  (know the incentives)  ×  (read the other players)  ×  (know which game you're playing)

7 · The bias that wrecks good strategy: sunk cost

One human flaw quietly sabotages all of the above — the sunk cost fallacy: throwing good money (or time) after bad because of what you've already spent. Rationally, spent-and-unrecoverable resources should never enter a forward-looking decision. Big Think frames it through poker, then life:

"We'll often go to ourselves ah well I've gone this far I've put this much in I might as well see it through to the end but if you have very strong information that actually from this point onwards putting more money in the pot is a bad idea then you shouldn't."

— Big Think, "3 game theory tactics, explained" · in your corpus

Notice how Sections 1 and 7 connect: a sunk cost is the opposite of an opportunity cost. Opportunity cost is the future you forgo (always relevant); sunk cost is the past you can't recover (never relevant). Good strategists weigh the first and ignore the second.

8 · Check yourself

The British government couldn't reduce convict-ship deaths with laws or appeals. What finally worked?

Changing the incentive — pay on safe arrival, not departure — pushed the survival rate to 99% overnight. Change the reward, change the behavior.

Two firms both advertise and each ends up at $40M, when both staying quiet would have earned each $50M. What is this outcome called?

Each defects because advertising is individually best whatever the rival does — so both land in a stable but worse-for-everyone Nash equilibrium, the classic prisoner's dilemma.

You've read 100 pages of a book you're not enjoying. The "rational" reason to keep reading is…

The 100 pages are spent and unrecoverable — a sunk cost. A forward-looking decision ignores them and asks only: is the next page worth it?

⚠️ "The market will sort it out" — except when it won't

Price signals are powerful, but markets fail. CrashCourse is blunt: "most economists understand that markets are imperfect and that they often fail to meet society's needs." Price gouging, predatory pricing, pollution, and information gaps all distort the signal. Treat the invisible hand as a strong default, not a law of nature — sometimes a referee (regulation, or a ban like the 1971 ad rule) produces a better outcome than the unguided game.

🔎 One subtlety → your bridge to the next course

Everything here assumed players are rational and act on full information. Real humans cling to sunk costs, misread signals, and herd toward the center of the beach for reasons no spreadsheet predicts. That gap — between the rational agent of microeconomics and the messy, biased decider in the mirror — is exactly where behavioral economics and marketing pick up. Strategy tells you the optimal move; the next course asks why people so often don't make it.
