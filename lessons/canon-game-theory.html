Every strategy you've learned so far assumed you were acting on a board that holds still. Game theory is what happens when the board pushes back — when the people across from you are running their own calculations about what you'll do. The single discipline of this whole field is one habit: before you move, work out the other side's best response — and then move as if they've already made it. This lesson builds that habit from a poker-table mathematician, a 1971 cigarette ban that made tobacco companies richer, and two ice-cream carts on a beach. Tap the cards, the payoff grids, and the quizzes as you go.

1 · The one habit: anticipate the response

Game theory was laid out by John von Neumann — who built it, of all things, on a stripped-down version of poker — in The Theory of Games and Economic Behavior, written with economist Oskar Morgenstern. Its definition is deceptively plain:

"Game theory is the study of decision making under conditions of uncertainty over time… any time you're interacting with another person who has their own interests and is trying to achieve their own ends — you're interacting in a strategic situation."

— Big Think, "3 game theory tactics, explained" · in your corpus

Against a sharp opponent, von Neumann's first prescription is the minimax rule: don't chase your best case — minimize your maximum loss. Ask "what's the worst my opponent could do to me?" and play the move that best guards against it. In a pure zero-sum game, the corpus notes, this isn't just safe — it's provably optimal.

Before you move → assume the other side plays their best response → then choose

This is what the canon calls backward induction (Dixit & Nalebuff): "look forward, reason back." Solve the game from its end state to decide your first move. The rest of the lesson is just this habit applied to harder and harder situations.

2 · The Prisoner's Dilemma — when "rational" makes everyone worse off

The most important game in business is the one where both sides, each acting perfectly sensibly, walk straight into a worse outcome for both. Your corpus has a real example: in 1971 the U.S. banned cigarette TV advertising — and all four major tobacco firms posted higher profits afterward. Why? Because the ban did for them what they couldn't do for themselves: it ended a ruinous advertising arms race.

Imagine two firms. Advertising is expensive and mostly just steals share from the other. Tap each cell to see why they're trapped.

"It turns out tobacco companies would be better off without advertising — but only if all companies don't do it… Nash's equilibrium indicates companies will choose to advertise, and that's precisely what happened in practice."

— 365 Financial Analyst, "Game theory lessons — Tobacco companies" · in your corpus

A Nash equilibrium is a combination of choices where no player can do better by changing their move alone. At "both advertise" ($40M each), if either firm unilaterally stops advertising it drops to $25M — so neither will. It's stable but not optimal: the better joint outcome ($50M each) isn't an equilibrium because each side is tempted to defect from it. That gap — between what's individually rational and what's collectively best — is the heart of game theory.

3 · Dominant strategies and why competitors cluster

In the tobacco game, "advertise" is a dominant strategy — it's the better move no matter what the other firm does (60 beats 50 if they stay clean; 40 beats 25 if they advertise). When every player has a dominant strategy, the equilibrium is locked in, even when it's bad for everyone. Dominant strategies are rare, but when they exist they're decisive.

Nash equilibria also explain things that look irrational until you draw the game. Why do three coffee shops cluster on one corner while half the town has none? The TED-Ed "beach vendor" example in your corpus shows it: two ice-cream sellers who should spread out to serve the whole beach instead each creep toward the center to steal the other's customers — until they're back-to-back in the middle.

"In order to maximize sales… each vendor creeps toward the center. Game theorists see the spread-out version as the socially optimal solution — but it isn't the equilibrium. The equilibrium is both carts in the middle."

— TED-Ed, "Why do competitors open their stores next to one another?" · in your corpus

The customers at the ends get a worse deal; the vendors get a stable standoff. Nash equilibrium ≠ best outcome — it's just the place no one can unilaterally improve on. Spotting the difference is where strategy lives.

4 · The escape hatch: repeated games change everything

Here's the twist that rescues us from the dilemma. The prisoner's dilemma is brutal when you play once. But when you'll meet the same player again and again, cooperation becomes rational — because defection gets punished next round. Your corpus shows the highest-stakes version: how Reagan and Gorbachev escaped the Cold War's zero-sum trap.

"Rather than getting rid of a large percentage and hoping the USSR would too, they broke it into little interactions — the USSR eliminates a few weapons, then the U.S. does, they check, then the next stage. Taking a big interaction and breaking it into small parts can change a bad social dilemma into a positive interaction."

— Big Think, "3 game theory tactics, explained" · in your corpus

That is exactly the discovery behind Robert Axelrod's famous tournament (The Evolution of Cooperation): the winning strategy for a repeated prisoner's dilemma was the simplest one, Tit for Tat — cooperate first, then mirror whatever the other side did last. Its four winning traits: be nice (never defect first), retaliatory (punish defection at once), forgiving (return to cooperation the moment they do), and clear (be predictable so others can cooperate with you). In repeated games, predictable fairness beats clever maneuvering.

Simon Sinek frames the same idea as finite vs. infinite games: play a long-term relationship as if it's a one-shot contest and you'll win battles while losing the war.

6 · The highest move: change the game itself

The masters don't just play the game in front of them — they change it. Three canonical levers:

Worked example — a price war. You and a rival keep cutting prices; it's the tobacco grid in disguise, and you're both sliding toward the bad Nash equilibrium ($40M/$40M). Don't ask "how do I win this round?" Ask the game-theory questions: (1) Is this repeated? If you'll compete for years, signal restraint and reward it — play Tit for Tat, not scorched earth. (2) Can I make a credible commitment? A public "everyday low price, price-matched" policy can paradoxically stop the war by making further cuts pointless for both. (3) Is there a complementor? Maybe the bigger prize is partnering with someone whose product makes yours more valuable, expanding the market instead of splitting it. You changed the game instead of losing it.

⚠️ Nash equilibrium is a description, not a goal

The classic error is treating "find the Nash equilibrium" as the answer. It isn't — it's the diagnosis of where naive self-interest lands. Often the whole point of strategy is to escape the equilibrium (repeat the game, commit credibly, add a complementor) or to reshape it. And remember the model's big assumption: it presumes rational players with known payoffs. Real people are predictably irrational and rarely know the true payoff matrix — which is exactly the bridge to behavioral decision science. Use game theory to frame the interaction, never to hand you a single number.

🔎 One subtlety → your bridge to the rest of the shelf

Notice the throughline: the difference between a one-shot game and a repeated one changed everything — from defect to cooperate, from war to treaty. That single variable, "will we meet again?", is the hidden engine under negotiation (relationships vs. transactions), culture (reputation compounds), and trust itself. The deepest strategic skill isn't computing the equilibrium — it's recognizing which game you're actually in, and whether you can change it.

Pick one competitive situation you're in (a pricing fight, a negotiation, a turf clash between teams). On paper: (1) sketch the 2×2 payoff grid — your two options × their two options, with rough payoffs in each cell; (2) circle the Nash equilibrium and ask whether it's actually good for you; (3) write one way to change the game — is it repeated (play Tit for Tat)? Can you make a credible commitment? Is there a complementor who'd grow the pie? The grid almost always reveals a move you couldn't see from inside the fight.
