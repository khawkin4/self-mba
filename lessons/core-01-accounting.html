Before you can judge a business, run one, or invest in one, you have to read its scoreboard. That scoreboard is three financial statements. Learn to read them and you can understand any company on earth — from a corner cafe to Apple. This lesson teaches you to read all three, using Apple's real, most-recent numbers.

The one idea to hold onto

A company publishes three statements because there are three different questions you can ask about money — and no single statement answers all three:

  • Income Statement — “Did we sell things profitably?” (covers a period, like a video of the whole year)
  • Balance Sheet — “What do we own and what do we owe?” (a single moment, like a photo)
  • Cash Flow Statement — “Did real cash actually move?” (a period again)

Part 1 · The vocabulary (in plain English)

Every term below is industry-standard — these are the exact words analysts, founders, and bankers use. We'll define each, then immediately see it in Apple's real results.

Revenue (a.k.a. “the top line,” “sales”)
The total money customers paid you for your products/services in the period. It's called the “top line” because it sits at the top of the income statement.
COGS (Cost of Goods Sold)
The direct cost of making the thing you sold — for Apple, the chips, glass, aluminum, and assembly that go into an iPhone. Not rent or marketing — just the cost of the product itself.
Gross Profit & Gross Margin
Gross Profit = Revenue − COGS. What's left after paying to make the product, before any other costs. Gross Margin is that as a percentage of revenue — a pure read on pricing power. A 70% gross margin means the product sells for far more than it costs to build.
Operating Expenses (OpEx)
The costs of running the business that aren't tied to one product unit: salaries, R&D, marketing, rent.
Net Income (a.k.a. “the bottom line,” “earnings,” “profit”)
What's left after everything — COGS, OpEx, interest, taxes. The single most-quoted number in business. Net Margin = Net Income ÷ Revenue.
Assets / Liabilities / Equity
Assets = everything the company controls (cash, factories, inventory). Liabilities = everything it owes (debt, bills). Equity = what's truly left for the owners after debts — the “net worth” of the business.

Part 2 · The accounting equation (the law that never breaks)

The balance sheet is built on one unbreakable rule. It's called the accounting equation, and it always holds — that's why it's a “balance” sheet:

Assets  =  Liabilities  +  Equity

Part 4 · Worked example — what these numbers actually tell you

Gross margin = 195.2 ÷ 416.2 = 46.9%. Almost half of every sale is left after building the product. That's pricing power — customers pay a premium Apple doesn't have to “earn” with cheap inputs.

Net margin = 112.0 ÷ 416.2 = 26.9%. After all costs, more than a quarter is profit. For comparison, a typical grocery chain runs a ~2% net margin. Same word — “profit” — wildly different businesses.

The earnings-quality check: Apple's operating cash flow was $111.5B — almost identical to its $112.0B net income. That's a green flag: the reported profit is backed by real cash arriving, not accounting estimates. When cash flow is much lower than net income, dig deeper.

⚠️ The thing that trips up every beginner: profit ≠ cash

A company can be profitable on paper and still go bankrupt if cash doesn't arrive in time (you booked a $1M sale, but the customer pays in 90 days, while payroll is due Friday). That's exactly why the cash flow statement exists as a separate scoreboard. “Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact.”

🔎 One subtlety to graduate you to Course 02

Apple's equity looks small ($73.7B) next to its $112B profit — that would imply a >150% return on equity, which seems impossible. The reason: Apple has spent years buying back its own shares, which shrinks equity. So a ratio can “lie” without context. How a company chooses to use its profit — buybacks vs. dividends vs. reinvestment — is the subject of Capital Allocation (Course 02 & Exec module E2).

Check yourself

1. A company has Revenue $500, COGS $300, all other costs $150. What's its gross profit, net income, and net margin?

Gross profit = 500 − 300 = $200. Net income = 200 − 150 = $50. Net margin = 50 ÷ 500 = 10%.

2. If a company's Assets are $80 and Liabilities are $50, what is Equity — and what does it represent?

Equity = 80 − 50 = $30. It's the owners' true stake — what would remain for them if every asset were sold and every debt paid.

3. A startup reports $2M in profit but can't make payroll. How is that possible — and which statement reveals it?

Its profit is tied up in unpaid customer invoices (or inventory), so the cash hasn't arrived. The cash flow statement exposes this gap that the income statement hides.
