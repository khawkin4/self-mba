Every business keeps score with three financial statements. Learn to read them and you can size up any company — a corner cafe or Apple. This is a hands-on lesson: tap the cards, the diagrams, and the quiz. We'll use Apple's real, latest numbers throughout.

1 · Three statements, three questions

Tap each to see the question it answers 👇

"Did we sell things profitably?" Covers a period (a quarter or year) — like a video of the whole season. Starts with sales at the top, subtracts costs down to profit at the bottom.

"What do we own, and what do we owe?" A single moment in time — like a photo. Lists everything the company controls and everything it owes, on one date.

"Did real cash actually move?" A period again. Profit can be an accounting opinion; this statement tracks actual dollars in and out — the reality check.

"This is one of the three major financial statements in accounting, along with a balance sheet and the cash flow statement."

5 · What the numbers actually tell you

Gross margin = 195.2 ÷ 416.2 = 46.9% → nearly half of every sale survives building the product. That's pricing power.

Net margin = 112.0 ÷ 416.2 = 26.9% → over a quarter is profit after everything. A typical grocer runs ~2%. Same word "profit," wildly different business.

Earnings-quality check: Apple's operating cash flow was $111.5B — almost identical to its $112.0B net income. Green flag: the profit is backed by real cash, not estimates.

⚠️ The #1 beginner trap: profit ≠ cash

A company can be profitable on paper yet go bankrupt — you book a $1M sale, but the customer pays in 90 days while payroll is due Friday. That's why the cash flow statement exists. "Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact."

6 · Check yourself

A company has Revenue $500, COGS $300, all other costs $150. What's its net income?

Gross profit = 500 − 300 = $200, then − $150 of other costs = $50 net income (a 10% net margin).

If Assets are $80 and Liabilities are $50, what is Equity?

Equity = Assets − Liabilities = 80 − 50 = $30 — the owners' true stake.

A startup reports $2M profit but can't make payroll. Which statement reveals the problem?

The profit is tied up in unpaid invoices — the cash flow statement exposes the cash gap the income statement hides.

🔎 One subtlety → your bridge to Course 02

Apple's equity ($73.7B) looks tiny next to its $112B profit — implying a >150% return on equity. The reason: years of share buybacks shrink equity, so a ratio can "lie" without context. How a company uses its profit — buybacks vs. dividends vs. reinvestment — is Capital Allocation (Course 02 & Exec E2).
