Accounting told you what a business did. Finance asks the harder question: what is it worth right now? The whole machine reduces to one idea — a dollar in the future is worth less than a dollar today, so to value anything you forecast its future cash and discount it back. This lesson builds that machine piece by piece, then turns it on two real companies: NVIDIA (high-growth) and Coca-Cola (a stable cash cow). Tap the cards, the diagrams, and the quizzes as you go.

1 · The one idea everything rests on: time value of money

Would you rather have $100 today or $100 in five years? Today — obviously. You could invest it, and there's risk you never see the future money at all. So future cash must be shrunk to compare it with cash in your hand. That shrinking is called discounting, and the rate you shrink it by is the discount rate.

Present Value  =  Future Cash Flow  ÷  (1 + r)n

Here r is the discount rate and n is how many years away the cash is. The further out the cash, the harder it shrinks. Tap each bar to watch $100 melt as it moves into the future at a 10% discount rate:

It's the return you demand to part with your money, given the risk. Safe cash (a government bond) gets a low rate; risky cash (a startup's future profits) gets a high one. A higher discount rate shrinks future cash harder — which is exactly why risky companies are worth less per future dollar. We'll name this rate properly in Section 4.

2 · Value vs. price — they are not the same thing

The market price is what people will pay today. Intrinsic value is what the cash flows are actually worth. They drift apart constantly — and the gap is where investors make (or lose) money. Damodaran hammers this point: pricing and valuing are two different jobs.

"The only way to address the question of whether the value dropped is to actually do an intrinsic valuation... pricing and value are different processes and we need to treat them differently."

— Aswath Damodaran, "Session 1: Valuation - Themes and Overview" · in your corpus

The technique for computing intrinsic value is Discounted Cash Flow (DCF): forecast a company's future cash, discount each year back to today, add it all up. That sum is the value. Everything else in this lesson is plumbing for that one sentence.

4 · The discount rate, named: cost of capital (WACC)

A business raises money two ways — from equity investors (owners) and from lenders (banks, bondholders). Each wants a return. Damodaran frames it cleanly: you must decide whose cash flows you're valuing.

"If I take a weighted average of your cost of equity and your cost of debt... You're taking a corporate finance class, right? What's that called? The cost of capital."

— Aswath Damodaran, "Session 3: Intrinsic and DCF Valuation - Laying the Groundwork" · in your corpus

To value the whole business, you discount the firm's cash by the blended cost of both money sources — the WACC:

WACC  =  (E/V × Cost of Equity)  +  (D/V × Cost of Debt × [1 − tax])

Where E is equity, D is debt, V = E + D. The cost of equity itself is built from a risk-free rate plus a risk premium scaled by beta (how volatile the stock is vs. the market):

Cost of Equity  =  Risk-Free Rate  +  Beta × Equity Risk Premium

Corporate Finance Institute's example "Brick-and-Mortar Co": cost of equity 15%, cost of debt 10%, 30% tax, $10M debt + $40M equity (so 80% equity / 20% debt). After-tax cost of debt = 10% × (1 − 0.30) = 7%. WACC = (0.80 × 15%) + (0.20 × 7%) = 12% + 1.4% = 13.4%. That 13.4% becomes the discount rate r from Section 1.

"The combined cost is the weighted average cost of capital, which is the cost of funding the assets of the company."

— Corporate Finance Institute, "Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)" · in your corpus

5 · Putting it together: the DCF formula

Now the machine assembles. Forecast free cash flow each year, discount each by the WACC, and add the terminal value for everything beyond your forecast:

Value  =  Σ  FCFt ÷ (1 + WACC)t  +  Terminal Value ÷ (1 + WACC)n

The Finance Storyteller describes exactly this assembly line — and warns it only works if your forecasts do:

"The value of a company today... is seen as the cash that the company currently has plus the estimates of future Free Cash Flows discounted back (using the Weighted Average Cost of Capital) to their present value."

— The Finance Storyteller, "WACC explained" · in your corpus

Once you have a business value, subtract debt to get equity value, divide by shares, and you have a per-share intrinsic value to compare against the market price.

6 · Two real companies: NVIDIA vs. Coca-Cola

A DCF is only as good as the story behind the numbers — and these two companies tell opposite stories. NVIDIA is a high-growth engine; Coca-Cola is a slow, dependable cash cow. Their real, latest figures show why. Tap each bar.

NVIDIA — Gross margin = 153.46 ÷ 215.94 = 71.1%. Net margin = 120.07 ÷ 215.94 = 55.6%. Operating cash flow: $102.72B. It keeps more than half of every sales dollar as profit — a sign of a near-monopoly on AI chips.

Coca-Cola — Gross margin = 29.54 ÷ 47.94 = 61.6%. Net margin = 13.11 ÷ 47.94 = 27.3%. Operating cash flow: $7.41B. Lower margins, but a century of predictability.

Why this matters for a DCF: the discount rate and the growth story differ. Coca-Cola's cash is so predictable you can forecast it confidently and lean on a steady terminal value. NVIDIA's cash could compound spectacularly — or get competed away — so its forecast carries far more risk, which a careful analyst reflects in a higher discount rate and a much wider range of outcomes.

7 · Check yourself

At a 10% discount rate, is $100 received in 5 years worth more, the same, or less than $100 today?

$100 ÷ (1.10)⁵ = $62.10. Future cash is always worth less today; that shrinkage is discounting.

A company is 80% equity (cost 15%) and 20% debt (after-tax cost 7%). What is its WACC?

(0.80 × 15%) + (0.20 × 7%) = 12% + 1.4% = 13.4% — the weighted blend of both money sources.

A stock's market price is $42 but your DCF says its intrinsic value is $36. What does that gap suggest?

Price ($42) above intrinsic value ($36) means the market is paying more than the cash justifies — a potential sell. Price and value are different processes.

⚠️ Garbage in, garbage out: the discount-rate trap

Tiny changes in the discount rate swing the answer wildly. In Damodaran's Kennecott case, bankers justified an overpriced acquisition simply by using too low a discount rate: "You take those same cash flows, you discount them at 10½%, you're going to come up with a much higher value... they used the wrong" rate. A DCF can be made to "prove" almost any number. Always sanity-check the inputs, not just the output.

🔎 One subtlety → your bridge to Course 03

Notice WACC contains an after-tax cost of debt, while equity gets no tax break — so how much debt a company carries changes its cost of capital and therefore its value. That trade-off — how much to borrow vs. fund with equity — is capital structure, the heart of the next course. Valuation tells you what a business is worth; capital structure asks how to finance it to be worth more.

📚 From the Canon — Klarman & Mauboussin on valuing under uncertainty

The DCF above produces a point estimate — one crisp number for what NVIDIA or Coca-Cola is worth. The canon insists you treat that number as a guess wrapped in a fog. Klarman bolts a margin of safety around it so you survive being wrong; Mauboussin reminds you that the skill–luck mix in any single outcome means a precise DCF can still be a coin flip. Together they are the operator's humility band on everything in Sections 1–6.

Klarman's key move isn't precision — it's bolting the cushion on at the end, after a conservative forecast, never as windage smuggled into every input. Buffett, asked exactly how he does it:

"Calculate them reasonably conservatively, but you don't try and put too much windage in at every level, and then when you get all through you apply the margin of safety… I want to be conservative at all the levels and then I want to have that significant margin of safety at the end."

— The Financial Review, "Warren Buffett Explains How He Calculates a Margin of Safety" · in your corpus

Mauboussin supplies the reason the cushion is non-negotiable: the more luck in a domain, the harder a single result reverts to the mean — so don't read one good (or bad) NVIDIA quarter as proof your model is right.

"In activities where both skill and luck contribute to outcomes, it is often the case that as skill increases — skill improves — luck becomes more important."

— Michael Mauboussin, "The Success Equation: Untangling Skill and Luck" (Talks at Google) · in your corpus

The pairing is sharp: your DCF point estimate is the skill; the margin of safety is the room you leave for luck to go against you. Coca-Cola's predictable cash needs a thin band; NVIDIA's could-compound-or-get-competed-away cash needs a wide one — same lesson as Section 6, now with a name.
