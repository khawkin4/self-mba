A public company is owned by thousands of shareholders who can't all run it — so they elect a board of directors to hire the CEO, watch the money, and represent them. This lesson teaches who actually sits in the boardroom, what they do, and what happens when an outside investor storms the gates. Hover the cards, scan the diagrams, and take the quiz — we'll use Disney's and JPMorgan's real numbers throughout.

3 · The split that makes governance necessary

The whole system exists because the people who own a company are not the people who run it. The board sits in the gap to keep those interests aligned:

Shareholders  → elect →  Board  → hires / fires & oversees →  CEO

"corporate governance is the framework used to run a company this is the responsibility of the board of directors a group of elected individuals who represent the interests of shareholders"

— jpmorgan, "Corporate Governance Explained | Unpacked" · in your corpus

4 · Why pay needs a watchdog — Disney's real scale

A core board job is setting executive pay. To see why that matters, look at the size of the business those executives run. Here is Disney's real FY2025 income, top to bottom — tap each bar:

Net margin = 12.40 ÷ 94.42 = 13.1% → Disney keeps about 13¢ of profit on every revenue dollar.

Return on equity (ROE) = NetIncome ÷ Equity = 12.40 ÷ 109.87 = 11.3% → for every $1 of owner capital, Disney earned ~11¢ of profit. The board's job is to judge whether management is putting that owner capital to good use.

The governance question: Disney famously combined and then split the Chairman and CEO roles. A lead independent director exists precisely so that, even when power concentrates, someone independent still speaks for shareholders.

5 · One person, two hats — the JPMorgan case

At JPMorgan Chase, Jamie Dimon has held both the Chairman and CEO roles. That concentrates power — so the board appoints a lead independent director to preserve independent oversight. Click to see why the stakes are enormous here:

JPMorgan's real FY2025 figures: $182.45B revenue, $57.05B net income (a 31.3% net margin), and $4,424.90B in assets against $362.44B equity. That means assets are roughly 12× equity — a highly leveraged balance sheet where strong oversight isn't optional.

"at JP Morgan Chase Lee Raymond as former lead independent director provided essential independent oversight when Jamie Diamond held both the chairperson and CEO roles ensuring balanced board decisions"

— Business School 101, "What is Board of Directors?" · in your corpus

JPM leverage check: Assets ÷ Equity = 4,424.90 ÷ 362.44 = 12.2×. Banks run on borrowed money, so the board's risk and audit oversight is the safety system standing between depositors and disaster.

6 · When outsiders force change — activist investors

Normally the board changes itself slowly. An activist investor short-circuits that: buy a big stake, then use it as leverage to demand board seats, spin-offs, buybacks, or a sale. They rarely own a majority — so they must win over the other shareholders' proxy votes.

"activists generally never own more than 5 or 10% of a business so they don't have control so the way they get influence is they have to convince the other... shareholders to support them"

— Lex Clips, "Activist investing explained | Bill Ackman and Lex Fridman" · in your corpus

The board isn't always the neutral referee it claims to be

In theory directors are watchdogs. In practice, board seats often go to well-connected insiders, pay can be very generous, and the biggest shareholders deciding elections are usually a few giant index funds. A "rubber-stamp" board can wave through record CEO pay that isn't really in shareholders' interest. Independence and engagement are what separate a real board from a club.

7 · Check yourself

A company's CEO is performing poorly and destroying value. Who has the formal power to remove them?

The board hires and fires the CEO on shareholders' behalf — that's its sharpest oversight tool.

Why does a company appoint a lead independent director (as JPMorgan did under Jamie Dimon)?

When the Chairman and CEO are the same person, a lead independent director keeps an unbiased voice for shareholders.

An activist typically owns only 5–10% of a company. How do they actually force change?

Without control, the activist must win over the other shareholders — "it's a bit like running for office," as Ackman puts it.

One subtlety → your bridge to the next course

Notice what the board actually decides: whether to pay a dividend, buy back stock, fund a $46B project, or approve a merger. That single question — what to do with the cash the business throws off — is capital allocation, the heart of executive strategy. The board governs who decides; capital allocation is how they decide.
