The great-books shelf will teach you strategy, persuasion, and human nature — but it goes quiet on the one question a marketing team actually argues about on a Monday: who exactly are we for, what do we say to them, and does the math work? This lesson fills that gap with four practitioner tools that fit together into one pipeline. Segment → target → position decides who you're for and what you stand for; Dunford's method makes the positioning concrete; Byron Sharp's laws tell you how brands actually grow (and demolish a few myths); and CAC/LTV unit economics tells you whether any of it pays. The discipline of the whole field is one habit: stop marketing to "everyone," pick a buyer, and prove the numbers close. Hover the cards, scan the diagrams, and take the quizzes as you go.

1 · STP: you cannot be for everyone

The foundational error in marketing is believing anyone can be your customer. The STP model — Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning — is the antidote, and it builds in order: carve the market into groups, pick the attractive ones, then stake a position in their minds.

"A mistake many companies make is to think that anyone can be their customer… there isn't really a product in existence that should target everybody as its customer. Even a company like Coca-Cola… offers different products to suit different types of people."

— Two Teachers, "STP Marketing (Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning)" · in your corpus

You can segment four classic ways: demographic (age, income, gender — who they are), geographic (country, region, urban/rural), psychographic (lifestyle, values, attitudes — why they buy), and behavioral (usage level, benefits sought, loyalty — how they use it). Adam Erhart names the pet peeve every marketer shares:

"One of my biggest pet peeves… is when I ask a potential client about who their target market is and they respond with something like 'everyone' or 'people with money.' I'm sorry, that's just too broad."

— Adam Erhart, "MARKETING 101: Segmentation, Targeting, and Positioning" · in your corpus

Targeting is then choosing which segments to chase — and the trap is judging on size alone. The corpus is explicit: weigh size & growth, profitability (what will the segment pay?), and ease of reach (can your channels even get there affordably?). A small, rich, reachable segment beats a huge, broke, scattered one. Click each stage of the funnel.

Because size is only one of three levers. A giant segment that won't pay much (low profitability) or that costs a fortune to reach (high CAC, poor ease of reach) can lose money on every customer, while a smaller segment that pays premium prices and clusters in one cheap-to-reach channel prints cash. This is exactly why STP and the unit-economics section of this lesson are the same conversation: targeting is a financial decision dressed up as a marketing one.

2 · Positioning is context — Dunford's 5-component method

Most teams "fall into" their positioning by accident. April Dunford — author of Obviously Awesome and, per the corpus, "almost certainly" the most-practiced positioning consultant alive — defines it precisely:

"Positioning defines how your product is the best in the world at delivering some value that a well-defined set of companies care a lot about."

— April Dunford, Lenny's Podcast · in your corpus

Positioning is context-setting. The cautionary tale in the corpus: put a stick in a cake and you've made a "Franken-cake" nobody wants; roll it into a ball and call it a cake pop — a snack-on-the-go for coffee-drinking adults — and the same object is suddenly obvious and desirable. Same product, different context, completely different value.

"The right kind of context can be the difference between a customer paying you one dollar for your service or a thousand dollars per month for that same service."

— "How to Nail Your Product Positioning (10 Steps) — Obviously Awesome" · in your corpus

Dunford's genius is the order of operations. Don't start with "what category are we in?" — start with what you have to beat, and let the rest fall out. Click each component.

The killer insight on step 1: don't discount the status quo. Dunford notes B2B loses ~40% of deals to "no decision" — meaning you lost to a spreadsheet, to pen and paper, to interns. If your positioning doesn't beat doing nothing, you never get the deal.

3 · How brands actually grow — Byron Sharp's laws

Byron Sharp's How Brands Grow (Ehrenberg-Bass Institute) is, per the corpus, "the most influential modern book on marketing" — and it overturns the comfortable myths. There are two levers, and they're less glamorous than most campaigns:

"There really are two levers marketers can pull to grow… The first is increasing physical availability — making your brand easy to find and easy to buy… The second is increasing mental availability — having your brand come to mind more often when consumers are looking to buy."

— "How Brands Grow by Byron Sharp (5 Minute Summary)" · in your corpus

Then the bombshell, backed by replicated data across Coke, Pepsi, toothpaste, cars, and handbags: growth comes from penetration (more buyers), not loyalty. Most of your sales come from light buyers who buy once or twice a year — there are simply far more of them than heavy buyers.

"When you increase your penetration — which means acquiring new customers — you also increase your loyalty. But the opposite is not true… Loyalty is a side effect of penetration. This is the double jeopardy law."

— "How Brands Grow by Byron Sharp (5 Minute Summary)" · in your corpus

Brands are "leaky buckets" — buyers constantly come and go, so you must keep refilling with new and lapsed buyers. The double jeopardy law explains why small brands suffer twice: fewer buyers, who also buy slightly less often. The strategic implication flips most loyalty-program thinking on its head:

"Even market leaders find two-thirds of buyers are super light, yet they contribute a third of sales… To grow you must constantly attract new buyers and reattract past ones. The key: reach more people more often."

— "How Brands Grow by Byron Sharp | Book Summary" · in your corpus

So: broad reach beats narrow targeting for established mass brands; consistent distinctive assets (logo, colors, slogans) build the memory structures that make you the effortless mental choice. Note the tension with STP — Sharp says reach everyone in the category; STP says narrow down. The reconciliation is in the pitfall below.

5 · Unit economics: does the math actually close?

Positioning and reach mean nothing if you lose money on every customer. The two numbers that decide it are CAC (what one new customer costs to acquire) and LTV (what one customer is worth in profit). CAC is the simpler one:

CAC = total marketing spend ÷ new customers acquired

"Let's say in January you spent $1,000 in marketing and you got 120 new customers — so that means you spent on average $8.33 to get one new customer. That is your CAC."

— Eric Andrews, "How to Calculate Customer Lifetime Value" · in your corpus

LTV is the total gross profit one customer throws off before they churn. The corpus is emphatic about the most common mistake — LTV is margin, not revenue:

"LTV is not long-term revenue. LTV is the operating margin that you make from a customer after allocating all the direct spends you'd have to in serving that customer."

— "LTV vs. CAC: The Simple Math of Unit Economics Explained" · in your corpus

For a subscription business, one clean formula chains it together. Average customer lifetime (in months) = 1 ÷ monthly churn rate. Multiply monthly gross profit by that lifetime:

LTV = (Avg Order Value × Gross Margin %) × (1 ÷ churn)  |  you can profitably spend up to LTV on CAC

Worked example — the subscription coffee company (Eric Andrews' real numbers). Average order value $25; gross margin 60%, so each order yields $25 × 0.60 = $15 in gross profit. Monthly churn is 22%, so the average customer stays 1 ÷ 0.22 ≈ 4.5 months. With ~1.35 orders per customer per month baked into his model, the lifetime gross profit lands at roughly LTV ≈ $92. The verdict: "we can spend up to $92 to acquire a customer and still be profitable. If we spend more than that, ultimately we're going to lose money." Notice the leverage points the formula exposes: cut churn from 22% to 11% and lifetime doubles to ~9 months — LTV roughly doubles to ~$184 with zero new marketing spend. Retention is a growth lever hiding inside the cost line. A healthy SaaS rule of thumb most practitioners cite is LTV:CAC ≥ 3:1 — the second corpus video's chocolate example works the same ratio (₹15 LTV ÷ ₹10 CAC = a 1.5 ratio, a warning sign).

6 · Check yourself

A founder says their target market is "everyone with money." What's the STP fix?

"Everyone" is the beginner's mistake. STP says split the market into segments, then pick the commercially attractive one(s) — not on size alone, but on what they'll pay and how cheaply you can reach them.

According to Byron Sharp, the bigger lever for brand growth is:

The double jeopardy law: grow penetration and loyalty rises with it, but not vice versa. Most sales come from many light buyers, so "reach more people more often" beats milking a small loyal core.

A subscription has $25 AOV, 60% gross margin, and 22% monthly churn. Roughly how long does the average customer stay, and what does that imply for CAC?

Average lifetime = 1 ÷ churn = 1 ÷ 0.22 ≈ 4.5 months. Chaining margin × lifetime gives LTV ≈ $92 — the ceiling on what you can spend to acquire a customer and stay profitable.

STP vs. Sharp: don't pick the wrong one for your stage

The sharpest trap here is treating "narrow your targeting" (STP) and "reach the whole category" (Sharp) as a contradiction and choosing dogmatically. They're stage-dependent. A new or niche product with a tiny budget must segment hard — it cannot afford broad reach and needs Dunford-style positioning to beat the status quo for a specific buyer. An established mass brand (Sharp's evidence base is FMCG giants) grows by going broad, because its ceiling is penetration and most of its untapped sales sit with light buyers it isn't reaching. Misapply Sharp to a seed-stage startup and you'll burn cash spraying ads at people who'll never buy; misapply rigid niche targeting to a category leader and you'll cap your own growth. And watch the loyalty myth either way: loyalty programs feel productive but, per the data, mostly reward buyers you already had — penetration is where growth actually lives.

One subtlety → your bridge to the rest of the MBA

Notice the throughline: every tool here collapsed into a number. Targeting was secretly a profitability decision; positioning changed willingness-to-pay ($1 vs. $1,000/month for the same thing); Sharp's "growth" was a penetration metric; and the whole pipeline only "works" if LTV > CAC by a healthy multiple. That's the bridge: marketing is the front end of unit economics and finance — a CAC is just a capital allocation, an LTV is a discounted cash flow, and churn is the discount rate in disguise. A practitioner in the corpus learned the human side the hard way: a marketer blasted a "thank you for ordering" email to everyone instead of the segment who'd actually purchased — the failure to segment created the mess (which they cleverly turned into a $20k "Oops!" recovery email). Segmentation, positioning, growth, and the math are one conversation, not four.

Your move

Pick one real product (yours, your employer's, or one you'd start). On one page: (1) write its STP — name one specific segment (with the demographic + psychographic + behavioral traits) and reject "everyone"; (2) run Dunford's sequence — list what it must beat including the status quo, the 2–3 differentiated capabilities, the value those create, and the market category that makes that value obvious; (3) ask Sharp's question — are you growing by penetration (more buyers) or fooling yourself with loyalty? where's your physical and mental availability weak?; (4) do the unit math — estimate CAC and LTV (= AOV × gross margin × [1 ÷ churn]) and check the LTV:CAC ratio against 3:1. If any one of the four is broken, you've found the real problem — and it's almost never the product.
