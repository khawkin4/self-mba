A startup is a machine for turning an idea into a business people actually pay for. This lesson hands you the two tools every founder uses: a one-page map of how a business works (the Business Model Canvas), and the real playbook for building and funding it (straight from Y Combinator). This is hands-on — tap the cards, the diagrams, and the quiz.

1 · Business model vs. strategy — don't blur them

These two words get used interchangeably, but they answer different questions. Tap each 👇

"How does this business run?" A description of how a company creates value for itself while delivering products or services to customers — how all the pieces (customers, revenue, costs) fit together. Walmart's model: slash costs by cutting chandeliers, carpets, and service in exchange for lower prices.

"How will we beat our rivals?" Walmart's strategy was to target rural "one-horse towns" everyone else ignored — cheap land, customers no one chased, and rivals locked out. Kmart had a fine model but tried to appeal to everyone — not a distinctive strategy — and struggled.

"While business model is a description of how your business runs, a competitive strategy explains how you will do better than your rivals."

— Harvard Business Review, "The Explainer: What is a Business Model?" · in your corpus

3 · The Business Model Canvas — nine boxes, one page

Alexander Osterwalder's canvas captures an entire business on a single sheet. The trick: the right side is about customers (and generates revenue), the left side is your infrastructure (and creates cost), with the value proposition in the middle bridging them. Tap each block.

4 · Read three real models

Two linked segments: people who search (free) and people who advertise (paying). Advertiser money subsidizes the free product. The free side attracts users; the users attract advertisers. Each side makes the other more valuable.

Most users make internet calls for free; about 10% pay for premium calls to phones. Skype can offer this because it owns almost no infrastructure — just back-end software and servers, not a telecom network.

A cheap razor handle is the bait (often sold at a loss); the ongoing blade purchases are the hook. The same pattern powers SaaS: a free first month leads to a recurring subscription.

6 · Idea → users → money: the order that actually works

Beginners think the sequence is raise money → build → find users. The best founders flip it: build a tiny version → get real users → then (maybe) raise. Investors want to jump on trains already in motion.

Validate the problem  →  Ship a small MVP  →  Delight a few real users  →  Raise

Zepto (a billion-dollar grocery-delivery company) began as a WhatsApp group solving grocery runs for 30 aunties. The lesson its founder taught at YC Startup School India: customer delight is the only metric that matters at the start — make 30 people obsessed and you have the foundation for a revolution.

Solugen, a chemical-manufacturing startup, didn't pitch for $20M to build a plant. They built a reactor that fit on a desk, scaled it just enough to sell hydrogen peroxide to hot-tub supply stores for ~$10k/month — then raised easily, because they were already in motion.

The validation trick (from a 17-year-old founder at YC India): before building, post a one-page waitlist describing the problem and solution, push it on social media, and see if strangers leave their email. Only then ship the MVP. "Firstly you validate the idea, then you ship it."

"The best Founders they build the first version of the product first even if it's a simple almost toy-like version of it and then they go get some users for it... and only then... do they start thinking about raising money."

— Y Combinator, "How Startup Fundraising Works | Startup School" · in your corpus

7 · How fundraising really works

Forget Shark Tank. Real seed fundraising is "a bunch of one-on-one meetings on Zoom over and over again while you try to collect checks." It's a grind, not a glamorous pitch — and rejection is normal. One YC founder was rejected 50+ times before her first check; her company later sold for $275M.

⚠️ You don't need magic words — you need traction

Founders beg, "what do I say to make investors invest?" There are no magic words. The best startups sound terrible at first (an air mattress on a stranger's floor; food delivery to the suburbs). If investors pass, it's usually not your pitch — it's that the startup isn't good enough yet. The fix is a better product with happier users, then explaining it like a normal human.

"You don't need to impress investors you need to convince them and that's a slightly different thing."

— Y Combinator, "How Startup Fundraising Works | Startup School" · in your corpus

8 · Check yourself

In the Business Model Canvas, which block sits in the middle and bridges the customer side to the infrastructure side?

The Value Proposition is the heart of the canvas — the value you deliver. Right-side blocks describe customers; left-side blocks describe what it takes to deliver that value.

A founder has a big idea and thinks "first I'll raise $2M, then build." What does the YC playbook advise instead?

It's cheaper than ever to ship a first version and find users. Doing that gives you leverage — "investors want to jump on trains that are in motion."

Gillette sells a cheap handle and profits from repeat blade sales. What is this pattern called?

Bait & hook: an attractive, cheap (or loss-making) initial offer locks in profitable ongoing purchases. The same shape powers many SaaS free-trial-to-subscription models.

🔎 One subtlety → your bridge to the next course

The SAFE lets founders raise without giving up board seats or control — but every dollar still carries a valuation cap that quietly sets how much of the company you're selling. How you price a round, dilute equity, and structure ownership over multiple rounds is the domain of venture financing & cap tables — where entrepreneurship hands off to corporate finance.

🎯 Your move

Pick a company you use every day (or your own idea) and fill in all nine canvas blocks on a single sheet — start with the right side (segments, value proposition, channels, relationships, revenue), then build the left (resources, activities, partners, costs). Then ask the canvas's most powerful question for each block: "What if we removed or changed this?" That's where new business models are born.
